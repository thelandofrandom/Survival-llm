# ATP 3-37.10 / MCRP 3-40D.13 Base Camps

## Purpose

ATP 3-37.10 / MCRP 3-40D.13, *Base Camps* (U.S. Army and U.S. Marine Corps, 27 January 2017, 190 pages) — the joint Army/Marine Corps doctrine for planning, designing, constructing, operating, maintaining, securing, and closing base camps in support of contingency operations. It supersedes ATP 3-37.10 / MCRP 3-17.7N (26 April 2013). A base camp is a facility that provides the full range of support services — billeting, dining, medical, power, water, waste, security — for a deployed force. This skill is for anyone who must site, build, run, defend, or close a base camp: the planner who sizes it, the engineer who builds it, the base operations center that runs it, and the commander who defends it and hands it over or closes it.

**What a base camp is, in one sentence.** A base camp is an evolving, scalable facility — not a fixed installation — that is planned from the start to grow, change standards, and eventually transfer or close.

## 1. Overview — What a Base Camp Is and What It Must Survive

**Operational challenges.** Base camps are built in uncertain conditions: the deployed force's size and composition change between planning and construction and will almost certainly change again over the camp's life. The two hardest estimates are the intended population (personnel, vehicles, and equipment on the camp at any one time) and the expected life span, which follows from mission duration. Planners must plan and design from valid assumptions — false assumptions produce inadequate facilities or wasted construction — and commanders must tolerate the ambiguity and mitigate risk through extensive planning and continuous coordination.

**Entry conditions.** A base camp begins with entry — the initial arrival of forces into an area of operations. The entry force establishes basic services with organic capabilities and pre-positioned stock, then expands as the force grows.

**Threat levels (Table 1-1).** Threat levels are based on the activity, capability, and intent of enemy agents or forces, and they drive what security forces the camp needs:

| Level | Threat | Capability required |
|---|---|---|
| I | Agents, saboteurs, sympathizers, terrorists, insider threats, civil disturbances | Organic base security forces |
| II | Small tactical units; irregular forces, which may include significant stand-off weapons threats | Base security forces plus a mobile security force (MSF) |
| III | Large tactical force operations, including airborne, heliborne, amphibious, infiltration, and major air operations | Base security forces, a mobile security force, and a tactical combat force (TCF) |

**Classification by duration (Table 1-2).** A contingency base camp is expected to operate 2–10 years or less; an enduring base camp is expected to operate more than 5 years. Facilities transition from contingency to enduring standards when appropriate, typically any time within a 6-month to 5-year period; the actual trigger is conditions, not the calendar. Construction standards by phase:

| Phase | Construction standard | Expected duration |
|---|---|---|
| Contingency | Organic | Up to 90 days |
| Contingency | Initial | Up to 6 months |
| Contingency | Temporary | Up to 5 years |
| Contingency | Semipermanent | 2–10 years |
| Enduring | Permanent | 5 years or longer |

The combatant commander (CCDR), in coordination with Service components and the Services, specifies the construction standards for facilities in the theater, balancing engineer effort against health, safety, and mission adequacy. Enduring standards are not typically used during the contingency phase, though semipermanent standards are sometimes used in place of initial or temporary standards when site considerations or mission parameters require it. Semipermanent facilities have a life expectancy of less than 10 years, extendable to 25 years with maintenance.

**Classification by size.** Typical base camp populations: platoon ~50, company ~300, battalion/battalion landing team ~1,000, brigade combat team/regimental combat team ~3,000, support area 6,000 or greater. Size drives land area, perimeter length, and every planning factor in Appendix E.

**Classification by purpose.** Base camps support the mission — they are not an end in themselves. Purpose shapes the facilities mix: a camp supporting combat operations needs different facilities than one supporting humanitarian assistance or a training mission.

**Service levels.** Services rise in three levels as the camp matures:

- **Basic services** — established as part of initial entry, primarily with organic capabilities and pre-positioned stock. Essential for sustaining operations for a minimum of 60 days. Characterized by rapid deployment and emplacement; facilities and infrastructure are highly flexible and movable and are built with minimal engineering effort.
- **Expanded services** — improved to increase efficiencies and expanded to sustain operations for a minimum of 180 days. Built from additional engineer effort above basic facility standards.
- **Enhanced services** — surpass expanded services: improved to operate at optimal efficiency and sustain operations for an unspecified duration. Flexible, durable, nearly self-sustaining, and primarily implemented through contracted support.

**Principles.** Four principles govern base camp planning and design:

- **Scalability** — the camp must grow and shrink with the force; plan for the population you expect, not the population you have.
- **Sustainability** — the camp must be supportable over its expected life: power, water, waste, and supply must keep pace with growth.
- **Standardization** — use standardized plans and designs (Army Facilities Components System, Joint Construction Management System, Unified Facilities Criteria) so camps are buildable, maintainable, and interchangeable.
- **Survivability** — the camp must protect its occupants: dispersion, hardening, overhead cover, and a defense that matches the threat level.

**Activities.** Base camp work falls into five activities: master planning, construction, operation and maintenance, protection, and sustainment/combat service support. → `references/overview-and-classification.md`

## 2. Planning and Design — Before Ground Is Broken

**Planning principles.** Plan early, plan with the supported commander, and plan for change. The base camp plan is part of the operation plan, not a separate engineering exercise. Planners work from the mission, the force to be supported, the threat, the terrain, and the expected duration — and they revisit the plan as any of those change.

**Basing strategy.** The basing strategy answers the big questions first: how many camps, where, how large, and for how long. It weighs mission requirements against threat, terrain, host-nation considerations, lines of communication, and the availability of existing facilities. Fewer, larger camps are more efficient to support but present a bigger target and longer lines to the force; more, smaller camps disperse risk but multiply the support and security bill.

**Planning approach.** Planning proceeds from the top down and the ground up at the same time: the higher headquarters sets the basing strategy and standards, while the engineer and the supported unit reconnoiter the site and feed ground truth back into the plan. The plan is a living document — it is updated as the site survey, the force list, and the threat picture mature.

**Master planning.** Master planning is the long view: it lays out the camp's ultimate footprint, zones, and infrastructure so that today's construction does not block tomorrow's expansion. Appendix B gives the master-planning process and its relationship to the military decisionmaking process (MDMP) and the Marine Corps Planning Process (MCPP). A master plan covers land use, circulation, utilities, force protection, and expansion areas, and it is approved by the commander who owns the camp.

**Development planning.** Development planning turns the master plan into buildable projects: it sequences construction by priority, assigns resources, and sets standards for each facility. Development planning is where the service level (basic, expanded, enhanced) and the construction standard (organic, initial, temporary, semipermanent, permanent) are matched to each facility and each phase.

**Funding sources and authority.** Components fund their participation in an operation with operations and maintenance (O&M) funds. Typical funding sources for contingency construction are O&M, military construction (MILCON), and separate acts of Congress. The judge advocate advises on current contingency construction limits and requirements. Funding authority determines what can be built, how fast, and by whom — and the funding source must be identified before construction starts, not after.

**Contingency construction (10 USC 2804).** The Secretary of Defense is authorized by 10 USC 2804 to execute contingency military construction projects. Joint Publication 3-34 provides joint contingency construction standards. The Army Facilities Components System (AFCS) designs help solve problems associated with contingency construction constrained by time, materials, and engineer capacity. The Joint Construction Management System (JCMS) is a database and reporting system used by military engineers for contingency construction activities, and a tool for base camp development, planning, and design. → `references/planning-and-design.md`

## 3. Construction — Building the Camp

**Facilities and infrastructure.** A base camp is a system of facilities and infrastructure: billeting, dining, medical, headquarters and administration, maintenance, storage, power generation and distribution, water production and distribution, sewage, waste management, roads, airfields and helipads, force protection works, and communications. Appendix F gives design detail for each.

**Contingency construction.** Contingency construction is construction under operational constraints — limited time, limited materials, limited engineers, and a changing mission. It is governed by 10 USC 2804 and JP 3-34, and it uses standardized designs to the maximum extent possible.

**Standards and standardized plans/designs.** Standardized designs come from the AFCS, the JCMS, and the Unified Facilities Criteria (UFCs). Using them is not bureaucracy — it is how a camp gets built on time with the materials and engineers actually available. Deviations are the exception and need justification.

**General requirements.** Every facility must meet the theater's construction standards, environmental, safety, and occupational health (ESOH) requirements, and the commander's force-protection standards. Health and safety are not traded away for speed.

**Project approval and acquisition review board.** Construction projects are approved through the chain of command and, for larger efforts, an acquisition review board that validates the requirement, the standard, the funding, and the construction agent before work starts.

**Construction management — six phases.** Construction or project management involves six phases, which may be used for a single project or for the camp as a whole:

1. **Initial project planning** — begins when a sponsoring entity originates, sponsors, and funds a construction project, or on receipt of a construction directive (operations order, fragmentary order, or warning order) for a design-build project.
2. **Preliminary construction planning** — begins on receipt of a construction directive for projects already designed; gives the engineering unit commander a quick overview, an assessment of unit capacity, and time to develop the commander's intent, assign primary personnel, and acquire plans and specifications.
3. **Detailed project planning** — provides a schedule for the entire construction project and an accurate estimate of tools, equipment, materials, and personnel for each construction activity, including a bill of materials, product and resource-leveling schedules, and quality control.
4. **Final planning and project turnover** — includes project confirmation briefings.
5. **Project execution and monitoring** — the direction, monitoring, and control of construction.
6. **Final project transfer and close-out** — turn-in of all tools and materials and formal transfer of the completed facility.

**Construction plan, priorities, and surveying.** Base camps are constructed in phases based on priorities and sound construction practices. Surveying proceeds in three stages: reconnaissance (a quick look to confirm the site will work), preliminary location (a closer survey to fix the layout), and final location and construction layout (staking the facilities to the ground).

**Materials.** Use what is available locally first — local materials reduce the logistics burden — but verify quality and suitability. Imported materials are reserved for what local sources cannot provide. Material selection is driven by the construction standard, the expected duration, and the threat.

**Site preparation.** Clear the site (remove vegetation and obstacles), grub (remove roots and organic material), strip (remove topsoil), then cut and fill to grade, and provide drainage. Drainage is not an afterthought: standing water breeds disease, undermines foundations, and turns a camp into a swamp. Grade the site so water runs away from facilities.

**Construction methods.** Methods, in order of increasing permanence and effort:

- **Existing structures** — use what is already there (buildings, warehouses, airfields) when it meets the requirement; fastest and cheapest.
- **Tents** — the default for initial and temporary standards; fast to erect, flexible, and movable, but they degrade with long exposure to the elements and are less likely to be repackable and reusable the longer they are used. Tents used outside the United States are subject to host-nation considerations.
- **Pre-engineered metal or fabric structures** — faster than traditional construction, more durable than tents.
- **Modular or trailer units** — factory-built, delivered and emplaced; good for billeting, offices, and medical facilities.
- **Prefabricated structures** — components built off-site and assembled on-site.
- **Traditional construction** — wood, steel, or concrete masonry unit (CMU); slowest, most durable, used for semipermanent and permanent standards.

**Construction means.** Who builds the camp:

- **Troop construction** — organic engineer units build with their own personnel and equipment.
- **Defense construction agents** — the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) and the Naval Facilities Engineering Command (NAVFAC) execute larger or more complex construction.
- **Contracted construction** — civilian contractors build under contract; the primary means for enhanced services and enduring camps.
- **Horizontal and vertical projects** — horizontal construction is earthwork, roads, airfields, and utilities; vertical construction is buildings and structures. The two are planned and resourced separately and sequenced so horizontal work leads vertical work. → `references/construction.md`

## 4. Operations and Maintenance — Running the Camp

**The base operations center (BOC).** Chapter 4 centers on the operation of base camp management centers and base operations centers (BOCs). The BOC is the nerve center of the camp, analogous to a G-3/S-3 operations section: it coordinates daily operations, tracks the camp's status, and directs the response to emergencies. BOC sections cover operations, logistics, administration, and facilities. Base cluster operations centers (BCOCs) coordinate multiple camps in a base cluster.

**Utility services.** Utilities are the camp's lifeblood, and they are planned as systems, not afterthoughts:

- **Electrical power** — generation and distribution sized to the camp's load, with redundancy for critical facilities (medical, communications, water production). Appendix F gives the power life cycle.
- **Energy conservation** — reducing demand is cheaper than generating more power; conservation measures are part of the plan from the start.
- **Water purification and supply** — production, storage, and distribution sized to the population and the mission; water quality is a health issue, not a comfort issue. Appendix F gives water production and distribution design.
- **Sewage** — collection and treatment, including lagoons and septic systems; sewage failures are a force-protection and health failure.

**Field services and quality of life.** Field services — laundry, showers, latrines, barber, postal, exchange, morale and recreation — sustain the force. Quality of life is not a luxury: a force that cannot rest, wash, and eat properly degrades.

**Supply and distribution.** The camp is a distribution node: supplies arrive, are stored, and are issued to units. Storage, accountability, and distribution routes are planned so that resupply does not choke the camp's circulation.

**Waste management.** Solid waste, hazardous waste, and wastewater are managed as a system: collection, segregation, disposal, and — for hazardous materials and hazardous waste (HAZMAT/HW) — transport under regulation. Landfills, soakage pits, and burn pits are sited and recorded because they must be closed properly later (see Chapter 6).

**Maintenance and repair.** Facilities maintenance and work orders keep the camp running: preventive maintenance on generators, water plants, and vehicles; corrective maintenance on what breaks; and a work-order system that prioritizes by mission impact. Deferred maintenance is a debt that comes due in the middle of the operation.

**Emergency management.** The camp must respond to emergencies 24 hours a day: fire protection, police operations, medical response, and mass-casualty handling. Emergency plans are written, rehearsed, and known to every unit on the camp.

**Training support and unit processing.** The camp supports training (ranges, training areas) and the processing of units arriving and departing — reception, staging, onward movement, and integration (RSOI) for incoming units, and the reverse for departing ones.

**Contracted functions.** Many camp functions — food service, laundry, power, waste, maintenance — are performed by contractors, especially at the enhanced service level. Contracted functions are managed, not abdicated: the government retains oversight, quality assurance, and the authority to direct the work. → `references/operations-security-and-closure.md`

## 5. Security and Defense — Protecting the Camp

**Protection framework.** The camp is defended in three zones: the **outer security area** (beyond the perimeter, where threats are detected and delayed), the **perimeter zone** (the fence, walls, towers, and fighting positions), and the **inner security area** (the camp itself, where critical facilities are protected). Defense in depth means a threat that penetrates one zone meets another.

**Threats.** Two kinds of attack: **penetrating attacks** (an attacker who tries to get inside the perimeter — by stealth, by force, or by insider action) and **standoff attacks** (mortars, rockets, snipers, and other weapons fired from outside the perimeter). Standoff attackers are typically elusive targets; Level I and Level II threats may rely on blending in with the local population.

**Protection forces.** The forces that defend the camp, in order of employment:

- **Organic forces** — the units on the camp defend it from a Level I threat with their own resources.
- **Security forces** — dedicated base security forces, which with organic forces defend against Level I and Level II threats.
- **Protective services** — specialized protection for designated personnel and facilities.
- **Reinforcement** — a mobile security force (MSF) and a tactical combat force (TCF), employed as required based on the threat level; the TCF is organized by the area of operations commander.
- **Allied and coalition forces** and **host-nation forces** — where available and authorized, they reinforce the camp's defense.

The base camp must be able to protect itself against a Level I threat; Level I and II threats are defeated with base camp security forces, and Level III threats are delayed within a joint security area until the TCF arrives.

**Defense tasks.** Two tasks: **area defense** (defending the area around the camp, including the outer security area) and **perimeter defense** (defending the perimeter itself). Both are planned, rehearsed, and resourced.

**Security operations.** The daily work of defense:

- **Area and local security** — patrolling and observing beyond the perimeter.
- **Rules of engagement (ROE)** — every defender knows when force may be used; ROE is briefed, not assumed.
- **Perimeter security** — the fence, barriers, and fighting positions, manned and maintained.
- **Preplanned fires** — indirect fire planned on likely avenues of approach and registered before they are needed.
- **Observation posts and fighting positions** — sited for observation and fields of fire, with overhead cover.
- **Early warning** — sensors, patrols, and local sources that give the camp time to react.
- **Entry control and entry control points (ECPs)** — every person and vehicle enters through a controlled point, is searched, and is accounted for; ECPs are hardened because they are the most likely point of attack.
- **Patrols** — internal patrols inside the camp and external patrols outside the perimeter.
- **Towers, bunkers, and overhead cover** — towers for observation, bunkers for protection, overhead cover for fighting positions and critical facilities.

**The base cluster.** Where several camps are grouped, a base cluster operations center (BCOC) coordinates their defense, and the cluster's security forces are employed as a whole. → `references/operations-security-and-closure.md`

## 6. Transfer and Closure — Leaving the Camp

**Transfer and closure plan.** Every base camp needs a transfer and closure plan from the start — not written when the departure order arrives. The plan covers five areas: environment, waste disposal, property, contracted support, and records/documentation.

**Environment.** Environmental site closure surveys document the condition of the site before departure. Latrines, soakage pits, landfills, and septic systems are closed, marked, and recorded. Hazardous materials and hazardous waste are transported under regulation — they are not abandoned, buried, or burned in place.

**Waste disposal.** All waste streams are closed out: solid waste, hazardous waste, wastewater, and medical waste. Disposal sites are recorded so the host nation and any follow-on force know what is in the ground.

**Property.** Property is disposed of by one of five means: **retain** (keep it for a follow-on force), **reutilize** (use it elsewhere in theater), **redistribute** (send it to another unit), **retrograde** (ship it home), or **dispose** (destroy or abandon it under proper authority). The decision is driven by the commander's guidance, the property's condition, and the cost of moving it versus its value.

**Contracted support.** Contracts are closed out: work is completed or terminated, contractors are paid, and government-furnished property is recovered. Contractor departure is sequenced with the force's departure so services do not collapse before the last unit leaves.

**Records and documentation.** The camp's records — master plan, as-built drawings, environmental surveys, property records, contracts, and the closure survey — are preserved and handed over. A camp with no records is a liability for whoever inherits the site.

**Emergency abandonment and destruction.** If the camp must be abandoned under emergency conditions, destruction procedures deny the enemy use of facilities, equipment, and supplies. Destruction is authorized by the commander, prioritized by military value, and executed only when the tactical situation requires it. → `references/operations-security-and-closure.md`

## Appendices A–H

- **Appendix A — Metric Conversion.** Conversion tables for the measurements used in planning (length, area, volume, weight, temperature).
- **Appendix B — Master Planning.** The master-planning process in detail and its relationship to the military decisionmaking process (MDMP) and the Marine Corps Planning Process (MCPP).
- **Appendix C — Sample Army Base Camp Appendix/Annex.** A worked example of a base camp appendix to an operation plan, showing the format and content a staff should produce.
- **Appendix D — Land Use Planning.** Camp layout designs, including the rectangular box design and the wheel design, with their advantages and disadvantages for circulation, security, and expansion.
- **Appendix E — Planning Factors.** The numbers planners use: population by unit type, land area, perimeter length, and facility requirements by camp size.
- **Appendix F — Facilities and Infrastructure Design.** Design detail for power (life cycle), water production and distribution, billeting, dining, medical, and waste facilities.
- **Appendix G — Reachback.** Reachback support: the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) — including the USACE Reachback Operations Center (UROC) and the Rapid Engineering Deployable (REDi) teams — the Naval Facilities Engineering Command (NAVFAC), and the Air Force Civil Engineer Center (AFCEC).
- **Appendix H — Communications Support.** Communications configurations for company, battalion, support-area, and Marine air-ground task force (MAGTF) base camps.

## Output Contract

When answering a base-camp question from ATP 3-37.10 / MCRP 3-40D.13, give the doctrine first, then the numbers that make it work (population, duration, service level, construction standard, threat level), then the sequence (plan, build, operate, defend, transfer/close). State the scale explicitly — a platoon camp of 50 and a support area of 6,000+ are different problems — and name the phase the advice belongs to, since a camp at initial entry is not run like a camp at enhanced service level. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Never invent a number.** Every population, duration, standard, and threat level in this skill comes from the manual's tables; if the manual does not give a number, say so.
2. **Scale before detail.** Establish camp size, duration, and threat level before giving facility or defense advice.
3. **Standards are a system.** Construction standard, service level, and duration move together — do not mix a permanent facility with a 90-day camp.
4. **The plan is living.** Master planning and development planning are continuous; a plan written at entry and never updated is worse than no plan.
5. **Defense matches threat.** A Level I defense against a Level III threat is a failure of planning, not of the defenders.
6. **Closure starts at entry.** Transfer and closure are planned from the beginning; a camp built with no closure plan is a camp that cannot be left cleanly.
7. **Use the appendices.** Appendix E (planning factors) and Appendix F (facilities design) carry the numbers; Appendix B (master planning) and Appendix D (land use) carry the method.
