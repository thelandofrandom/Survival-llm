# Operations, Security, and Closure — ATP 3-37.10 / MCRP 3-40D.13

## The Base Operations Center (BOC)

Chapter 4 centers on the operation of base camp management centers and base operations centers (BOCs). The BOC is the nerve center of the camp, analogous to a G-3/S-3 operations section: it coordinates daily operations, tracks the camp's status, and directs the response to emergencies. BOC sections cover operations, logistics, administration, and facilities. Base cluster operations centers (BCOCs) coordinate multiple camps in a base cluster.

## Utility Services

- **Electrical power** — generation and distribution sized to the camp's load, with redundancy for critical facilities (medical, communications, water production). Appendix F gives the power life cycle.
- **Energy conservation** — reducing demand is cheaper than generating more power; conservation measures are part of the plan from the start.
- **Water purification and supply** — production, storage, and distribution sized to the population and the mission; water quality is a health issue, not a comfort issue. Appendix F gives water production and distribution design.
- **Sewage** — collection and treatment, including lagoons and septic systems; sewage failures are a force-protection and health failure.

## Field Services and Quality of Life

Field services — laundry, showers, latrines, barber, postal, exchange, morale and recreation — sustain the force. Quality of life is not a luxury: a force that cannot rest, wash, and eat properly degrades.

## Supply and Distribution

The camp is a distribution node: supplies arrive, are stored, and are issued to units. Storage, accountability, and distribution routes are planned so that resupply does not choke the camp's circulation.

## Waste Management

Solid waste, hazardous waste, and wastewater are managed as a system: collection, segregation, disposal, and — for hazardous materials and hazardous waste (HAZMAT/HW) — transport under regulation. Landfills, soakage pits, and burn pits are sited and recorded because they must be closed properly later.

## Maintenance and Repair

Facilities maintenance and work orders keep the camp running: preventive maintenance on generators, water plants, and vehicles; corrective maintenance on what breaks; a work-order system that prioritizes by mission impact. Deferred maintenance is a debt that comes due in the middle of the operation.

## Emergency Management

The camp must respond to emergencies 24 hours a day: fire protection, police operations, medical response, and mass-casualty handling. Emergency plans are written, rehearsed, and known to every unit on the camp.

## Training Support and Unit Processing

The camp supports training (ranges, training areas) and the processing of units arriving and departing — reception, staging, onward movement, and integration (RSOI) for incoming units, and the reverse for departing ones.

## Contracted Functions

Many camp functions — food service, laundry, power, waste, maintenance — are performed by contractors, especially at the enhanced service level. Contracted functions are managed, not abdicated: the government retains oversight, quality assurance, and the authority to direct the work.

## Protection Framework

The camp is defended in three zones: the **outer security area** (beyond the perimeter, where threats are detected and delayed), the **perimeter zone** (the fence, walls, towers, and fighting positions), and the **inner security area** (the camp itself, where critical facilities are protected). Defense in depth means a threat that penetrates one zone meets another.

## Threats

- **Penetrating attacks** — an attacker who tries to get inside the perimeter, by stealth, by force, or by insider action.
- **Standoff attacks** — mortars, rockets, snipers, and other weapons fired from outside the perimeter. Standoff attackers are typically elusive targets; Level I and Level II threats may rely on blending in with the local population.

## Protection Forces

- **Organic forces** — the units on the camp defend it from a Level I threat with their own resources.
- **Security forces** — dedicated base security forces, which with organic forces defend against Level I and Level II threats.
- **Protective services** — specialized protection for designated personnel and facilities.
- **Reinforcement** — a mobile security force (MSF) and a tactical combat force (TCF), employed as required based on the threat level; the TCF is organized by the area of operations commander.
- **Allied and coalition forces** and **host-nation forces** — where available and authorized, they reinforce the camp's defense.

The base camp must be able to protect itself against a Level I threat; Level I and II threats are defeated with base camp security forces, and Level III threats are delayed within a joint security area until the TCF arrives.

## Defense Tasks

- **Area defense** — defending the area around the camp, including the outer security area.
- **Perimeter defense** — defending the perimeter itself.

## Security Operations

- **Area and local security** — patrolling and observing beyond the perimeter.
- **Rules of engagement (ROE)** — every defender knows when force may be used; ROE is briefed, not assumed.
- **Perimeter security** — the fence, barriers, and fighting positions, manned and maintained.
- **Preplanned fires** — indirect fire planned on likely avenues of approach and registered before they are needed.
- **Observation posts and fighting positions** — sited for observation and fields of fire, with overhead cover.
- **Early warning** — sensors, patrols, and local sources that give the camp time to react.
- **Entry control and entry control points (ECPs)** — every person and vehicle enters through a controlled point, is searched, and is accounted for; ECPs are hardened because they are the most likely point of attack.
- **Patrols** — internal patrols inside the camp and external patrols outside the perimeter.
- **Towers, bunkers, and overhead cover** — towers for observation, bunkers for protection, overhead cover for fighting positions and critical facilities.

## Transfer and Closure

Every base camp needs a transfer and closure plan from the start, covering five areas:

- **Environment** — environmental site closure surveys document the condition of the site before departure. Latrines, soakage pits, landfills, and septic systems are closed, marked, and recorded. HAZMAT/HW is transported under regulation — never abandoned, buried, or burned in place.
- **Waste disposal** — all waste streams are closed out (solid, hazardous, wastewater, medical). Disposal sites are recorded so the host nation and any follow-on force know what is in the ground.
- **Property** — disposed of by one of five means: **retain** (keep it for a follow-on force), **reutilize** (use it elsewhere in theater), **redistribute** (send it to another unit), **retrograde** (ship it home), or **dispose** (destroy or abandon it under proper authority).
- **Contracted support** — contracts are closed out: work completed or terminated, contractors paid, government-furnished property recovered. Contractor departure is sequenced with the force's departure so services do not collapse before the last unit leaves.
- **Records and documentation** — the master plan, as-built drawings, environmental surveys, property records, contracts, and the closure survey are preserved and handed over.

**Emergency abandonment and destruction.** If the camp must be abandoned under emergency conditions, destruction procedures deny the enemy use of facilities, equipment, and supplies. Destruction is authorized by the commander, prioritized by military value, and executed only when the tactical situation requires it.
