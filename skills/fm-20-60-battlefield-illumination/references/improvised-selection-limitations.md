# Improvised Means, Selection, and Limiting Factors — FM 20-60

## Improvised Means of Illumination

Chapter 8 and FM 20-33 describe field-expedient illumination devices successfully employed on the battlefield.

**Fires:** thatched roofs, grass, brush, or woods set alight by white phosphorus shells, illuminating shells, flares, or tracer ammunition. Besides harassing the enemy, these fires often provide sufficient illumination to silhouette enemy forces and allow effective fire to be placed on them.

**High-danger flare:** a No. 10 can partially filled with sand saturated with a mixture of lubricating oil and gasoline, placed on a likely route of approach to a defensive position and ignited. Burns for several hours and produces sufficient illumination to silhouette movement.

**Flame illuminator:** a 5- to 55-gallon container filled with thickened fuel. Three turns of detonating cord are secured along the inside of the top rim, leaving 3 to 5 feet of detonating cord as the main line; a nonelectric firing system is attached near the end of the main line by taping a nonelectric blasting cap to the detonating cord with the base of the cap pointing in the direction the detonation wave is to travel. A detonating-cord/white-phosphorus hand grenade assembly (five to seven turns of detonating cord wrapped around the grenade body, one end left loose as the branch line) is attached to the top of the container or in a small hole nearby so the explosive charge and grenade function simultaneously, igniting the fuel. The branch line is connected to the main line with a girth hitch forming an angle of not less than 90 degrees, or with standard M1 detonating cord clips. For electrical firing, replace the nonelectric firing system with an electric blasting cap and connect the lead wires to firing wires run to a power source. **Caution: before attaching the electric blasting cap lead wires to the firing wire, definitely ascertain that the firing wire is not connected to a power source (blasting machine or battery).** FM 5-25 covers electric and nonelectric detonating cord firing systems; TM 3-366 covers preparation, handling, and storage of flame fuels.

**Husch flare:** a special flare illuminator that permits thickened fuel to be vaporized and expelled as a burning jet through a small hole. It consists of one or more metal cylindrical tubes (such as propellant or shell containers) filled with thickened fuel and inserted into a larger metal container also containing thickened fuel. The manual's example uses a smoke pot shipping container, two 81-mm mortar shell metal containers, thickened fuel, and a WP illumination grenade: holes of ⅛ inch are punched in the bottom of the 81-mm containers, which are filled about three-fourths full, sealed with heavy grease on the caps, placed caps-down in the smoke pot container (filled one-third full), wedged upright with stones, and a WP or incendiary grenade tied between them just above the top level of the container, with a pull or trip wire extending to an activation point at least 25 meters from the device. The example burns for about 1 hour, illuminating an area about 90 meters in diameter. Bury one-half to three-fourths of the container in the ground or sandbag it for protection from shell fragments and small-arms fire and to prevent tipping.

**Headlight device:** a sealed-beam or regular headlight with reflector placed in a No. 10 can, wires extended out from the bottom, the lamp secured so it cannot move; the can is tied to a strong forked stake with a crossbar extending at least 1 inch on each side, the stake placed in a hole at the desired location, and guide lines and power lines run to it.

**Other devices in the manual's figures:** trip-wire and trip-flare arrangements, thermate grenades with dry logs, closed gas cans (5 to 10 gallons), 55-gallon drums with sand or dirt and thickened fuel mixture, and metal reflectors with old tires and tubes.

## Selection of the Means

**Capabilities compared (Chapter 9, Section I):** illuminating shells are true pyrotechnics — after firing, the shell bursts in the air and ejects a burning canister of illuminant that descends by parachute; one shell lights 500–2,000 meters in diameter. Searchlights give maximum illumination in the direct role, but the observer-to-target distance matters more than the searchlight's range (normally under 4,000 meters; 2,000 meters most effective). Army aircraft can illuminate large areas adjacent to and immediately in front of friendly elements, limited by flare-carrying and fuel capacity — minimized by using two aircraft and resupplying from forward landing areas. Airborne illumination systems (AVQ-5) serve landing zones, target acquisition, surveillance, reconnaissance, convoy escort, and search and rescue. Improvised illuminators are covered in Chapter 8 and FM 20-33.

**Indirect illumination:** artificial moonlight, by diffusion or reflection from a defilade position (see the searchlights reference). Diffusion gives up to about quarter-moon intensity on dark clear nights; reflection from good cloud cover gives nearly full-moon intensity.

**Factors influencing selection (paragraph 9-4):** (1) enemy and friendly situation; (2) purpose or use of the illumination; (3) number and characteristics of illumination means available; (4) time available for planning and preparation; (5) area to be illuminated and its size; (6) intensity required; (7) degree of control required; (8) duration of time the area is to be illuminated; (9) terrain, vegetation, and structures; (10) weather and atmospheric conditions; (11) vulnerability of available means to enemy countermeasures; (12) economy of use of available means; (13) communication between the unit requesting and the source providing the illumination.

## Limiting Factors

**Weather and terrain (paragraph 10-1):** rain has a greater effect on direct illumination than on indirect. High vegetation, trees, shrubs, and structures cast shadows in prolongation of the searchlight beam and greatly reduce direct searchlight effectiveness; flares normally provide the best illumination of areas covered with high vegetation or structures, since a flare is affected only by conditions in its immediate area while a searchlight is affected by conditions over its whole range. Solid cloud cover with clear atmosphere between illuminant and ground may enhance coverage by reflecting light; high thin cloud is a poor reflective surface. High winds rapidly blow flares away from the area, materially reducing illumination time; a strong gusting or shifting wind may blow a flare toward friendly troops and inadvertently illuminate their position, or land in friendly territory and ignite a grass or brush fire. High winds have no effect on searchlight illumination — when winds are extremely high or blowing from an unfavorable direction, searchlights may be the only satisfactory means.

**Vulnerability to countermeasures (paragraph 10-3):** any means employed continuously is susceptible to neutralization; when practical, establish an alternate means capable of illuminating the same area, and for long periods use two separate means (for example, searchlight and aircraft) alternating. Aircraft are the most vulnerable — Army aircraft fly at low altitudes and relatively slow speeds and must return over the same point, at the same altitude, at about 3-minute intervals throughout the period illumination is required; the burning flares between aircraft and ground blind ground troops to some extent and reduce the effectiveness of manually-aimed weapons against the aircraft. Searchlights are less vulnerable: in the direct role use a minimum of two in separate locations, alternating; turn them off and move immediately to an alternate position when the enemy begins an artillery adjustment on them, or hand the mission to a searchlight from another position or to an aircraft (which can also direct counterbattery fire). To degrade location by enemy aircraft short of turning off, focus the beam on the aircraft to blind the pilot and provide a focal point for friendly weapons. Artillery and mortar shells are comparatively free from neutralization because countering them requires neutralizing the weapon; artillery and mortar units should be prepared to assume an illumination mission if aircraft and searchlights are neutralized. Illuminating grenades have no direct countermeasure and must be destroyed; trip flares and improvised devices may be rendered ineffective by artillery or mortar fire or by inactivation, which requires enemy troops on the ground — so all emplaced devices must be covered by friendly fires.

**Safety (paragraph 10-4):** the carrier (empty projectile body) of a projectile-delivered illuminating round exceeds the range determined for a non-functioning round as listed in the firing tables; the functioning of the expelling charge accelerates the carrier while expelling the payload. Delivery units must consider this and inform the appropriate headquarters and requester of the danger. Evaluation of areas in possible danger can be predetermined by illumination planning, and delivery units can be selected to lessen or eliminate the hazard. When the hazard cannot be eliminated, other means should be employed or the commander must accept the risk. The artillery unit will never refuse illumination support but will make every attempt to notify friendly personnel in danger areas. A practical solution: consider the non-functioning round impact point as the center of an area of projectile debris (some projectiles may tumble after functioning and lose range) and mark an area around it. Firing tests concluded the carrier exceeds the non-functioning range; further tests were required to determine the debris pattern and exact carrier range.

**Night-vision recovery:** once artificial daylight is commenced it must continue without interruption until the need is satisfied — it temporarily eliminates night vision, and during an interruption supported troops may be incapable of seeing in the resulting darkness. About 15 minutes are required for troops to regain night vision.
