# Ground Devices and Illuminating Shells — FM 20-60

## Ground Signals

**Role:** primarily signaling; minimal illumination. Available to the individual or small unit, they can provide emergency close-in illumination for short periods. Not suitable for continuous illumination; little application outside defensive operations.

**M127A1 (signal, illumination, ground, white star, parachute, handheld):** 50,000 candlepower; ~36 seconds burning time; descent 3–4.5 meters per second; ~450 meters maximum range; ~200 meters diameter of area illuminated; 5-second delay fuze; hand fired, no weapon or launcher required. Other ground signal devices (rifle-projected and handheld) are listed in TM 9-1370-200.

**Firing angle:** high angle (45°–90°) uses the full burning time and illuminates the largest area; low angle (15°–45°) gives greater range and a lower burst to pinpoint or silhouette enemy troops; below 15° gives a low airburst with burnout on the ground.

**Control:** used by the individual, squad leader, or platoon leader per the appropriate commander's instructions. When used solely for signaling, plan and coordinate within the unit and with adjacent units well in advance; standardize signal meanings within and between major units, especially for emergency signals. When used for limited illumination, consider the effect on adjacent units so the flare is not mistaken for a true signal. Outposts, security personnel, and perimeter troops use ground signals to detect infiltrating enemy and as a harassing means to discourage infiltration; use by ambush or patrol troops discloses their location immediately.

## Illuminating Grenades

**Mark 1:** the only grenade designed specifically for battlefield illumination and the only one practicable for it. Incendiary and white phosphorus smoke grenades are of little value for illumination and should not be used for it. 55,000 candlepower; ~25 seconds burning time; ~200 meters maximum range; 7-second delay fuze. Hand-thrown, employed as a trip flare, or activated by remote control. Not suitable for continuous illumination; little application outside defensive operations.

**Use:** outpost personnel, perimeter defense, ambush positions, and night patrols use it to locate and deliver accurate fire on enemy troops.

**Cautions:** on soggy or swampy ground it may sink and produce less than expected illumination. The compound burns with a very hot, intense flame and often starts fires in dry grass, leaves, or brush; thatched roofs are easily ignited. As a trip flare, anchor the grenade securely with the safety pin and pull ring positioned to be easily pulled; account for the 7-second delay in remote activation. A reflector behind the grenade directs and intensifies the light toward the expected area of enemy movement.

## Trip Flares

**M49 and M49A1 (flare, surface, trip):** 40,000 candlepower; burn with a yellowish-white light for approximately 1 minute; ~300 meters maximum range; fixed to a stake or tree, detonated by pull-pin release or trigger release action. When arming either flare, follow the cautions in TM 9-1370-200.

**Use:** early warning of attacking or infiltrating enemy troops; placed along and across likely avenues of approach, in barbed-wire barriers with mines, and in gaps between defensive positions. Not suitable for continuous illumination, but may be used under emergency conditions for temporary close-in illumination.

## Artillery and Mortar Illuminating Shells

**The shell:** all artillery and mortar illuminating shells then in production contain a parachute-supported flare producing sustained high-candlepower illumination. Primary function is illumination; also used for target identification and signaling. One shell lights an area 500–2,000 meters in diameter; used for close-in and intermediate-area illumination depending on weapon and shell. Provided for 105-mm, 155-mm, and 8-inch artillery and for 81-mm and 4.2-inch mortars (technical data in the manual's Table 2; specific models in TM 9-1300-203 for artillery and TM 9-1300-206 for mortars).

**Control:** artillery illuminating shells are centrally controlled — requests through normal artillery channels, clearance from the division headquarters concerned before use within the division zone of action. In stability operations, control may be delegated to commanders of outposts or isolated units who have field artillery under their control. Mortar illuminating shell control is exercised by the commander having overall control of the operation, after coordination with adjacent units. See paragraph 10-4 for hazards of employment.

**Height of burst:** for maximum effective illumination the flare should burst over the target at approximately the same range as the observer-target distance, at a height that lets it burn out just above the ground. Too high and the flare drifts and burns out on the ground, wasting burning time; too low and coverage shrinks. A low burst may be desirable to avoid illuminating a nearby friendly position, to mark targets for aircraft, or to silhouette the enemy. Strong wind requires increased height of burst or an upwind shift of the aim point. Flares should not be fired where they may start fires that cause smoke and haze.

**Adjustment types:**

| Type | Method |
|---|---|
| One-gun | One round from a single piece, adjusted for the mission |
| Two-gun | Two rounds fired simultaneously, same time setting, deflection, and range |
| Two-gun lateral spread | Two guns bursting simultaneously at the same range, separated laterally; normal spread 800 meters between flares; normal method for ground offensive and defensive operations |
| Two-gun range spread | Two guns bursting simultaneously at different ranges along the gun-target line; normal spread 800 meters; illuminates an area in depth |
| Four-gun | One round from each of four guns bursting simultaneously in a diamond pattern |
| Illumination for HE adjustment | Observer requests CONTINUOUS ILLUMINATION while adjusting HE fire; if the target is immobile or illumination must be conserved, request COORDINATED ILLUMINATION, control BY SHELL, AT MY COMMAND |
| Self-illumination for close defense | 105-mm and 155-mm batteries illuminate their own positions; with charges and fuzes prepared in advance, excellent self-illumination within two minutes |
| Starlight illumination | 105-mm and 155-mm rounds fired to burst 6,000 meters beyond and 1,000 meters above a target provide sufficient light for night-vision devices |

**Adjustment procedures:** procedures for adjusting 105-mm and 155-mm howitzer illuminating shells by observers follow FM 6-40. Factors affecting employment include height of burst, wind, visibility, and the flare's descent rate.

**Continuous illumination:** sustaining it for longer than a few minutes requires a dedicated firing unit and an adequate supply of illuminating ammunition; the manual's Table 2 gives the continuous-illumination rate (rounds per minute) for each caliber.
