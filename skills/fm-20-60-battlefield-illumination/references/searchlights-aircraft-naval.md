# Searchlights, Aircraft Flares, and Naval Shell — FM 20-60

## Searchlights

**Employment:** searchlights give a responsive, reliable light source capable of continuous light for long periods with minor maintenance. A minimum of one field artillery searchlight battery is organic to corps artillery, with the mission of furnishing direct and indirect illumination in support of tactical operations. Searchlights in a secondary role must not interfere with their primary employment.

**Direct illumination:** the beam is placed directly on the target for maximum illumination. The most effective range at which an observer can reasonably be expected to detect a majority of targets is 2,000 meters; observers beyond 4,000 meters from the target are generally unable to distinguish vehicle types under direct illumination. Most effective observation is with the observer within 1,500 meters of the target. Night-vision devices further enhance detection. Two searchlights increase effectiveness by 50 percent over one. Detailed direct-mode employment is in FM 6-115.

**Indirect illumination (artificial moonlight):** the searchlight is employed from a defilade position, by diffusion or reflection. Both the 23-inch and 30-inch xenon searchlights may be used in the indirect role.

- **Diffusion:** the beam is elevated until approximately 98 percent of it passes the crest of a ground mask; light scattered by atmospheric particles illuminates the area beneath and to the flanks of the beam. Best results at elevation less than 75 mils, optimum between 25 and 50 mils with a focused beam. On dark clear nights the maximum illumination by diffusion is approximately equal to a quarter moon. Aimed fire can be directed about 525 meters forward of observers with a 23-inch searchlight (focused, 50 mils) and about 1,300 meters with a 30-inch (spread beam, 25 mils). Intensity can be increased laterally with two searchlights; range is not increased by diffusion. Enemy troops are not adversely affected by diffusion and may enjoy the advantage of being silhouetted if they break the skyline — and enemy flash bases can rapidly locate searchlights used this way.
- **Reflection:** the beam is directed against low-lying clouds (150–900 meters above ground) or another reflective surface. Best cloud condition is 100 percent coverage; satisfactory illumination needs a minimum of 60 percent. Intensity is nearly equivalent to full moonlight with good coverage, softly shadowed. A single searchlight, platoon, or battery illuminates about the same area by reflection as by diffusion (not necessarily the same intensity). Illumination may also be reflected from smoke clouds placed above or beyond the objective by aircraft or artillery fire; effectiveness depends on how quickly wind dissipates the clouds.

**Types (xenon family):** 15-inch AN/VSS-3, 23-inch, and 30-inch AN/TVS-5. The 23-inch was issued to artillery searchlight batteries in lieu of the larger 30-inch carbon-arc light, at a basis of two 23-inch for each 30-inch authorized; the 30-inch xenon is the standard item for artillery searchlight units. All have an infrared ("pink" mode, using pink light filters) capability for use with night-vision devices; a pencil beam proved best for infrared operation. The manual notes testing of the xenon searchlights was not complete and the data are based on limited testing — ranges are planning estimates, valid under the stated conditions, and ranges beyond 7,500 meters were not tested. Xenon searchlights generate radio-frequency energy in the 1.5–2.5 MHz range that may interfere with AM radios in proximity; initial testing indicated FM radios were not affected.

## Aircraft Flares

**Mk 24 aircraft flare:** the flare most commonly employed by Army aircraft. Requests are submitted in the same manner as requests for other air support missions, through the Tactical Air Support Element at the Fire Support Coordination Center; control of Army aviation flares rests with the aviation officer. Direct communication between the ground unit and the aircraft is necessary for timely response.

**Release altitude and fuze settings:** wind causes flares to drift and must be accounted for in determining the drop point; the best burst altitude must be determined to use the full burning time. Initial flares should be dropped well upwind of the target until drift and burst altitude are established. Fuzes must be set so the flare burns out before striking the surface — flares are likely to ignite combustible materials. The flare falls approximately 300 feet during the first 5 seconds, 700 feet during the next 5 seconds, and approximately 1,000 feet for each 5 seconds thereafter; a Mk 24 Mod 2A burns for approximately 1,125 feet of fall. Minimal flight altitudes are based on burnout at approximately 350 feet above ground. Procedures for other flare types are in TM 9-1370-200.

**Flare-carrying capacity (Mk 24 Mod 5):** O-1A 12, U-1A 60, U-6A 24, UH-1A&B 30, UD-1D 44, CH-47A 300. Maximum planning time for continuous illumination is (burning time × number of flares) × 5/6, limited by fuel capacity — about 30 minutes for the O-1A, 150 minutes for the U-1A, 60 for the U-6A, 75 for the UH-1, 110 for the UD-1D. The U.S. Air Force C-130 may be equipped with large-volume automatic flare dispensers accommodating high-speed launching of up to 400 Mark 24 flares; tactical aircraft with the SUU-25 dispenser carry eight Mark 24 flares.

**Training:** aviators must be trained in handling, storage, and proper installation of flares and in releasing them at correct altitudes, including jettisoning carriers or flares in emergencies. Frequent training exercises, including radar-vectoring missions, should be scheduled and coordinated with supported units.

## Airborne Illumination Systems

**Light Set, General Illuminating, Airborne AVQ-5** (mounted in a C-123): 28 liquid-cooled xenon arc lamps installed in the aircraft doors, powered by a 400-cycle, 200-KVA generator set. A 6-million candlepower beam in a 50-degree cone. At 12,000 feet altitude the cone covers about 3.5 square miles with illumination comparable to four times that of a full moon (equal to three Mark 24 flares); at 6,000 feet it covers about one square mile at 16 times full-moon brightness. Continuous light for 1½ hours. Also used to illuminate staging areas, drop zones, disaster areas, logistical depots, bridging sites, and port facilities, and for search and rescue. Airborne illumination systems mounted on fixed- or rotary-wing aircraft may be used in the direct role for illuminating landing zones, target acquisition and engagement, surveillance and reconnaissance, and convoy escort.

## Naval Illuminating Shell

Available for 5-inch and 6-inch naval guns only. Maximum ranges: 5-inch/38 — 16,400 meters; 5-inch/54 — 23,600 meters; 6-inch — 21,100 meters. Actual inland range depends on the distance the ship must stay from shore. The supply of illuminating shell on a ship is limited and expenditure must be controlled. Troop support by naval illuminating shell is requested through the naval gunfire spotter or naval gunfire liaison officer, with information similar to that required for aircraft flare illumination; final assignment of ships depends on availability and the tactical situation. Destroyers and light cruisers are normally assigned to troop-unit support. See paragraph 10-4 for hazards associated with employment.
