# FM 20-60 Battlefield Illumination

## Purpose

FM 20-60, *Battlefield Illumination* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, January 1970, 79 pages) — the Army's guide for commanders and staff at all echelons employing battlefield illumination. It covers the principles of employment, the characteristics and capabilities of the various illumination means, and the considerations in selecting illumination means for given missions. The material is stated to apply without modification to nuclear and nonnuclear warfare, general war, limited war, and cold war operations, including internal defense and internal development assistance. The manual supersedes FM 20-40, 19 October 1964.

**Historic status — read this first.** This is a 1970 manual. Its equipment (M127A1 ground signals, Mark 1 illuminating grenades, M49 trip flares, carbon-arc and early xenon searchlights, Mk 24 aircraft flares) and its organizations are of that era, and much of the materiel has long since been replaced. Current Army illumination doctrine lives in newer publications. Use FM 20-60 for its enduring principles — the purposes of illumination, the selection factors, the coordination discipline, and the limiting factors — and treat its equipment data as a historical record, not a current inventory.

Battlefield illumination gives friendly forces light to operate in darkness and makes enemy movement more hazardous. It makes offensive operations more effective, aids organizing and conducting the defense, and through harassing and interdictory illumination denies areas and routes to the enemy while helping aircraft detect and attack enemy troops, weapons emplacements, command posts, base camps, repair facilities, and supply installations. In rear areas it permits maintenance, repair, recovery, and evacuation of vehicles and equipment, and expedites bridging, road repair, and construction. As illumination intensity approaches daylight, daylight tactics and techniques can be employed.

## 1. Definitions — Areas, Intensity, and Kinds of Light

**1.1. Areas to be illuminated.** The manual groups illumination by distance from friendly forces:

- **Close-in illumination** — the area in the immediate vicinity of friendly individuals or units, extending outward about **2,000 meters**, roughly the effective range of direct-fire weapons.
- **Intermediate area illumination** — from the forward edge of the close-in area (about 2,000 meters) out to about **15,000 to 18,000 meters**, the maximum effective range of the bulk of division artillery cannon weapons.
- **Deep illumination** — from the far edge of the intermediate area out to about **160 kilometers**.

**1.2. Illumination intensity.**

- **Artificial moonlight** — illumination of an intensity between that of starlight and that of a full moon. Produced by indirect (diffused or reflected) searchlight employment.
- **Artificial daylight** — illumination approaching daylight intensity, produced by direct searchlight employment or by artillery illuminating shells and aircraft flares at high intensity. Once artificial daylight is commenced it must continue without interruption until the need is satisfied: it temporarily eliminates night vision, and during an interruption supported troops may be incapable of seeing in the resulting darkness, at the mercy of an enemy who kept his eyes closed or used filtering goggles. About **15 minutes** are required for troops to regain night vision.

**1.3. Purposes in the offense.** Illumination may be used to assist patrols and night reconnaissance and to conceal diversionary actions; facilitate the advance by aiding direction, terrain orientation, and control of troops; site close-support direct-fire weapons; locate and detect enemy positions; blind the defending enemy while promoting troop confidence and morale; facilitate detection and removal of minefields and reduction of obstacles; permit more rapid displacement of supporting arms; facilitate handling and servicing of weapons and vehicles; facilitate embarking and debarking of watercraft, bridging and river-crossing operations, and road repairs; attain more distant objectives by increasing control and expediting forward movement of reserves and support units; provide deception (for example, illuminating a rear area to deceive the enemy about the true area of operations); improve airstrike effectiveness and aid adjustment of artillery fires; facilitate on-site repair, recovery, and evacuation of materiel; facilitate shore-party operations in an amphibious operation once the beachhead is secured; and assist airmobile operations.

**1.4. Purposes in the defense.** Illumination may be used to facilitate organization of defensive positions (reconnaissance, siting direct-fire weapons, adjusting indirect fires, laying minefields, erecting wire, building obstacles and foxholes); facilitate early detection and blinding of an attacking enemy and placing effective fires on them; promote confidence and morale; discourage enemy patrol action, infiltration, and night attacks; deny the enemy freedom of movement and canalize his movements into established kill zones; facilitate night counterattacks; facilitate communications (wire laying, antenna erection, messenger movement); facilitate tactical and administrative movement of troops, vehicles, and supplies; facilitate location, treatment, and evacuation of casualties; facilitate displacing, handling, and servicing weapons and vehicles; facilitate search and rescue; facilitate security of critical installations in internal-defense operations; and assist night aerial resupply and medical evacuation. → `references/ground-devices-and-shells.md`

## 2. Principles of Employment

**2.1. Provide it wherever and whenever needed.** Battlefield illumination must be provided in the intensity required and throughout the period required. It can frequently be the critical factor that influences the course of battle in favor of friendly troops; insufficient or improper use could result in failure of the mission. It is particularly important as a means of counterbalancing a lack of training or experience in night attacks.

**2.2. Employment is the commander's responsibility.** The decision to employ illumination — type, means, degree, and area — must be made by the commander after considering the effects illumination of one area will have on units in adjacent areas.

**2.3. Maximize the user's advantage.** Wherever possible, use a means that gives maximum advantage to the user — for example, an illumination means other than organic weapons frees those weapons for their primary mission of conventional supporting fires.

**2.4. Prefer searchlights, airborne systems, and aircraft flares.** Illumination should be provided by searchlights, airborne illumination systems, and aircraft flares wherever and whenever practicable.

**2.5. Provide it from the highest level practicable.** This conserves the illuminants available to lower echelons so they are prepared at all times to meet emergency requirements. Each ground unit should have enough means to initiate the mission and maintain it until a higher echelon assumes it; if it cannot do so with organic means, higher headquarters must make illumination support available before the unit initiates the operation. 

**2.6. Keep artillery shells in reserve.** Artillery illuminating shells must be available for delivery to firing positions by vehicle, helicopter lift, or other means whenever needed, to ensure continuous illumination if the primary means fails.

**2.7. Coordinate at the highest level practicable.** Illuminants frequently light areas outside the using unit's zone and may disclose adjacent units' operations to the enemy. Units planning illumination should coordinate directly with adjacent units whenever it might possibly affect them.

**2.8. Keep an alternate means ready.** An alternate means (artillery illuminating shells, searchlights, airborne systems, aircraft flares) for intermediate-area illumination must always be maintained.

**2.9. Coordinate with supporting aircraft.** Battlefield illumination by artillery or naval gunfire for an engaged unit must be closely coordinated with all aircraft supporting that unit.

## 3. Ground Signals, Illuminating Grenades, and Trip Flares

**3.1. Ground signals.** Used primarily for signaling, they provide only a minimal amount of illumination, but since they are normally available to the individual or small unit they can provide emergency close-in illumination for short periods. They are not suitable for continuous illumination. The most common device in small units is the **M127A1** signal, illumination, ground, white star, parachute (handheld): **50,000 candlepower**, about **36 seconds** burning time, descent about **3 to 4.5 meters per second**, about **450 meters** maximum range, about **200 meters** diameter of area illuminated, **5-second delay** fuze, hand fired without a weapon or special launcher. Firing angle controls the effect: a high angle (45° to 90°) gives a height of burst that uses the full burning time and illuminates the largest area; a low angle (15° to 45°) gives greater range and a lower burst, placing the flare over or behind enemy troops to pinpoint or silhouette them; below 15° gives a low airburst with burnout on the ground. Used solely for signaling, it requires prior planning and coordination within the unit and with adjacent units well in advance, and standardized signal meanings within and between major units — especially for emergency signals.  → `references/ground-devices-and-shells.md`

**3.2. Illuminating grenades.** The **Mark 1** illuminating grenade was designed specifically for battlefield illumination and is the only grenade practicable for the purpose; incendiary and white phosphorus smoke grenades are of little value for illumination and should not be used for it. The Mark 1: **55,000 candlepower**, about **25 seconds** burning time, about **200 meters** maximum range, **7-second delay** fuze; it may be thrown by hand, employed as a trip flare, or activated by remote control. It is not suitable for continuous illumination. Outpost personnel, perimeter-defense troops, ambush positions, and night patrols use it to locate and deliver accurate fire on enemy troops. Cautions: on soggy or swampy ground it may sink and produce less than expected illumination; the illuminating compound burns with a very hot and intense flame and often starts fires in dry grass, leaves, or brush, and thatched roofs are easily ignited. As a trip flare, anchor it securely with the safety pin and pull ring positioned to be easily pulled, and account for the 7-second delay fuze in remote activation. 

**3.3. Trip flares.** The **M49 and M49A1** surface trip flares burn with a yellowish-white light for approximately **1 minute** at **40,000 candlepower**, with a maximum range of about **300 meters**, and can be detonated by pull-pin release or trigger release action. They are used to provide early warning of attacking or infiltrating enemy troops, placed along and across likely avenues of approach, in barbed-wire barriers with mines, and in gaps between defensive positions. When arming either flare, follow the cautions in TM 9-1370-200. Trip flares are not suitable for continuous illumination, but may be used under emergency conditions to provide temporary close-in illumination.

## 4. Artillery and Mortar Illuminating Shells

**4.1. The shell.** All artillery and mortar illuminating shells then in production contain a parachute-supported flare producing sustained high-candlepower illumination. Their primary function is illumination, though they may also be used for target identification and signaling. One illuminating shell lights an area **500 to 2,000 meters in diameter** and is used for close-in and intermediate-area illumination depending on weapon and shell. Illuminating shells are provided for 105-mm, 155-mm, and 8-inch artillery and for 81-mm and 4.2-inch mortars. Artillery illuminating-shell control is centralized: requests go through normal artillery channels, with clearance from division headquarters before use within the division zone of action; in stability operations control may be delegated to commanders of outposts or isolated units. Mortar illuminating-shell control rests with the commander having overall control of the operation, after coordination with adjacent units.

**4.2. Height of burst.** For maximum effective illumination for the ground observer, the flare should burst at a point in the air over the target area at approximately the same range as the observer-target distance — the correct height of burst is normally that which allows the flare to burn out just above the ground. If the shell bursts too high, the flare drifts and burns out on the ground, wasting the latter part of its burning time; if too low, coverage is reduced. A low burst may be desirable to avoid illuminating a nearby friendly position, to mark targets for aircraft, or to silhouette the enemy. Strong wind requires an increase in the height of burst or a shift of the aim point upwind. → `references/ground-devices-and-shells.md`

**4.3. Types of adjustment.** The size and shape of the area, observer-target distance, visibility, and shell candlepower influence the selection:

- **One-gun illumination** — one round from a single piece, adjusted for the mission.
- **Two-gun illumination** — two rounds fired simultaneously with the same time setting, deflection, and range, for increased illumination during the period.
- **Two-gun lateral spread** — two guns bursting simultaneously at the same range but separated laterally; normal spread **800 meters** between flares; the normal method for ground offensive and defensive operations.
- **Two-gun range spread** — two guns bursting simultaneously at different ranges along the gun-target line; normal spread **800 meters**; illuminates an area in depth.
- **Four-gun illumination** — one round from each of four guns bursting simultaneously in a diamond pattern, a combination of the lateral and range spreads.
- **Illumination for HE adjustment** — the observer requests CONTINUOUS ILLUMINATION while adjusting HE fire on the target; if the target is immobile or illumination must be conserved, request COORDINATED ILLUMINATION with control BY SHELL, AT MY COMMAND.
- **Self-illumination for close defense** — 105-mm and 155-mm batteries can illuminate their own positions; with charges and fuzes prepared in advance a battery can provide itself excellent self-illumination within two minutes.
- **Starlight illumination** — 105-mm and 155-mm rounds fired to burst 6,000 meters beyond and 1,000 meters above a target provide sufficient light for night-vision devices.

**4.4. Continuous illumination.** Continuous illumination for periods longer than a few minutes requires a sustained supply of illuminating ammunition and a firing unit dedicated to the mission. Adjustment procedures for 105-mm and 155-mm howitzer illuminating shells follow FM 6-40.

## 5. Searchlights

**5.1. Employment.** Searchlights give the commander a responsive, reliable light source capable of delivering continuous light for long periods with minor maintenance. A minimum of one field artillery searchlight battery is organic to corps artillery, with the mission of furnishing direct and indirect illumination in support of tactical operations. Searchlights in a secondary role must not interfere with their primary employment.

**5.2. Direct illumination.** The beam is placed directly on the target for maximum illumination of the target. The most effective range at which an observer can reasonably be expected to detect a majority of targets is **2,000 meters**; observers at ranges greater than **4,000 meters** from the target are generally unable to distinguish vehicle types under direct illumination. Most effective observation is obtained when the observer is within **1,500 meters** of the target. Night-vision devices further enhance detection. Two searchlights increase effectiveness by 50 percent over a single light.

**5.3. Indirect illumination (artificial moonlight).** Provided by diffusion or reflection of the beam from a defilade position:

- **Diffusion** — the beam is elevated until approximately 98 percent of it passes the crest of a ground mask; light scattered by atmospheric particles illuminates the area beneath and to the flanks of the beam. Best results at an elevation of less than 75 mils, optimum between **25 and 50 mils** using a focused beam. On dark clear nights the maximum illumination obtainable by diffusion is approximately equal to the light of a quarter moon. 
- **Reflection** — the beam is directed against low-lying clouds (**150 to 900 meters** above ground) or another reflective surface. Best cloud condition is 100 percent coverage; satisfactory illumination needs a minimum of **60 percent**. Intensity is nearly equivalent to full moonlight with good coverage, and the illumination is softly shadowed. Reflected illumination may also be obtained from smoke clouds placed above or beyond the objective by aircraft or artillery, though effectiveness depends on how quickly wind dissipates the clouds.

**5.4. Types.** The Army family of xenon searchlights: **15-inch AN/VSS-3**, **23-inch**, and **30-inch AN/TVS-5**. The 23-inch was issued to artillery searchlight batteries in lieu of the larger 30-inch carbon-arc light, at a basis of two 23-inch for each 30-inch authorized; the 30-inch xenon is the standard item for artillery searchlight units. All have an infrared capability ("pink" mode, using pink light filters) for use with night-vision devices; a pencil beam proved best for infrared operation. The manual notes that testing of the xenon searchlights was not complete and the data are based on limited testing — ranges listed are planning estimates. Xenon searchlights generate radio-frequency energy in the **1.5–2.5 MHz** range that may interfere with AM radios in proximity. → `references/searchlights-aircraft-naval.md`

## 6. Aircraft Flares and Airborne Illumination

**6.1. Aircraft flares.** The **Mk 24** aircraft flare is the flare most commonly employed by Army aircraft. Flares are requested in the same manner as other air support missions, through the Tactical Air Support Element at the Fire Support Coordination Center; control of Army aviation flares rests with the aviation officer, and direct communication between the ground unit and the aircraft is necessary for timely response. Wind causes flares to drift and must be accounted for in determining the drop point; the best altitude for burst must be determined to use the full burning time. Initial flares should be dropped well upwind of the target until drift and burst altitude are established. Fuzes must be set so the flare burns out before striking the surface. The flare falls approximately **300 feet during the first 5 seconds, 700 feet during the next 5 seconds, and approximately 1,000 feet for each 5 seconds thereafter**; a Mk 24 Mod 2A burns for approximately **1,125 feet of fall**. Minimal flight altitudes are based on burnout at approximately **350 feet** above ground. Aviators must be trained in flare handling, storage, installation, and release at correct altitudes.

**6.2. Flare-carrying capacity.** Army aircraft flare capacity (Mk 24 Mod 5) ranges from **12 flares in an O-1A** to **300 in a CH-47A**; maximum planning time for continuous illumination is computed as (burning time × number of flares) × 5/6, limited by fuel capacity — for example, about 30 minutes for the O-1A and 150 minutes for the U-1A. The U.S. Air Force **C-130** may be equipped with large-volume automatic flare dispensers accommodating up to **400 Mark 24 flares**; tactical aircraft with the SUU-25 dispenser carry eight Mark 24 flares.

**6.3. Airborne illumination systems.** The **Light Set, General Illuminating, Airborne AVQ-5**, mounted in a C-123, consists of 28 liquid-cooled xenon arc lamps installed in the aircraft doors, powered by a 400-cycle, 200-KVA generator set. It provides a **6-million candlepower** beam in a 50-degree cone: at **12,000 feet** altitude the cone covers about **3.5 square miles** with illumination comparable to four times that of a full moon (equal to three Mark 24 flares); at **6,000 feet** it covers about one square mile at 16 times full-moon brightness. It can provide a continuous light source for **1½ hours**. It is also used to illuminate staging areas, drop zones, disaster areas, logistical depots, bridging sites, and port facilities, and for search and rescue.

## 7. Naval Illuminating Shell and Improvised Means

**7.1. Naval illuminating shell.** Naval illuminating shells are available for **5-inch and 6-inch** naval guns only. Maximum ranges: **5-inch/38, 16,400 meters**; **5-inch/54, 23,600 meters**; **6-inch, 21,100 meters**. Actual inland range depends on how far the ship must stay from shore; the supply of illuminating shell on a ship is limited and expenditure must be controlled. Requests go through the naval gunfire spotter or naval gunfire liaison officer, with information similar to that required for aircraft flare illumination. Destroyers and light cruisers are normally assigned to troop-unit support. See paragraph 10-4 for hazards.

**7.2. Improvised means.** Chapter 8 and FM 20-33 describe field-expedient illumination devices that have been successfully employed on the battlefield:

- **Fires** — thatched roofs, grass, brush, or woods set alight by white phosphorus shells, illuminating shells, flares, or tracer ammunition; besides harassing the enemy, these fires often provide sufficient illumination to silhouette enemy forces.
- **High-danger flare** — a No. 10 can partially filled with sand saturated with a mixture of lubricating oil and gasoline, placed on a likely route of approach and ignited; burns for several hours and silhouettes movement.
- **Flame illuminator** — a 5- to 55-gallon container filled with thickened fuel, with detonating cord secured along the inside of the top rim and a detonating-cord/white-phosphorus grenade assembly attached so the explosive charge and grenade function simultaneously to ignite the fuel. For electrical firing, replace the nonelectric system with an electric blasting cap. **Caution: before attaching electric blasting cap lead wires to the firing wire, definitely ascertain that the firing wire is not connected to a power source.**
- **Husch flare** — one or more metal cylindrical tubes (such as propellant or shell containers) filled with thickened fuel, inserted into a larger metal container also containing thickened fuel; the fuel vaporizes and is expelled as a burning jet through a small hole. The example in the manual burns for about **1 hour**, illuminating an area about **90 meters** in diameter. It should be buried one-half to three-fourths in the ground or sandbagged for protection from fragments and small-arms fire and to prevent tipping; the pull or trip wire should extend to an activation point at least **25 meters** from the device.
- **Headlight device** — a sealed-beam or regular headlight with reflector in a No. 10 can, on a forked stake, powered by vehicle batteries. → `references/improvised-selection-limitations.md`

## 8. Selection of the Means

**8.1. Capabilities compared.** No one means is effective or suitable under all circumstances. One illuminating shell lights 500 to 2,000 meters in diameter; searchlights give maximum illumination in the direct role, but the observer-to-target distance matters more than the searchlight's range — it must normally be less than 4,000 meters, and 2,000 meters is the most effective detection range. Army aircraft can illuminate large areas adjacent to and immediately in front of friendly elements, limited by flare-carrying and fuel capacity — minimized by using two aircraft and resupplying from forward landing areas. 

**8.2. Factors influencing selection.** (1) Enemy and friendly situation; (2) purpose or use; (3) number and characteristics of means available; (4) time available for planning and preparation; (5) area to be illuminated and its size; (6) intensity required; (7) degree of control required; (8) duration of illumination; (9) terrain, vegetation, and structures; (10) weather and atmospheric conditions; (11) vulnerability of means to enemy countermeasures; (12) economy of use; (13) communication between the requesting unit and the providing source. Appendix D lists illumination requirements for various organizations; Appendix B is a checklist for planning continuous illumination in support of infantry and armor operations; Appendix C lists techniques in which artillery and mortar personnel must be trained; Appendix E compares searchlights and artillery illuminating shells; Appendix G covers infrared and night-viewing devices.

## 9. Limiting Factors

**9.1. Weather and terrain.** Rain affects direct illumination more than indirect; high vegetation, trees, shrubs, and structures cast shadows in prolongation of the searchlight beam and greatly reduce direct searchlight effectiveness — flares normally provide the best illumination of areas covered with high vegetation or structures. Solid cloud cover with clear atmosphere between illuminant and ground may enhance coverage by reflecting light; high thin cloud is a poor reflective surface for indirect illumination. High winds rapidly blow flares away from the area, reducing illumination time, and a strong gusting or shifting wind may blow a flare toward friendly troops or land in friendly territory and ignite a grass or brush fire. High winds have no effect on searchlight illumination, so when winds are extremely high or blowing from an unfavorable direction, searchlights may be the only satisfactory means.

**9.2. Vulnerability to enemy countermeasures.** Any means employed continuously is susceptible to neutralization; when practical, an alternate means capable of illuminating the same area should be established, and for long periods two separate means (for example, searchlight and aircraft) should alternate. Aircraft are the most vulnerable means — Army aircraft fly at low altitudes and relatively slow speeds, and must return over the same point, at the same altitude, at about **3-minute intervals** throughout the period illumination is required. Searchlights are less vulnerable; in the direct role a minimum of two in separate locations should alternate, and they should be turned off and immediately moved when the enemy begins an artillery adjustment on them — or the mission taken over by a searchlight from another position or by an aircraft. The most successful means of degrading the location of a searchlight by enemy aircraft, short of turning it off, is to focus the beam on the aircraft to blind the pilot and provide a focal point for friendly weapons. Artillery and mortar illuminating shells are comparatively free from neutralization because countering them requires neutralizing the weapon itself, so artillery and mortar units should be prepared to assume an illumination mission if aircraft and searchlights are neutralized. Illuminating grenades have no direct countermeasure — they must be destroyed; trip flares and improvised devices may be rendered ineffective by artillery or mortar fire or by inactivation, which requires enemy troops on the ground.

**9.3. Safety (paragraph 10-4).** Projectile-delivered illumination carries a debris hazard: the carrier (the empty projectile body) exceeds the range determined for a non-functioning round, and the functioning of the expelling charge accelerates the carrier while expelling the payload. Evaluation of areas in possible danger can be predetermined by illumination planning, and delivery units selected to lessen or eliminate the hazard; when it cannot be eliminated, other means should be employed or the commander must accept the risk. The artillery unit will never refuse illumination support but will make every attempt to notify friendly personnel in danger areas. A practical solution is to consider the non-functioning round impact point as the center of an area of projectile debris and mark an area around it.  → `references/improvised-selection-limitations.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a battlefield-illumination question from FM 20-60, give the purpose and the means first, then the numbers that make it work (candlepower, burning time, height of burst, spread, range), then the coordination and safety constraints. State which area (close-in, intermediate, deep) and which intensity (artificial moonlight or artificial daylight) the answer belongs to, and name the echelon that must provide or approve the illumination. Where the 1970 equipment differs from current issue, say so and treat the manual's data as historical.

## Operating Rules

1. **The commander decides.** Type, means, degree, and area of illumination are the commander's decision, coordinated at the highest level practicable.
2. **Provide from the highest level practicable.** Conserve lower echelons' illuminants for emergencies; keep an alternate means ready.
3. **Never interrupt artificial daylight.** Once commenced it continues without interruption; troops need about 15 minutes to regain night vision.
4. **Height of burst is the adjustment.** The flare should burn out just above the ground over the target; wind shifts the aim point upwind.
5. **Flares and grenades start fires.** Account for dry vegetation, thatch, and wind drift before firing.
6. **Debris falls downrange.** Projectile-delivered illumination puts the carrier beyond the non-functioning round's impact point — plan the danger area and warn friendly troops.
7. **Historic equipment.** The 1970 devices and data are a record, not a current inventory.
