# ST 31-205 Special Forces Caching Techniques

## Purpose

ST 31-205, *Special Forces Caching Techniques* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, undated, 39 pages) — a Special Forces training text on caching: hiding supplies and equipment in a secure place with a view to future recovery for operational use. It covers caching considerations and site selection, packaging, emplacement (burial, submersion, concealment), caching of communications equipment, the cache report, and recovery. An appendix gives the Twelve-Point Cache Report.

**Historic status — read this first.** This is an undated Cold War-era Special Forces text (its examples assume a Communist occupation, guerrilla bands, and resistance operations). Its tradecraft — site selection, pinpointing, packaging, and reporting — is the enduring content; its political assumptions and some of its materials are of its era.

**The governing rule.** Security is always the overriding consideration. Every decision — what to cache, where, how, and who knows — is judged against the risk that the cache will be discovered, compromised, or rendered inaccessible, and against the risk that discovery will compromise the people involved.

## 1. Caching Considerations

**Uses.** Three situations are particularly noteworthy: emergency needs where personnel may be barred from normal supply sources by sudden developments, or where travel documents and extra funds are needed for quick escape; supply problems of long-term operations conducted far from a secure base; and anticipated needs of wartime operations in areas likely to be overrun by the enemy.

**Initial considerations.** Planning starts with the **contents and purpose** of each cache: small barter items can be cached at any secure site, but rifles for a guerrilla band need an isolated site where the band can establish at least temporary control. Weigh **anticipated enemy action**, **local population activity** (chance discovery is probably more dangerous than deliberate enemy action), **Allied actions** (one site should not serve several covert operations), **packaging and transport**, and **personnel** (only the most reliable persons, no more than necessary).

**The three methods.** Choice depends on the particular situation; it is unsound to lay down general rules, with one exception: always think in terms of suitability. **Concealment** uses permanent man-made or natural features to hide or disguise the cache: emplacement and recovery take minimum time and labor, and a cache inside a building or dry cave is protected from the elements — but there is always the chance of accidental discovery. **Burial** is laborious and time-consuming, but once in place a properly buried cache is generally secure; its difficulties are the need for a high-quality container or special wrapping, the long time the work takes, and the difficulty of locating a buried cache. In most cases burial is the best way of achieving lasting security. **Submersion** requires a container meeting such high technical standards that field expedients are seldom workable, and at least two agents are usually essential. It suits only rare occasions — most notably maritime resupply. → `references/site-selection-and-pinpointing.md`

## 2. Criteria for the Site

**Accessibility — three questions.** (1) Can the site be located by simple instructions, unmistakably clear to someone who has never visited the location? (2) Are there at least two secure routes to the site, both providing natural concealment? (3) Can the cache be emplaced and recovered in all seasons? Snow and frozen ground make special problems for a buried cache.

**The alternate site.** Select an alternate site in case unforeseen difficulties prevent use of the best site — far enough away to be screened from view from the primary site, but near enough to reach without a second trip.

**The concealment site.** Concealment sites resemble drop sites, but must be far more durable, usually need less precise timing, and need greater capacity. A site that looks ideal may reveal itself to the opposition for that very reason. The only real key is inspired, meticulous casing combined with great familiarity with local residents and their customs. Likely sites: caves, abandoned mines and quarries, walls, abandoned buildings, infrequently used structures, memorial edifices, public buildings, ruins, culverts, and sewers. A building site must remain accessible as long as the cache may be needed; discovery must not compromise any person involved; and it must be physically secure. A custodian is inadvisable.

**The burial site.** **Drainage** — good drainage makes high ground preferable; moisture is one of the greatest natural threats; near a stream the cache must be well above the all-year high-water mark. **Ground cover** — deciduous roots make digging very difficult; tall grass reveals trampling, while leaves and humus can be replaced easily and conceal a refilled hole. **Concealment** — vegetation or terrain should conceal the parties; consider seasonal foliage changes. **Soil** — sandy loam is ideal; avoid clay (sticky when wet, rock-hard when dry). **Snow and freezing** — frozen ground impedes digging, and snow is especially hazardous: it is practically impossible to restore snow over an excavation to normal appearance. **Rocks** — probe the exact spot before digging.

**The submersion site.** A body of water must have characteristics determinable only by a thorough survey. Submersion usually requires a boat — first for reconnoitering, then for emplacement — so accessibility narrows to availability of a boat and action cover for using it.  → `references/site-selection-and-pinpointing.md`

## 3. Finding the Site

**Map survey.** Finding a suitable site usually requires a thorough systematic survey of the general area, first with as large a scale map as available: rule out sectors too near factories, homes, busy thoroughfares, or probable military targets, and look for roads or trails, natural concealment, and adequate drainage. A good military-type map also shows the reference points: stream confluences, dams, road junctures, villages, bridges, churches, and cemeteries.

**Personal reconnaissance.** A map survey normally yields several promising sites, but a well-qualified observer must examine each firsthand to select and pinpoint the best, equipped with maps, a compass, a drawing pad, a metallic measuring line (a wire knotted at regular intervals; twine or cloth tapes should not be used because stretching or shrinkage makes them inaccurate when wet), and a probe rod. Action cover is of utmost importance.

**Reference points.** The final and absolutely essential requirement is that the site can be located by simple, unmistakable instructions: the general area (place names down to the nearest village), an **immediate reference point (IRP)** — any durable landmark definitely identifiable by title or simple description — and a **final reference point (FRP)** meeting four requirements: definitely identifiable; fixed as long as the cache may be used; near enough to pinpoint the cache by precise linear measurements; and related to the IRP by a simple route description. 

**Pinpointing techniques (buried cache).** (1) **Directly beside the FRP** — the simplest method. (2) **Sighting by projection** — project a line along a flat side of the FRP, or between two FRPs; ordinarily unreliable beyond **50 meters**. (3) **Intersection of measured lines** — project one line from each of two FRPs; neither projected line should be more than twice the base line between them. (4) **Compass azimuth** — preferably to a cardinal point; no more than **10 meters** from the FRP.

**Measuring and marking.** Use whole numbers in a system the recovery agent understands (6 meters, not 6.3 or 6½); keep measurements simple and locate the cache accordingly. On uneven ground, measure on a direct line from point to point. Mark the point of emplacement during reconnaissance — for a night burial, during a daylight reconnaissance when possible — with a marker meaningless to an unwitting observer but easily recognized, such as a small rock or a branch with the butt placed at the selected point. During reconnaissance also gather the best route and at least one alternate route, security hazards, and information to overcome them. → `references/site-selection-and-pinpointing.md`

## 4. Packaging

**General.** Packaging involves not only packing the material but additional processing to protect it from all adverse storage conditions. The importance of proper packaging can scarcely be exaggerated — inadequate packaging very likely will result in the material being unusable when needed. Since special equipment and skilled technicians are needed for best results, packaging should be done at headquarters or a field packaging center whenever possible.

**Determining factors.** The first rule: all processing is tailored to the specific requirements of each cache. The method of packaging, and the size, shape, and weight of the package, are determined by the material, the caching method, and especially how the material will be recovered and used — if one man may have to recover the cache alone, the container should be no larger than a small suitcase and the total weight no more than **30 pounds**; even with more than one person, divide material into separate one-man packages whenever possible. Adverse storage conditions may include moisture, external pressure, freezing temperatures, bacteria and corrosive chemicals in soil and water, and animal life. The method of caching should be determined before packaging.

**Steps.** **Inspection** — equipment complete, serviceable, and free of corrosive substances. **Cleaning** — clean corrodible items thoroughly; handle contents with rubber or freshly cleaned cotton gloves (sweat corrodes metal; fingerprints may identify the packers). **Drying** — remove every trace of moisture by wiping, heating, or desiccant; heating is usually best: at least **three hours at about 110°F** (improvise an oven from a large metal can or drum); in a humid climate dry the oven first at **212°F**. **Coating** — light oil on unpainted metal. **Wrapping** — each article wrapped separately, waterproof, tight-fitting, folds sealed. **Packing** — remove all moisture from the container and pack a long-lasting desiccant inside (**15 kilograms of silica gel per cubic meter**); eliminate air pockets; use dried padding liberally; never let different metals touch. **Instructions** — simple, unmistakably clear, with self-explanatory diagrams. **Sealing** — lid sealed watertight (the closing joint is the most vulnerable point). **Submersion test** — submerge the sealed container and watch for air bubbles; use hot water if possible, since it reveals leaks a cold-water test would not.

**Wrapping materials.** The most important requirement is that the material be moistureproof, sealable, pliable enough to fit closely with tight folds, and tough enough to resist tearing and puncturing — combine a thin, pliable inner wrapping with a heavier outer wrapping. Recommended field expedients: **aluminum foil** (best widely available inner wrapping); **moisture-resistant papers** (inner wrapping to keep rubber or wax from sticking); **rubber repair gum** (excellent outer wrapping; 2 millimeters is the most satisfactory thickness; seal at least a half inch wide); **Grade "C" barrier material** (wax-impregnated cloth; self-sealing; outer wrapping over aluminum foil); **wax** (coating/sealant).

**Containers.** The ideal container is completely watertight and airtight after sealing, noiseless when handled, resistant to shock and abrasion, able to withstand crushing pressures, lightweight, proof against rodents, insects, and bacteria, equipped with a sealing device that can be closed and reopened easily, and able to withstand highly acid or alkaline soil or water. **The standard stainless steel container**, designed particularly for burial caching, comes in several sizes and is more satisfactory than anything improvised in the field — use it whenever possible. (Note: the manual's listed container dimensions are garbled in the extracted text and are not reliably readable — see the packaging reference.) **Field-expedient containers** must stay unpunctured and in shape under rough handling, and the lid seal is critical — a rubber gasket with a sharp metal rim pressed by a clamp or spring, or a threaded lid with heavy grease on the threads (never metallic solder). Suitable containers: instrument containers (half-gallon to ten-gallon), steel ammunition boxes with rubber-gasketed closures, and steel drums (full removable-head drums with lock-ring closures). → `references/packaging-and-containers.md`

## 5. The Emplacement Operation

**Burial.** A vertical hole is the norm; a horizontal hole (dug into a bank) is workable when level ground is unavailable. **Dimensions:** about **30 centimeters longer and wider** than the container, deep enough to cover it with about **45 centimeters** of soil — shallower risks exposure; deeper makes probing for recovery harder. **Shoring:** boards or bags of subsoil if the soil may cave in. **Equipment:** tape and compass, paper and pencil, a probe rod at least as long as the hole depth (about 1 centimeter in diameter, pushed in by hand), two ground sheets, sacks, pickax, hatchet, crowbar, and a lamp for night work. **The burial party** must be kept as small as possible, each member selected with utmost care and with adequate cover for absence, travel, and equipment. **Schedule:** a careful burial job probably takes longer than novices expect, so a dry run is advisable; night burial is probably necessary unless the site is exceptionally secluded. **Approach and security:** the immediate approach must be completely unobserved; return by a different route. Time at the site is the most critical period — maximum vigilance, at least one lookout constantly, minimal masked light. **Digging and refilling:** mark the final pinpoint and hole dimensions; probe the whole area to container-bottom depth; remove the sod in replaceable pieces; dig, keeping topsoil separate from subsoil; place the package (about 3 centimeters between packages on the same level); refill, tamping firmly; replace the sod. **Sterilizing the site:** dispose of excess soil far enough away (flushing into a stream is ideal); check all tools and equipment against a checklist; carry nothing except essentials; inspect for traces.

**Submersion.** Two steps: **weighting** the container to keep it from floating, and **mooring** to keep it in place — the moorings must also serve as a handle for pulling the cache to the surface on recovery. Weights ordinarily rest on the bottom as anchors; if the moorings are not accessible for recovery, another line must extend to a fixed, accessible object in the water or on shore. Four types of moorings, including **spider web** (cables radiating to anchors; the container must be buoyant so the cables can be secured by grappling; located by sightings to fixed landmarks; recovery by dragging the bottom or diving — the most difficult method) and **line-to-shore** (weighted container, a line to an immovable object along the shore). Essential data: depth, bottom type, currents, water clearness (paint the container to match the bottom in clear water; paint shiny fixtures dull), freezing dates, and salt water (much more corrosive; avoid tidal estuaries and lagoons except for temporary maritime resupply).

**Concealment.** There are so many ways of concealing objects in natural or ready-made hiding places that exact procedures cannot be generalized: hiding weapons and ammunition in a cave reduces emplacement to simply locating the site; sealing a packet of jewels in a brick wall needs a skilled mason, his tools, and mortar expertly mixed to match the original. Planning requires the greatest familiarity with local residents and their customs; final sterilization of the site is especially important, since a concealment site is usually open to frequent observation.

**Communications equipment.** As a general rule the whole kit for a particular operational purpose should be included in one container — except communications equipment, which is so sensitive that the kit should be divided among two or more containers: anyone acquiring a whole R/T set with the signal plan and cryptographic material would be able to play the set back. The signal plan and cryptographic material must never be placed in the same container; ideally the kit is distributed among three containers: (1) the R/T set, including the crystals; (2) the signal plan and operational supplies for the R/T operator (currency, barter items, small arms); (3) the cryptographic material. **Dispersion:** containers must be far enough apart that if one is discovered the others will not be detected nearby, yet close enough to be recovered in one operation — ordinarily at least **10 meters** apart. One final reference point is ordinarily used for a multiple cache. Avoid placing multiple caches in a repeated pattern. → `references/emplacement-recovery-and-report.md`

## 6. The Cache Report and Recovery

**The cache report.** The final, indispensable step in every emplacement operation is the preparation of a cache report recording the essential data for recovery — all the information someone unfamiliar with the locality would need to find the site, recover the cache, and return safely. The **Twelve-Point Cache Report** (Appendix A) points out the minimum essential data: (1) type of cache and functional purpose of the material; (2) method of caching; (3) contents — an itemized list of all materials in each container and how each is packaged; (4) description of containers, each numbered on the sketch; (5) general area — place names down to the nearest town or village; (6) immediate area — the IRP and instructions for proceeding to the FRP; (7)–(12) the remaining points as detailed in Appendix A. A careless error or omission may make it impossible to recover the cache when needed. Draft the report before emplacement, complete the final report as soon as possible after emplacement, and have the locating instructions checked promptly by someone who has not visited the site previously.

**Recovery.** Recovery procedure is generally similar to emplacement, with these points stressed in training: **practical exercises** — anyone expected to serve as a recovery agent should have experience actually recovering dummy caches. **Equipment** — generally the same as for emplacement, plus a probe rod (definitely necessary for recovery), some object roughly the same size as the cache container for filling the cavity left by removal, wrapping material to conceal recovered material, and grappling lines and hooks for a heavy submerged cache. **Sketch of the site** — provide sketches of the site and the route if possible; if the agent must rely exclusively on verbal instructions, he should draw a sketch before starting. **Preliminary reconnaissance** — check the locating instructions, especially under stringent enemy controls or when recovery must be quick; if recovery must be done at night, locate the cache by daylight and place an unnoticeable marker directly over it. **Probing** — use a probe rod before starting to dig; push it in by hand so it will not puncture the container; never pound it with a hammer. **Digging and refilling** — the same as for emplacement except: never use a pick (it might puncture the container), and bring fill material roughly the size of the container if none is available at the site. **Sterilizing the site** — leave no traces, as thoroughly as time permits.

## Output Contract

When answering a caching question from ST 31-205, give the method and the security rule first, then the numbers (30-pound one-man limit, 45-centimeter cover, 30-centimeter hole clearance, 10-meter multiple-cache dispersion, 50-meter projection limit, 10-meter azimuth limit), then the reporting requirement. Always name the reference points (IRP and FRP) and the pinpointing technique. State the manual's historic status where relevant.

## Operating Rules

1. **Security is overriding.** Every decision is judged against discovery and compromise.
2. **Suitability, not rules.** Choose the method most suitable for each cache's purpose, locality, and the changes enemy occupation would bring.
3. **The report is the cache.** A cache that cannot be found by its report is lost; draft the report before emplacement and verify the instructions with someone who has never visited the site.
4. **Sterilize the site.** Leave no trace of the operation — excess soil, tools, or personal items.
5. **Small parties, reliable people.** Everyone who emplaces knows the location; keep the party as small as possible.
6. **Packaging decides survival.** Inadequate packaging means unusable material when needed; test the seal by submersion.
