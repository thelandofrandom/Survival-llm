# FM 21-26 Map Reading and Land Navigation

## Purpose

FM 21-26, *Map Reading and Land Navigation* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 7 May 1993, 176 pages) is the Army's 1993 land navigation course — the complete system for finding your way with a map and compass, and without them. It is written in two parts (map reading, then land navigation) in 14 chapters and 10 appendices: training strategy, maps, marginal information and symbols, grids, scale and distance, direction, overlays, aerial photographs, navigation equipment and methods, elevation and relief, terrain association, mounted land navigation, navigation in different types of terrain, and unit sustainment — plus appendices on field sketching, map folding, units of measure and conversion, joint operations graphics, exportable training material, orienteering, the M2 compass, additional aids, foreign maps, and the Global Positioning System.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 21-26 is the 1993 predecessor of **FM 3-25.26, *Map Reading and Land Navigation* (July 2001)**, already a skill in this vault as `land-navigation`. FM 3-25.26 is the newer treatment and the one to follow where the two differ — it reorganized and updated the material, including the GPS appendix, which in 1993 was new technology. FM 21-26 remains valuable for its fuller treatment of marginal information, its aerial-photograph chapter, its orienteering appendix, and its field-sketching and map-folding appendices, which the 2001 manual condensed. Use FM 3-25.26 as the authority and FM 21-26 as the deep reference.

The manual's core idea, stated in Chapter 11: **the key to success in land navigation is to know your location at all times.** With that knowledge, you can move to any objective; without it, every step makes the problem worse. There are four steps to land navigation: **(1) know where you are, (2) plan the route, (3) stay on the route, (4) recognize the objective.**

## 1. Training Strategy
**1.1. Progressive skill levels.** A soldier completing initial entry training must be able to read a map, use a compass, and navigate on foot. After completing primary leadership training, he adds nonmilitary maps and more advanced navigation skills. A soldier completing the basic NCO course has demonstrated proficiency in map reading and land navigation at intermediate skill levels 2 and 3, with final analysis at skill level 4.
## 2. Maps

**2.1. Definition.** A map is a graphic representation of a portion of the earth's surface drawn to scale, as seen from above, with the features in their correct relative positions. To be understandable, features must be portrayed by symbols — and the portrayal of many features requires similar symbols to be used consistently, so the map reader must learn the symbol set before the mission.

**2.2. Types.** The map of choice for land navigators is the **topographic map** — it shows relief (contour lines), hydrography, vegetation, and man-made features. Other types include planimetric maps (no relief), photomaps (aerial photographs with grid and marginal information added), and specialized maps (city maps, road maps, aeronautical charts).

**2.3. Care and security.** Protect maps from the weather (waterproof case or bag), fold them properly (Appendix B), and keep them current — the edition date matters when determining how accurately the map represents the ground. If a map is no longer needed, turn it in.

**3.2. Colors.** Military maps use standard colors: **black** identifies man-made features (buildings, roads, boundaries); **brown** identifies relief features (contour lines) and elevation; **blue** identifies water (hydrography); **green** identifies vegetation with military significance; **red** classifies cultural features such as populated areas and major roads; **purple** identifies updates and changes added after the map was printed.

**3.3. Symbols.** Symbols are standardized so any map reader can read any map. The legend explains the symbols on that sheet; topographic symbols cover roads by class, railroads, bridges, power lines, water features, vegetation types, and built-up areas.
## 4. Grids

**4.1. Reference system.** A reference system lets a point be located exactly and communicated exactly. Geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) appear on all standard military maps; military grids are the working system for tactical use.

**4.2. Geographic coordinates.** Latitude is the distance of a point north or south of the equator, expressed in degrees, minutes, and seconds; longitude is the distance east or west of the prime meridian (Greenwich). A second set of rings around the globe at right angles to the equator forms the graticule. Geographic coordinates are expressed in angular measure — for example, latitude 32°15'00" and longitude 84°45'00".

**4.3. Military grids.** The world is divided into grid zones (UTM: 60 zones, each 6 degrees of longitude wide; UPS for the polar areas). The U.S. Army Military Grid Reference System (MGRS) works in three parts: **grid zone designation** (the zone number and letter, e.g., 16S); **100,000-meter square identification** (two letters, e.g., GL); **grid coordinates** (the easting and northing within that square, e.g., 01948253 — the numbers are read right, then up). Example from the manual: **16S GL 0194 8253**. Precision depends on the number of digits: a 4-digit coordinate locates a point within 1,000 meters; 6 digits within 100 meters; 8 digits within 10 meters; 10 digits within 1 meter.

**4.4. Using the coordinate scale.** With a coordinate scale (or the military protractor, GTA 5-2-12), place the scale on the map, read the easting and northing, and record the coordinate. **Recording and reporting grid coordinates:** always give the grid zone designation, the 100,000-meter square identification, and the coordinates — a coordinate without its zone and square is meaningless.

**4.5. Other grid systems.** British grids (used in a few areas of the world), and the World Geographic Reference System (GEOREF) — a worldwide system based on 15-degree quadrangles, each divided and numbered, used for reporting positions when MGRS is not available. → `references/maps-grids-scale.md`

## 5. Scale and Distance
**5.1. Scale.** The representative fraction (RF) expresses the ratio of map distance to ground distance — for example, 1:50,000 means one unit on the map equals 50,000 units on the ground. The ground distance between two points is determined by measuring the map distance and multiplying by the scale.

**5.2. Measuring distance.** Straight-line distance: use the bar scale, a ruler, or the edge of a piece of paper marked with tick marks. Curved-line distance (a road or stream): use a string or a strip of paper laid along the curve, then measure the string against the bar scale; or use dividers, stepping them along the curve.

**5.3. Ground measurement.** **Pace count:** the number of paces it takes to walk 100 meters on flat ground — each soldier must determine his own pace count (it changes with slope, load, and fatigue) and use it to measure distance on the ground. **Odometer:** distances can be measured by vehicle odometer, corrected for the map scale. **Estimation:** when the tactical situation does not allow measurement, estimate distance by eye — and check the estimate against the map at the next known point. A **time-distance scale** can be constructed for a route: mark the distance that can be covered in one hour at the expected rate of march. The normal rate for an 8-hour foot march is **4 kilometers per hour** — but the rate varies with terrain, load, and weather.
## 6. Direction
**6.1. Methods of expressing direction.** **Degree:** the most common unit — a circle divided into 360 degrees. **Mil:** another unit of measure — a circle divided into **6,400 mils** (used in artillery and for precise angular measurement). **Grad:** a metric unit — a circle divided into 400 grads.

**6.2. Base lines.** Three norths: **true north** (a line from any point on the earth's surface to the North Pole — the direction of the geographic pole); **magnetic north** (the direction the compass needle points — toward the magnetic pole, which moves); **grid north** (the direction of the vertical grid lines on the map). The three norths are never the same except by coincidence, and the differences between them are the declination problem.

**6.3. Azimuths.** An azimuth is the horizontal angle measured clockwise from a base line (north) to a line. **Grid azimuth** (measured from grid north), **magnetic azimuth** (from magnetic north), and **true azimuth** (from true north). **Back azimuth:** the opposite direction — to obtain a back azimuth from an azimuth, add 180° if the azimuth is less than 180°, or subtract 180° if it is more than 180°. The back azimuth of 180° may be stated as 0° or 360°. Field-expedient methods of determining direction are discussed in Chapter 9.

**6.4. Grid azimuths and the protractor.** The military protractor (GTA 5-2-12) contains the scales for measuring azimuths and grid coordinates. When using the protractor, the base line is aligned with the grid lines, the center is placed on the point, and the value is read from the scale. To obtain an accurate reading, use the protractor's edge and read to the nearest degree (or the nearest 10 mils on the mil scale).

**6.5. Declination diagram.** The declination diagram (in the lower margin) shows the angular relationship between true north, grid north, and magnetic north, plotted to scale — do not use the diagram to measure an angle. **The grid-magnetic (G-M) angle** is the angular size between grid north and magnetic north; its value is given in degrees, with mil equivalents to the nearest 10 mils. Remember: there are no negative azimuths — if a conversion gives a negative value, add 360°.

**6.6. Intersection, resection, and polar coordinates.** **Intersection:** determine an unknown point by taking azimuths to it from two known points and plotting the intersection on the map. **Resection:** determine your own unknown position by taking azimuths to two or more known points and plotting the back azimuths — where they intersect is your position. **Modified resection:** when only one known point is visible, take an azimuth to it, estimate the distance, and plot a single back azimuth with the estimated distance. **Polar coordinates:** your position is reported as a known point, an azimuth, and a distance (in meters) from that point.
## 7. Overlays
An overlay is a transparent sheet placed over a map, carrying information that is not on the map itself — unit positions, routes, boundaries, obstacles, and control measures. Types include operational overlays (the commander's plan drawn on the map) and others by purpose. Preparing an overlay: orient it over the map (align the grid lines), add marginal information (title, map sheet, scale, date-time group, issuing headquarters, classification), and draw the information with standard symbols.
## 8. Aerial Photographs

**8.1. Comparison with maps.** An aerial photograph has advantages over a map: it shows the ground as it actually is (recent changes, vegetation, camouflage), in photographic detail. It has disadvantages: position, location, and scale are only approximate; relief is hard to judge; the view varies from the very familiar (vertical) to the unfamiliar (oblique).

**8.2. Types.** Vertical (camera pointing straight down — the most useful for navigation); low oblique (camera tilted slightly — shows the horizon and gives a sense of relief); high oblique (camera tilted more — the horizon dominates); trimetrogon (three photographs — one vertical, two oblique — taken simultaneously); multiple lens photography; convergent photography (two cameras angled toward each other); panoramic (a wide sweep). Film types include black-and-white, color, infrared (black-and-white film that shows vegetation and water differently — healthy vegetation appears light, water dark), and camouflage detection film (a special film that reveals camouflage by its different infrared signature).

**8.3. Scale determination and indexing.** The scale of an aerial photograph is determined by comparing the measured distance between two points on the photograph with the known ground distance between the same two points (from a map or a measured baseline). Indexing: the four-corner method (plot the four corners of each photograph on the map) and the template method (used when a large number of photographs cover an area). **Orientation:** if a map of the same area is available, orient the photograph to the map; if no map is available, orient by shadows — in the Northern Hemisphere the sun moves from east through south to west, so shadows point north at noon.

**8.4. Identification of features and stereovision.** Identify features on aerial photographs by their shape, size, tone, texture, and pattern — a paved highway has a smooth texture and a regular shape; a railroad has a different pattern; a river has a meandering shape. Stereovision (viewing a pair of overlapping photographs, one with each eye) gives a three-dimensional view — the requirement for stereovision is two photographs of the same area taken from slightly different positions, viewed so each eye sees one photograph.
## 9. Navigation Equipment and Methods
**9.1. Types of compasses.** The lensatic compass is the standard Army compass; the M2 compass (Appendix G) is the artillery survey compass; the wrist/pocket compass is a small backup. The lensatic compass has a cover (protects the floating dial), a base (the body, containing the dial, the sighting wire, and the lens), a floating dial (the card with the azimuth scale), a sighting wire, and a lens for reading the dial. **Accuracy:** a compass in good working condition is accurate to within a few degrees. **Protection:** keep the compass away from metal objects and electrical fields, which deflect the needle; carry it in its case when not in use.

**9.2. Compass handling.** Hold the compass level and steady; read the azimuth from beneath the fixed black index line. **Compass-to-cheek technique:** fold the cover down, hold the compass at eye level with both hands, sight through the lens at the target, and read the azimuth. **Presetting a compass and following an azimuth:** set the desired azimuth on the compass, pick a steering mark (a distant object on the azimuth), walk to it, and repeat — this is dead reckoning on foot. **Bypassing an obstacle:** when an obstacle blocks the azimuth, go around it by a series of right-angle offsets (turn 90° for a counted number of paces, turn back to the azimuth, pass the obstacle, turn 90° the other way for the same number of paces, and return to the azimuth) — or use a deliberate offset: plan to miss the objective by a known amount to one side, so when you hit a linear feature (a road, a stream) you know which way to turn. **Offset:** a deliberate offset is a planned magnetic azimuth a few degrees off the true azimuth, so the navigator knows which direction to turn when he reaches the linear feature beyond the objective.

**9.3. Field-expedient methods of direction finding.** **Shadow-tip method:** place a stick in the ground, mark the tip of its shadow, wait 10 to 15 minutes, mark the new tip — the line between the marks runs roughly west to east (first mark is west). With the shadow-tip you can also build a shadow clock and estimate the time. **Watch method:** point the hour hand at the sun; in the Northern Hemisphere, the line halfway between the hour hand and 12 o'clock points south. **Star method:** in the Northern Hemisphere, Polaris is found by the two pointer stars of the Big Dipper; in the Southern Hemisphere, the Southern Cross gives south.

**9.4. GPS.** The Global Positioning System is a space-based, global, all-weather navigation system. Specific uses for the GPS are position location (grid coordinates), distance and direction, and navigation equipment and methods — GPS makes determining grid coordinates, distance, and direction faster and more accurate, and it can establish and verify land navigation courses.
## 10. Elevation and Relief
**10.1. Methods of showing relief.** **Layer tinting:** color bands for elevation ranges. **Form lines:** lines that suggest relief but are not measured — used where the survey is incomplete. **Shaded relief:** shading that suggests relief by simulating light and shadow. **Hachures:** short, broken lines that show the direction and steepness of a slope (the thicker and closer the hachures, the steeper the slope).

**10.2. Contour lines and contour intervals.** Contour lines join points of equal elevation. The **contour interval** — the vertical distance between adjacent contour lines — is printed in the margin (commonly 10 or 20 meters at 1:50,000).

**10.3. Slope.** Slope may be expressed as a percentage, in degrees, or as a gradient. To determine the percentage of slope: divide the vertical distance (rise) by the horizontal distance (run) and multiply by 100.

**10.4. Terrain features.** Major terrain features: hill (a point of high ground with a summit), ridge (a line of high ground with a crest), valley (low ground between hills or ridges, with a drainage), saddle (a dip between two hills on a ridge), depression (a low point with no outlet — shown by contour lines with tick marks pointing inward). Minor terrain features: draw (a shallow valley), spur (a short ridge jutting from a hill or ridge), cliff (a steep, near-vertical face).

**10.5. Terrain association and profiles.** **SOSES** is a recommended technique for identifying terrain features from contour lines (Shape, Orientation, Size, Elevation, Slope — the manual's mnemonic for reading the pattern). **Ridgelining** is a technique for visualizing the ground by tracing the ridges on the map.
## 11. Terrain Association

**11.1. Orienting the map.** Three methods: using a compass (align the map's north-south grid lines with the compass needle); using terrain association (match the features on the map to the features on the ground and rotate the map until they agree); using field-expedient methods (shadow-tip, watch, stars).

**11.5. Movement and route selection — the four steps.** (1) **Know where you are** (Step 1): know your position on the map and on the ground at all times and in every possible way. (2) **Plan the route** (Step 2): consider travel time, distance, terrain, cover and concealment, and the enemy — several factors determine a good route, and the route is rarely a straight line (a 1,000-meter route across a large ridge may take longer than a 1,500-meter route on flat terrain). (3) **Stay on the route** (Step 3): know your position relative to the route at all times — use checkpoints, steering marks, and pace count, and correct errors as soon as they are detected rather than walking farther into a mistake. (4) **Recognize the objective** (Step 4): know what the objective looks like from the direction of approach, and confirm it by terrain association before committing the unit.
## 12. Mounted Land Navigation

**12.1. Principles.** The navigator must assemble the equipment (map, compass, protractor, logbook), service it (check the compass, the vehicle, and the navigation aids), record data for precise locations (during stops, record the grid, azimuth, and time), supply data to subordinate leaders, and maintain liaison with the commander. **Movement:** consider vehicle capabilities — the terrain the vehicle can negotiate (swamps, thickly wooded areas, and steep slopes limit vehicles); look at the route selected and check it against the vehicle's limits.

**12.2. Terrain association navigation.** Consider ease of movement (use roads and trails where possible, but avoid routes that canalize the unit). Use terrain features as steering marks.

**12.3. Dead reckoning.** Without steering marks, dead reckoning is used: set the azimuth on the compass, pick a steering mark, drive to it, and repeat. The distance factor in dead reckoning is easy — use the odometer. **Common errors:** failure to determine the distance to be traveled, failure to orient the map, and failure to check the position at halts.
## 13. Navigation in Different Types of Terrain
**13.1. Desert regions.** The desert normally permits observation and fields of fire over long distances, but it has few features for terrain association — dunes, wadis, and rock outcrops are the references. Interpretation and analysis: many desert features look alike, and the map may be old or inaccurate.

**13.2. Mountain regions.** Climate is always a factor — because of the elevations, it is always colder and the weather changes faster. Interpretation and analysis: the heights of mountains and the steepness of slopes are the dominant features.

**13.3. Jungle regions.** Interpretation and analysis: the jungle has both wet and dry seasons; in the wet season, trees and vegetation are dense and visibility is short. Navigation: areas such as jungles are generally difficult — terrain association is limited by visibility, so dead reckoning, pace count, and the compass are the primary methods; streams are useful handrails.

**13.4. Arctic regions.** Interpretation and analysis: the arctic has long periods of darkness and light, and the terrain is often featureless snow and ice. Navigation: special skills may be required — the compass is unreliable near the magnetic pole, so the sun, stars, and GPS become more important; whiteout can erase all references.
## 14. Unit Sustainment
**14.1. Training guidance.** The unit commander must develop a sustainment program for land navigation — it should be developed and then conducted as a program, not as a single event. **Certification:** the unit commander must also certify his soldiers in land navigation tasks — a soldier is not qualified until he has demonstrated proficiency on a course. **Program development:** determine which instructors will conduct each module of training, and build the course. **Conduct of training:** conduct training at night as well as in daylight; the course needs to be at least **25 square kilometers** so soldiers cannot memorize the terrain.
## Appendices

- **Appendix A — Field Sketching.** Area sketches include sketches of positions, observation posts, and other tactical features; the appendix gives the procedures for drawing a field sketch that another soldier can use.
- **Appendix B — Map Folding Techniques.** Folding methods, protection methods (waterproofing and covering), and practice cuts — a properly folded map can be read and used in the rain without unfolding the whole sheet.
- **Appendix C — Units of Measure and Conversion Factors.** English and metric systems of linear measure, equivalent units of angular measure (degrees, mils, grads), conversion factors, and ground distance at map scale.
- **Appendix D — Joint Operations Graphics.** The basic contents of joint operations graphics (air and ground) — power transmission lines, airports and landing facilities, obstructions to air navigation, elevations and contours, and the graphics' use in joint operations.
- **Appendix E — Exportable Training Material.** How to train basic land navigation skills, the route planning guide, dismounted land navigation technique, and land navigation sustainment.
- **Appendix F — Orienteering.** The ideal map for an orienteering course; control points (locate them where they can be found, with a safety lane); start/finish area (assembly area, start, master map area, recorder's sheets, results board, clue description card, scoring); the cross-country or free event is scored by the number of controls found in the time allowed; civilian orienteering practices — pacing, thumbing (keeping the thumb on the map at the current position), handrails (linear features followed as a guide), attack points (permanent known features near a control, used as a jumping-off point). The scale of orienteering maps is **1:15,000**.
- **Appendix G — M2 Compass.** The magnetic needle, circular level, reading the azimuth scale, setting up the compass, measuring an azimuth — the M2 is the artillery survey compass, more precise than the lensatic.
- **Appendix H — Additional Aids.** Devices that assist the land navigator: the AN/PVS-5 night vision device (passive, image intensification — assists navigation at night, with a range of about 150 meters in starlight); the Position and Azimuth Determining System (PADS); the Enhanced Position Location Reporting System (EPLRS); and other systems that provide position location and navigation data.
- **Appendix I — Foreign Maps.** Hydrography, and the challenge of using a foreign map — the symbols, the language, and the datum may differ; the map has an international language of its own (standardized symbols) that allows it to be read despite the language difference.
- **Appendix J — Global Positioning System.** Grid coordinates (GPS makes determining them faster and more accurate), distance and direction, navigational equipment and methods — GPS can establish and verify land navigation courses, and it is the primary navigation aid when it is available and working.

## Operating Rules

1. **Know where you are, at all times.** The first step of land navigation is the one that prevents every other failure.
2. **Two independent checks, always.** A map, a compass, a pace count, the terrain, and the sky each give a position; when two agree, you know where you are.
3. **Read the margin before the map.** Declination, datum, edition, and contour interval decide whether the map is telling the truth.
4. **Correct errors immediately.** Walking farther into a mistake is the single most common navigation failure.
5. **The route is rarely a straight line.** A longer flat route beats a shorter route across a ridge.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** FM 3-25.26 (2001) supersedes FM 21-26 (1993) where they differ; FM 21-26 is the deep reference, not the authority.
