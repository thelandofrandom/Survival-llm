# Direction and Compass — FM 21-26

## Methods of Expressing Direction

- **Degree:** the most common unit of measure — a circle divided into 360 degrees.
- **Mil:** another unit of measure, the mil (abbreviated "mil") — a circle divided into **6,400 mils**. Used in artillery and for precise angular measurement.
- **Grad:** a metric unit of measure — a circle divided into 400 grads.

## Base Lines

Three norths, and they are never the same except by coincidence:
- **True north:** a line from any point on the earth's surface to the North Pole — the direction of the geographic pole.
- **Magnetic north:** the direction the compass needle points — toward the magnetic pole, which moves over time.
- **Grid north:** the direction of the vertical grid lines on the map. Grid north may be east or west of true north depending on the map's location within its grid zone.

## Azimuths

An azimuth is the horizontal angle measured clockwise from a base line (north) to a line. **Grid azimuth** is measured from grid north; **magnetic azimuth** from magnetic north; **true azimuth** from true north.

**Back azimuth:** the opposite direction — the azimuth of a line in the reverse direction. To obtain a back azimuth from an azimuth: if the azimuth is less than 180°, add 180°; if it is more than 180°, subtract 180°. The back azimuth of 180° may be stated as 0° or 360°. There are no negative azimuths — if a computation gives a negative value, add 360°.

**Field-expedient methods** of determining direction (several are discussed in Chapter 9): the shadow-tip method, the watch method, and the star method.

## Grid Azimuths and the Protractor

The military protractor, **GTA 5-2-12**, contains the scales for measuring azimuths and grid coordinates. When using the protractor, the base line is aligned with the grid lines, the center of the protractor is placed on the point, and the value of the angle is read from the scale — this is the grid azimuth. To obtain an accurate reading, use the protractor's edge, align it carefully with the grid lines, and read to the nearest degree (or the nearest 10 mils on the mil scale). A careless protractor reading is a common source of navigation error — check the reading by measuring the back azimuth as well.

## Declination Diagram

**Location:** the declination diagram is a part of the marginal information, located in the lower margin of the map. **Declination** is the angular difference between any two norths. The declination diagram shows the angular relationship between true north, grid north, and magnetic north, plotted to scale — **do not use the diagram to measure an angle**; use it only to determine the relationships and the conversion values.

**The grid-magnetic (G-M) angle:** the angular size that exists between grid north and magnetic north. Its value is given in degrees, with mil equivalents shown to the nearest 10 mils. The G-M angle is important to the map reader because a conversion is necessary to go from one type of azimuth to another — a grid azimuth used as a magnetic azimuth (or vice versa) without applying the G-M angle will put the navigator off course by the size of the angle.

**Grid convergence:** an arc indicated by a line on the declination diagram showing the angular difference between true north and grid north — the convergence of the meridians toward the pole.

**Conversion:** the declination conversion notes on the margin of the map give the rule for the sheet — whether to add or subtract the G-M angle when converting from magnetic azimuth to grid azimuth, and vice versa. A rule to remember when solving such problems: when converting from magnetic to grid, apply the G-M angle in the direction the diagram indicates; when converting from grid to magnetic, apply it in the opposite direction. There are no negative azimuths — add 360° if the result is negative. The declination diagram is used for these conversions; the G-M angle changes over time (the magnetic pole moves), so the date of the diagram matters — an old map's G-M angle may be wrong by several degrees.

**Applications:** remember, there are no negative azimuths, and the conversion must be applied every time a compass azimuth is plotted on a map or a map azimuth is followed with a compass. The most common navigation error is forgetting the declination conversion entirely.

## Intersection

When using the map and compass method, intersection determines an unknown point: take azimuths to the unknown point from two known points, convert them to grid azimuths, plot the back azimuths from the known points on the map, and the intersection of the two lines is the unknown point. The straightedge method is used when a compass is not available — align the straightedge on the map from the known point toward the unknown point and draw the line.

## Resection

Resection determines your own unknown position: take azimuths to two or more known points (features that can be identified on the map and on the ground), convert them to grid azimuths, convert to back azimuths, and plot them from the known points on the map — the intersection is your position. The manual's steps: orient the map; identify two or more known points; take magnetic azimuths to them; convert to grid azimuths; convert to back azimuths; plot and find the intersection. If the plotted lines form a small triangle rather than a single point, your position is inside the triangle — re-check the azimuths and the identification of the points.

## Modified Resection

When only one known point is visible: orient the map using a compass or by terrain association; find a distant point that can be identified on the map; determine the magnetic azimuth from your position to that point; convert the magnetic azimuth to a grid azimuth; convert the grid azimuth to a back azimuth; estimate the distance to the point (by pace, by map, or by known terrain); and plot the back azimuth and the estimated distance from the known point — the end of that line is your position. Modified resection is less accurate than resection, but it is better than no fix at all.

## Polar Coordinates

A position can be reported as polar coordinates: present known location on the map; azimuth (grid or magnetic) from that location to the point; distance (in meters) from that location to the point. The use of the laser range finder to determine the range makes polar coordinates fast and accurate — but the azimuth and the distance must both be correct, and the known point must be plotted exactly.

## Navigation Equipment — Compasses

**Types of compasses:** the lensatic compass (the standard Army compass); the M2 compass (Appendix G — the artillery survey compass, more precise); the wrist/pocket compass (a small backup — useful but less accurate).

**Lensatic compass:** the cover protects the floating dial; the base is the body of the compass and contains the floating dial, the sighting wire, and the lens; the floating dial carries the azimuth scale; the sighting wire is used to aim at a target; the lens magnifies the dial for reading. **Accuracy:** a compass in good working condition is accurate to within a few degrees. **Protection:** keep the compass away from metal objects and electrical fields, which deflect the needle; carry it in its case when not in use; if traveling with the compass, keep it closed and protected.

**Compass handling:** hold the compass level and steady; read the azimuth from beneath the fixed black index line. **Using the compass-to-cheek technique:** fold the cover down, hold the compass in both hands at eye level with arms extended, sight through the lens at the target, and read the azimuth through the lens. **Presetting a compass and following an azimuth:** set the desired azimuth on the compass, pick a steering mark (a distant object on the azimuth), walk to it, and repeat — this is dead reckoning on foot. **Bypassing an obstacle:** when an obstacle blocks the azimuth, go around it by a series of right-angle offsets — turn 90° for a counted number of paces, turn back to the azimuth, pass the obstacle, turn 90° the other way for the same number of paces, and return to the azimuth. **Offset:** a deliberate offset is a planned magnetic azimuth that is a few degrees off the true azimuth to the objective, so the navigator knows which direction to turn when he reaches the linear feature beyond the objective — if you deliberately aim to the right of the objective, then when you hit the road or stream beyond it, you know to turn left.

## Field-Expedient Methods of Direction Finding

**Shadow-tip method:** place a stick or branch into the ground, mark the tip of its shadow, wait 10 to 15 minutes until the shadow tip has moved, mark the new tip — the line between the marks runs roughly west to east (the first mark is west). With the shadow-tip you can also build a shadow clock and estimate the time. **Watch method:** point the hour hand at the sun; in the Northern Hemisphere, the line halfway between the hour hand and 12 o'clock points south (in the Southern Hemisphere, the line halfway between the hour hand and 12 points north). **Star method:** in the Northern Hemisphere, Polaris (the North Star) is found by the two pointer stars of the Big Dipper and gives north; in the Southern Hemisphere, the Southern Cross gives south. Moss does not reliably grow on the north side of trees — do not rely on folklore.

## Global Positioning System

The Global Positioning System is a space-based, global, all-weather navigation system. Specific uses for the GPS are position location (grid coordinates), distance and direction, and navigation equipment and methods — GPS makes determining grid coordinates, distance, and direction faster and more accurate, and it can establish and verify land navigation courses. But GPS is a tool, not a replacement for map and compass: it depends on satellites, batteries, and a clear sky, and it can be denied. Appendix J covers the system in detail, including its use for position location, for comparison of planned versus executed routes, and for establishing land navigation courses.
