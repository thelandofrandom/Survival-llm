# Maps, Grids, and Scale — FM 21-26

## Training Strategy

A soldier completing initial entry training must be able to read a map, use a compass, and navigate on foot. After completing primary leadership training, he adds the use of nonmilitary maps and more advanced navigation skills. A soldier completing the basic NCO course has demonstrated proficiency in map reading and land navigation at intermediate skill levels 2 and 3, with final analysis at skill level 4. Officers follow a similar progression. Exportable training material and instructor support/certification are provided so units can run their own courses.

## Maps

**Definition:** a map is a graphic representation of a portion of the earth's surface drawn to scale, as seen from above, with the features in their correct relative positions. To be understandable, features must be portrayed by symbols — and the portrayal of many features requires similar symbols to be used consistently, so the map reader must learn the symbol set before the mission.

**Types:** the map of choice for land navigators is the **topographic map** — it shows relief (contour lines), hydrography, vegetation, and man-made features. Other types include planimetric maps (no relief), photomaps (aerial photographs with grid and marginal information added), and specialized maps (city maps, road maps, aeronautical charts). Atlases are collections of maps of a region or the world. Field sketches are preliminary maps drawn in the field (Appendix A).

**Care and security:** protect maps from the weather (waterproof case or bag); fold them properly (Appendix B); keep them current — the edition date is important when determining how accurately the map represents the ground. If a map is no longer needed, it must be turned in. Maps of some areas of the world are subject to security restrictions; some maps may be classified and must be handled accordingly. Stock numbers are listed in map catalogs.

## Marginal Information

The border of a military map carries the data that makes it usable — 23 numbered items:
1. **Sheet name** — top center. 2. **Sheet number** — top right and bottom right. 3. **Series name** — the map series name. 4. **Scale** — upper and lower margins, as a representative fraction and as bar scales. 5. **Series number**. 6. **Edition number** — with the date; important when determining how accurately the map represents the ground. 7. **Index to boundaries**. 8. **Adjoining sheets** — a diagram of neighboring sheets; on maps at 1:100,000 and larger, the adjoining sheets are named. 9. **Elevation guide** — normally found in the lower margin; shows the elevations of the area. 10. **Declination diagram** — located in the lower margin; shows true north, grid north, and magnetic north. 11. **Bar scales** — located in the lower margin. 12. **Contour interval note** — the vertical distance between contour lines. 13. **Spheroid note** — the reference ellipsoid the map is based on. 14. **Grid note** — the grid system used. 15. **Projection note** — the projection system (the manual notes the characteristics of conformal-type projection systems). 16. **Vertical datum note** — the vertical reference (for example, mean sea level) taken as a surface of reference for elevations. 17. **Horizontal datum note** — the horizontal reference for positions. 18. **Control note** — the survey control used. 19. **Preparation note** — how the map was prepared. 20. **Printing note** — printing information. 21. **Grid reference box** — normally in the lower margin; identifies the grid zone designation and the 100,000-meter square identification for the sheet. 22. **Unit imprint and symbol** — the unit that prepared the map. 23. **Legend** — located in the lower margin; explains the symbols used on the sheet.

Also: **special notes** (24) — any note that does not fit the standard items; **user's note** (25) — normally located in the lower margin; **stock number identification** (26) — all maps have a stock number; **conversion graph** (27) — normally found in the lower margin, for converting between units; **glossary** — an explanation of technical terms; **classification** — certain maps require a classification note; **protractor scale** — may appear in the margin; **coverage diagram** — on maps at scales of 1:100,000 and larger, shows the coverage of the sheet.

## Colors and Symbols

**Colors used on a military map:** black identifies man-made features (buildings, roads, boundaries); brown identifies all relief features and elevation (contour lines); blue identifies water (hydrography); green identifies vegetation with military significance; red classifies cultural features such as populated areas and major roads; purple identifies updates and changes added after the map was printed.

**Symbols:** symbols are standardized so any map reader can read any map. The legend explains the symbols on that sheet. Ideally, all features within an area would be shown; in practice, only the features that can be shown at the map's scale are portrayed, and the map reader must know which features are omitted. The center of a symbol remains in its true location — the symbol is drawn around the point, not instead of it. The manual's symbol tables (Figures 3-1 through 3-31 in the original) describe topographic features and their symbols in detail.

## Grids

**Reference system:** a reference system lets a point be located exactly and communicated exactly. Geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) appear on all standard military maps; military grids are the working system for tactical use.

**Geographic coordinates:** latitude is the distance of a point north or south of the equator; longitude is the distance east or west of the prime meridian. A second set of rings around the globe at right angles to the equator forms the graticule. Geographic coordinates are expressed in angular measure — degrees, minutes, and seconds. Example from the manual: latitude 32°15'00" and longitude 84°45'00" (Columbus, Georgia area). To determine the geographic coordinates of a point: find the nearest graticule lines, measure the difference with a scale, and add it to the line value — for example, add 2'47" to 32°20'00" to get 32°22'47". If you do not have a scale or ruler, use the proportional method the manual describes (measure with a strip of paper marked in equal parts). Maps made by some nations do not have the same graticule — check the datum and the grid note. Examples in the manual include Rome, Italy at 12°27'08"E.

**Military grids:** the world is divided into grid zones — UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator): 60 zones, each 6 degrees of longitude wide; UPS (Universal Polar Stereographic) for the polar areas. **The U.S. Army Military Grid Reference System (MGRS)** works in three parts: grid zone designation (the zone number and letter, e.g., 16S); 100,000-meter square identification (two letters, e.g., GL); grid coordinates (the easting and northing within that square). Example from the manual: **16S GL 0194 8253**. Precision depends on the number of digits: 4 digits locates a point within 1,000 meters; 6 digits within 100 meters; 8 digits within 10 meters; 10 digits within 1 meter.

**Using the coordinate scale:** with a coordinate scale (or the military protractor, GTA 5-2-12), place the scale on the map, read the easting (right) and northing (up), and record the coordinate. **Recording and reporting grid coordinates:** always give the grid zone designation, the 100,000-meter square identification, and the coordinates — a coordinate without its zone and square is meaningless. **Grid reference box:** the left portion identifies the grid zone designation; the right portion gives the 100,000-meter square identification and an example of how to read coordinates from the sheet.

**Other grid systems:** British grids (used in a few areas of the world); the World Geographic Reference System (GEOREF) — a worldwide system based on 15-degree quadrangles, each divided and numbered 0 to 59 from south to north, used for reporting positions when MGRS is not available. The authorized numerical code provides a capability for reporting positions by a code that is not a grid reference — use of unauthorized codes is, by regulation, unauthorized.

## Scale and Distance

**Scale:** the representative fraction (RF) expresses the ratio of map distance to ground distance — for example, 1:50,000 means one unit on the map equals 50,000 units on the ground. The ground distance between two points is determined by measuring the map distance and multiplying by the scale (RF = map distance / ground distance). Both the map distance and the ground distance must be in the same unit of measure.

**Measuring distance:** straight-line distance — use the bar scale, a ruler, or the edge of a piece of paper marked with tick marks. To measure, place the paper edge along the route, make tick marks at the start and end, then measure the tick marks against the bar scale. Curved-line distance (a road or stream) — use a string or a strip of paper laid along the curve, then measure the string against the bar scale; or use dividers, stepping them along the curve. When you have completed measuring, keep both tick marks on the paper so the measurement can be checked. When the distance you measure is longer than the bar scale, measure it in parts and add the parts.

**Ground measurement:** **pace count** — another way to measure ground distance; each soldier must determine his own pace count (the number of paces to walk 100 meters on flat ground) — it changes with slope, load, and fatigue. **Odometer** — distances can be measured by vehicle odometer, corrected for the map scale. **Estimation** — at times, because of the tactical situation, distance must be estimated by eye; check the estimate against the map at the next known point. **Time-distance scale:** to construct one, determine the distance that can be covered in one hour at the expected rate of march, mark that distance on the scale, and label it — the scale then reads time to any point on the route. The normal rate for an 8-hour foot march is **4 kilometers per hour**, but the rate varies with terrain, load, and weather.
