# Elevation, Relief, and Terrain Association — FM 21-26

## Methods of Showing Relief

- **Layer tinting:** a method of showing relief by color bands for elevation ranges — each band is a different color, and the bands step up with elevation.
- **Form lines:** lines that suggest relief but are not measured — used where the survey is incomplete or the terrain is too complex for contouring.
- **Shaded relief:** relief shading indicates relief by simulating light and shadow — the side of a hill facing the light is bright, the other side is shaded.
- **Hachures:** short, broken lines that show the direction and steepness of a slope — the thicker and closer the hachures, the steeper the slope; hachures point downhill.

## Contour Lines and Contour Intervals

Contour lines join points of equal elevation. The **contour interval** — the vertical distance between adjacent contour lines — is printed in the margin (commonly 10 or 20 meters at 1:50,000). Closely spaced lines mean steep ground; widely spaced lines mean gentle ground; a V shape pointing uphill marks a valley or drainage; a V pointing downhill marks a ridge or spur. Index contours (every fifth line, drawn thicker) carry the elevation numbers.

**Determining elevation:** determine the contour interval and the unit of measure; find the numbered index contour line nearest the point; count the contour lines between it and the point and multiply by the contour interval; determine if you are going from lower to higher ground or higher to lower, and add or subtract accordingly. To determine the exact elevation of a point on a contour line, read the line's elevation (or interpolate between the index contour and the next line). To determine the elevation of a hilltop, use the highest contour line plus a fraction of the interval (the hilltop is higher than the highest line but lower than the next line would be). To estimate the elevation of the bottom of a depression, use the lowest contour line minus a fraction of the interval. Bench marks and spot elevations give exact elevations at specific points — use them to check your contour reading.

## Slope

Slope may be expressed in several ways, but all express the same relationship between vertical distance and horizontal distance:
- **Percentage of slope:** divide the vertical distance (rise) by the horizontal distance (run) and multiply by 100. For example, a rise of 20 meters over a run of 200 meters is a 10 percent slope.
- **Slope angle in degrees:** the arctangent of rise over run.
- **Gradient:** expressed as a ratio (for example, 1:10 — one unit of rise for ten units of run).

To determine the percentage of slope, first determine the horizontal distance (from the map scale) and the vertical distance (from the contour lines), then apply the formula. The slope angle can also be expressed in degrees or as a gradient — the manual gives the conversions.

## Terrain Features

**Major terrain features:**
- **Hill:** a point of high ground with a summit; contour lines form closed loops around it.
- **Ridge:** a line of high ground with a crest; contour lines form U shapes pointing downhill.
- **Valley:** low ground between hills or ridges, with a drainage; contour lines form U or V shapes pointing uphill.
- **Saddle:** a dip between two hills on a ridge; contour lines form an hourglass shape.
- **Depression:** a low point with no outlet; contour lines form closed loops with tick marks pointing inward (downhill).

**Minor terrain features:**
- **Draw:** a shallow valley, smaller than a valley; contour lines form a shallow U pointing uphill.
- **Spur:** a short ridge jutting from a hill or ridge; contour lines form a U pointing downhill.
- **Cliff:** a steep, near-vertical face; contour lines are very close together or merge into a single line.

**Supplementary terrain features:**
- **Cut:** a man-made feature resulting from excavating through high ground (a road cut, a railroad cut).
- **Fill:** a man-made feature resulting from building an embankment across low ground (a road fill, a dam).

## Interpreting Terrain from Contour Lines

**Contour lines:** emphasizing the main contour lines is a technique used to interpret the terrain of an area — by studying these lines, you get a better understanding of the layout of the terrain and can decide on the best route. The manual's worked example (Figure 10-27) describes a complex landmass: a ridgeline running east to west; four prominent ridges (one on each end of the ridgeline and two extending south); a valley to the south sloping downward; a depression just east of the valley (higher ground in all directions from its bottom); several spurs extending generally south from the ridgeline (lower ground in three directions, higher in one; their contour-line U's point away from higher ground); draws between the ridges and spurs.

**SOSES:** a recommended technique for identifying specific terrain features and then locating them on the map is to make use of five of their characteristics, known by the mnemonic **SOSES** — **S**hape (the general form or outline of the feature at its base), **O**rientation (the general trend or direction of a feature from your viewpoint), **S**ize (the feature's dimensions), **E**levation (the feature's height above sea level and relative to its surroundings), and **S**lope (the steepness of the feature's sides). Terrain features can be examined, described, and compared with each other and with corresponding map contour patterns in terms of their shapes, orientations, sizes, elevations, and slopes. Through practice, you can learn to identify several individual terrain features in the field and see how they vary in appearance.

**Ridgelining:** a technique that helps you visualize the ground by tracing the ridges on the map — follow the ridge lines and the spurs that branch from them, and the terrain's skeleton becomes clear.

**Profiles:** a profile can be used for many purposes — to determine visibility, fields of fire, and the steepness of a route. A profile can be constructed from any contoured map by plotting elevations along a line. When time is short, or when a complete profile is not needed, a hasty profile is constructed in the same manner but with fewer plotted points.

## Terrain Association

**Orienting the map:** three methods — using a compass (align the map's north-south grid lines with the compass needle); using terrain association (match the features on the map to the features on the ground and rotate the map until they agree); using field-expedient methods (shadow-tip, watch, stars).

**Locations:** **know position** — the most important thing is to know your position at all times, on the map and on the ground, relative to your directional orientation, the direction and distance to your objective, other landmarks, impassable terrain, the enemy, danger areas, and the advantages and disadvantages of the terrain between you and your objective. **Known point / known distance (polar plot):** from a known point, take an azimuth and a distance to your position and plot it. **Resection:** see Chapter 6. **Intersection:** see Chapter 6.

**Terrain association techniques:** match the terrain to the map by features — compare what you see with what the map shows, and resolve differences before moving. Compare the vegetation depicted on the map with the vegetation on the ground. Be aware of masking by vegetation — important features can be hidden by trees and brush. Use hydrography — streams, rivers, and lakes are reliable reference features. Use man-made features — roads, buildings, and power lines are reliable when they are on the map. Examine the same piece of terrain during different conditions — a feature looks different in fog, rain, or darkness than in clear daylight.

**METT-T and movement:** tactical factors other than the terrain must be considered in route selection — **mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops, and time available**. Key terrain is any area whose control affords a marked advantage (high ground, bridges, towns, road junctions). Obstacles stop, slow, or divert movement. Cover is protection from fire; concealment is protection from observation — most features that offer cover also provide concealment from ground observation. Danger areas are open fields, roads, and streams with no concealment. Avenues of approach are routes by which a unit may reach an objective or key terrain — a route must provide enough width for the force using it. Weather has little impact on dismounted land navigation (rain and snow may slow the rate of march), but during mounted land navigation the navigator must know the effect of weather on his vehicle. Troops: the size and type of the unit, its capabilities, physical condition, training, and equipment all affect route selection — on ideal terrain (relatively level ground with little or no woods), a platoon can defend a front of up to 400 meters; heavily wooded or very hilly areas reduce that front.

**Movement and route selection — the four steps:** (1) **Know where you are** (Step 1): know your position on the map and on the ground at all times and in every possible way. (2) **Plan the route** (Step 2): consider travel time, distance, terrain, cover and concealment, and the enemy — the route is rarely a straight line (a 1,000-meter route across a large ridge may take longer than a 1,500-meter route on flat terrain). (3) **Stay on the route** (Step 3): know your position relative to the route at all times — use checkpoints, steering marks, and pace count, and correct errors as soon as they are detected. (4) **Recognize the objective** (Step 4): know what the objective looks like from the direction of approach, and confirm it by terrain association before committing the unit.
