# Mounted Navigation, Terrain Types, and Sustainment — FM 21-26

## Overlays

An overlay is a transparent sheet placed over a map, carrying information that is not on the map itself — unit positions, routes, boundaries, obstacles, and control measures. Types include operational overlays (the commander's plan drawn on the map) and others by purpose.

**Preparing an overlay:** orient it over the map (align the grid lines); add marginal information (title, map sheet, scale, date-time group, issuing headquarters, classification); draw the information with standard symbols. **Recording marginal information:** when all the information is recorded, the overlay can be used without the map it was drawn on — a good overlay is self-contained. **Orienting the overlay:** the overlay must be oriented correctly over the map — an overlay that is not oriented correctly is worse than no overlay at all. A title such as "Road Reconnaissance" is not as specific as a title that names the route, the unit, and the date-time group.

## Aerial Photographs

**Comparison with maps:** an aerial photograph has advantages over a map — it shows the ground as it actually is (recent changes, vegetation, camouflage) in photographic detail. It has disadvantages: position, location, and scale are only approximate; relief is hard to judge; the view varies from the very familiar (vertical) to the unfamiliar (oblique).

**Types:** vertical (camera pointing straight down — the most useful for navigation); low oblique (camera tilted slightly — shows the horizon and gives a sense of relief); high oblique (camera tilted more — the horizon dominates); trimetrogon (three photographs — one vertical, two oblique — taken simultaneously); multiple lens photography; convergent photography (two cameras angled toward each other across the direction of flight, permitting a panoramic view); panoramic (a wide sweep, developed for increasing coverage).

**Film types:** black-and-white, color, infrared (a black-and-white film that shows vegetation and water differently — healthy vegetation appears light, water dark), and camouflage detection film (a special film that reveals camouflage by its different infrared signature). Standard titling data for aerial photography includes the lens type and serial number and other items that identify the photograph.

**Scale determination:** the scale of an aerial photograph is determined by comparing the measured distance between two points on the photograph with the known ground distance between the same two points (from a map or a measured baseline).

**Indexing:** the four-corner method (plot the four corners of each photograph on the map) and the template method (used when a large number of photographs cover an area). After all photos have been plotted, write the identification on each.

**Orientation:** if a map of the same area as the photograph is available, orient the photograph to the map. If no map is available, orient by shadows — in the Northern Hemisphere the sun moves from east through south to west, so shadows point north at noon; within three hours of noon, a reasonably accurate north can be determined from the direction of shadow fall (shadows vary from north at noon through west in the afternoon). On a photograph that can be oriented to the map, a grid can be added.

**Identification of features:** identify features on aerial photographs by their shape, size, tone, texture, and pattern — a paved highway has a smooth texture and a regular shape; a railroad has a different pattern; a river has a meandering shape; buildings, bridges, canals, and power lines each have their own signature.

**Stereovision:** viewing a pair of overlapping photographs, one with each eye, gives a three-dimensional view. The requirement for stereovision is two photographs of the same area taken from slightly different positions, viewed so each eye sees one photograph. A stereoscope orients the pair for viewing. A method to orient a pair of aerial photographs for stereovision is given in the manual.

## Mounted Land Navigation

**Principles:** the navigator must assemble the equipment (map, compass, protractor, logbook); service it (check the compass, the vehicle, and the navigation aids); record data for precise locations (during stops, record the grid, azimuth, and time); supply data to subordinate leaders; and maintain liaison with the commander.

**Movement:** consider vehicle capabilities — when determining the route, consider the terrain the vehicle can negotiate (swamps, thickly wooded areas, and steep slopes limit vehicles); look at the route you have selected and check it against the vehicle's limits (if there is a contour line the vehicle cannot climb, find another route).

**Terrain association navigation:** consider ease of movement (use roads and trails where possible, but avoid routes that canalize the unit); use terrain features as steering marks; follow terrain features (ridges, valleys, streams) as handrails; determine directions by breaking the route into legs, each with an azimuth (usually an azimuth in degrees — there is no accurate way to follow a mil azimuth from a moving vehicle); determine distance by measuring the route on the map and converting to ground distance; make notes — mental notes are usually enough for short moves, but a written logbook is better for long or complex routes (the notes list easily identifiable terrain features at or near each turn); plan to avoid errors — restudy the route, check the map, and confirm the vehicle's position at every halt; use a logbook to record the route, the times, and the positions.

**Dead reckoning:** without steering marks, dead reckoning is used — set the azimuth on the compass, pick a steering mark, drive to it, and repeat. On arrival at the steering mark, or on any change of direction, the navigator re-checks the azimuth and picks the next mark. The distance factor in dead reckoning is easy — use the odometer. Terrain association is fast, is error-tolerant, and works well when features are visible; dead reckoning is very accurate if done carefully (but errors accumulate); frequently combine both. **Common errors:** failure to determine the distance to be traveled, failure to orient the map, and failure to check the position at halts.

## Navigation in Different Types of Terrain

**Desert regions:** the desert normally permits observation and fields of fire over long distances, but it has few features for terrain association — dunes, wadis, and rock outcrops are the references. Interpretation and analysis: many desert features look alike, and the map may be old or inaccurate. Navigation: use dead reckoning and the compass, with the sun and stars as checks; travel at night when possible. The manual notes the desert regions of Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and Iraq as examples.

**Mountain regions:** climate is always a factor — because of the elevations, it is always colder and the weather changes faster. Interpretation and analysis: the heights of mountains and the steepness of slopes are the dominant features. Navigation: existing roads and trails offer the best routes; off-trail movement is slow and must be planned with the time-distance formulas. The four steps and two techniques for navigation (terrain association and dead reckoning) apply in the mountains as elsewhere.

**Jungle regions:** interpretation and analysis — the jungle has both wet and dry seasons; in the wet season, trees and vegetation are dense and visibility is short. Navigation: areas such as jungles are generally difficult — terrain association is limited by visibility, so dead reckoning, pace count, and the compass are the primary methods; streams are useful handrails. The four steps and two techniques for navigation apply, with extra attention to security (larger forces are easier to detect in the jungle).

**Arctic regions:** interpretation and analysis — the arctic has long periods of darkness and light, and the terrain is often featureless snow and ice. Navigation: special skills may be required — the compass is unreliable near the magnetic pole, so the sun, stars, and GPS become more important; whiteout can erase all references. The manual notes that the four steps and two techniques for land navigation apply in the arctic as elsewhere, but the navigator must demonstrate his skill to prevail — the environment punishes the unprepared.

## Unit Sustainment

**Training guidance:** the unit commander must develop a sustainment program for land navigation — it should be developed and then conducted as a program, not as a single event. **Certification:** the unit commander must also certify his soldiers in land navigation tasks — a soldier is not qualified until he has demonstrated proficiency. **Program development:** determine which instructors will conduct each module of training, and build the course. **Conduct of training:** conduct training at night as well as in daylight; the course needs to be at least **25 square kilometers** so soldiers cannot memorize the terrain. Perform a map and ground reconnaissance of the course before using it. Determine the standards — the unit commander sets the standards for each task (time, accuracy, and the conditions under which the task is performed). The sustainment program should be developed and then conducted as a program, with assigned land navigation tasks and a schedule.

## Appendices

**Appendix A — Field Sketching.** Area sketches include sketches of positions, observation posts, and other tactical features; the appendix gives the procedures for drawing a field sketch that another soldier can use — the sketch must show the scale, the orientation, and the features that matter.

**Appendix B — Map Folding Techniques.** Folding methods (how to fold a map so the area of operations is on top and the map can be read without unfolding the whole sheet), protection methods (waterproofing and covering), and practice cuts — a properly folded map can be read and used in the rain.

**Appendix C — Units of Measure and Conversion Factors.** English system of linear measure, metric system of linear measure, equivalent units of angular measure (degrees, mils, grads), conversion factors, and ground distance at map scale.

**Appendix D — Joint Operations Graphics.** The basic contents of joint operations graphics (air and ground): power transmission lines are symbolized as a series of marks; airports, landing facilities, and related air information; the top of each obstruction to air navigation is marked with its elevation; along the north and east edges of the graphic, elevations and contours are located. The highest terrain features are shown with large open-faced figures in the legend. Runway lengths and landing surfaces are given.

**Appendix E — Exportable Training Material.** How to train basic land navigation skills, the route planning guide, dismounted land navigation technique, and land navigation sustainment — the material a unit needs to run its own course.

**Appendix F — Orienteering.** The ideal map for an orienteering course is a topographic map at large scale. Control points: locate the controls where they can be found, with a safety lane (a linear location where competitors who have not returned by the end of the event must report). Start/finish area: assembly area (where orienteers register), start (where the orienteer reports to the start official), master map area (three to five master maps from which competitors copy the course), recorder's sheets, results board (displays the orienteer's position), clue description card (prepared with the control descriptions), scoring (the cross-country or free event is scored by the number of controls found in the time allowed). Points of the compass are denoted by capital letters on the clue card. Control points within 100 meters of each other must be distinguished by their descriptions; for large features (up to 75 meters across), the control is placed at the center. Number control points in red on the master map, with a red line indicating the course's shape. The map symbols in the appendix are typical topographic symbols. **Civilian orienteering:** pacing (one of the basic skills — the orienteer's pace count); thumbing (keeping the thumb on the map at the current position — very simple but effective); handrails (linear features followed as a guide); attack points (permanent known features near a control, used as a jumping-off point). The scale of orienteering maps is **1:15,000**. An expert military map reader/land navigator is by no means automatically an expert orienteer — orienteering is a sport with its own rules and practices.

**Appendix G — M2 Compass.** The magnetic needle, circular level, reading the azimuth scale, setting up the compass, measuring an azimuth — the M2 is the artillery survey compass, more precise than the lensatic; the compass is set up in both hands at eye level with arms extended.

**Appendix H — Additional Aids.** The AN/PVS-5 is a passive night-vision device that uses image intensification — it can assist the land navigator in moonlight and starlight (about 150 meters in starlight) with identification, navigation, position location, and automatic gain control. The Enhanced Position Location Reporting System (EPLRS) is a system that allows units to navigate and report positions; the network control station is located at a central point, and the system will be fielded to infantry, armor, field artillery, and combat support units. The Position and Azimuth Determining System (PADS) can be used by the land navigator to determine position and azimuth; the two-man PADS survey party uses a high-accuracy system. The QRMP system has a map size of 24-by-30-inch paper and provides digital topographic support.

**Appendix I — Foreign Maps.** Hydrography, and the challenge of using a foreign map — the symbols, the language, and the datum may differ. In dealing with the challenge, remember that the map has an international language of its own (standardized symbols) that allows it to be read despite the language difference.

**Appendix J — Global Positioning System.** Grid coordinates (GPS makes determining them faster and more accurate), distance and direction, navigational equipment and methods — GPS can establish and verify land navigation courses, and it is the primary navigation aid when it is available and working. The system assists the user in performing position location, navigation, and timing; it can be operated in all weather, day or night.
