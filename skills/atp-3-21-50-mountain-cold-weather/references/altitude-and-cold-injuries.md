# Altitude and Cold Injuries — ATP 3-21.50

Source: ATP 3-21.50, Chapter 6 (Augmenting Combat Power), paragraphs 6-128 through 6-149. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Acclimatization Schedule

- There are **no shortcuts**. Any attempt to trim or bypass acclimatization usually results in injuries.
- Most soldiers can operate up to **2,400 m (8,000 ft)** with minimal effects.
- **2,400–4,200 m (8,000–13,800 ft):** about **1 to 2 weeks** on average.
- **Above 4,200 m:** add **another 2 weeks**.
- **Above 5,200 m (17,000 ft):** acclimatization is generally not possible for many personnel.
- Soldiers continue to train during acclimatization (crevasse crossing, foot marches, weapons firing, rock climbing).
- Stateside acclimatization is possible, but de-acclimatization is likely if transport to theater exceeds a few days — troops must acclimate in theater.
- Above 3,000 m (10,000 ft), vision and judgment are affected.

## Altitude Bands

| Band | Elevation | Effects and rules |
|---|---|---|
| Low | 0–1,000 m (0–3,300 ft) | No effect |
| Moderate | 1,000–2,400 m (3,300–8,000 ft) | Little to no effect; effects usually start around 2,400 m |
| High | 2,400–4,200 m (8,000–14,000 ft) | Slower movement/performance; judgment and cognitive skills may be affected; AMS; gain ≤300 m (1,000 ft) per day above 3,000 m; mandatory immediate evacuation for HAPE/HACE at 3,700 m (12,000 ft)+; 50–80% ineffective at 4,200 m |
| Very high | 4,200–6,000 m (14,000–19,800 ft) | Mandatory acclimatization (1–2 days for every 1,000 ft above 10,000 ft); HAPE/HACE increase in severity and incidence; limited time at elevation; highly experienced and trained personnel only; specialized equipment |
| Extremely high | Above 6,000 m (19,800 ft) | Weeks of acclimatization; no air assets above 5,500 m (18,000 ft); highly experienced and trained personnel only; specialized equipment |

## Altitude Illness

### Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)
- Symptoms: headache, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, fatigue, sleep disturbances.
- Incidence over **20% above 2,400 m (8,000 ft)**, rising with altitude.
- AMS itself is not fatal — but ascending through it invites HAPE or HACE.

### High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)
- Symptoms: severe shortness of breath, frothing at the mouth, cyanosis (blue skin).
- Life-threatening.

### High-Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)
- Symptoms: severe headache, stumbling, confusion, incoherent speech.
- Life-threatening.

### Treatment
- **The most effective treatment for all altitude sickness is descent and rest.**
- Well-acclimatized soldiers are less susceptible.
- Evacuate any soldier who is coughing and stumbling immediately to medical support at lower altitude.

## Cold Injuries

- **The greatest threat at high altitude.** Frostbite and snow blindness are the common injuries.
- **Four essentials for survival in cold weather:** warmth, food, water, shelter.
- Exertion causes sweating; sweat in very cold temperatures can freeze and cause frostbite.
- Prevention: layering discipline, shelter construction training, buddy system (check faces, ears, fingers, toes), regular warming rotations with warm non-caffeinated liquids.
- **Altitude sickness and cold injuries can occur simultaneously** and their signs can be confused — evacuate a coughing, stumbling soldier to lower altitude and let medical support sort it out.
- **Time rule:** add one-third of the normal time to any task in extreme cold weather.
- Heat injuries also occur in mountains: sunburn is easier at altitude, and strenuous climbing in summer sun can cause dehydration, heat exhaustion, or heat stroke.

## MEDEVAC vs. CASEVAC

- **MEDEVAC:** movement on dedicated, properly marked medical platforms with en route care by medical personnel.
- **CASEVAC:** movement aboard nonmedical vehicles or aircraft without en route care.
- Plan and rehearse both. Key planning points:
  - Organic medical personnel accompany each march element.
  - Coordinate air and ground MEDEVAC support; rehearse with air ambulance and ground ambulance crews.
  - Place ground ambulances strategically in direct support of each march element.
  - Identify ambulance exchange points along march routes.
  - Carry compact, lightweight casualty transport systems, VS-17 panels or other marking equipment (including night marking devices), and mobility equipment such as rope and carabiners.
- In mountains, evacuation is slow — plan for prolonged field care.

## Leader Actions in Extreme Temperatures

- Soldiers can't remember things: be patient, review orders and drills, keep minds busy with questions and challenges.
- Soldiers withdraw: pair them with acclimatized buddies, keep them talking, circulate among the troops.
- Soldiers grow irritable: stay patient, keep a sense of humor, vary duties.
- Soldiers shirk tasks: remind them the job is to fight and keep weapons in fighting order — cold makes tasks take longer, not impossible.
- Rotate soldiers into warming tents at frequent intervals; ensure hydration in cold and hot temperatures alike.
