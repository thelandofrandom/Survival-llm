# Movement Rates and Load — ATP 3-21.50

Source: ATP 3-21.50, Chapter 5 (Tactical Enabling Operations and Activities). Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Dismounted Movement Rates (Table 5-2)

Normal planning rate on hard-packed, gently rolling terrain: **4 kph**. In snow or hills it drops sharply.

| Movement mode | Unbroken trail | Broken trail |
|---|---|---|
| On foot, no snow cover | 2–3 kph cross-country | 3–4 kph trail walking |
| On foot, <1 ft of snow | 1.5–3 kph | 2–3 kph |
| On foot, >1 ft of snow | 0.5–1 kph | 2–3 kph |
| Snowshoe | 1.5–3 kph | 3–4 kph |
| Skiing | 1.5–5 kph | 5–6 kph |
| Skijoring | n/a | 8–24 kph (15 kph max recommended for safety) |

- **Unbroken trail** = undisturbed snow: slow, physically intense. **Broken trail** = packed or cleared snow: solid, easier.
- Trail breaking is time-intensive and exhausting — rotate multiple fire teams or squads on the lead.
- On foot with less than 1 foot of snow, the rate equals snowshoeing over more than a foot of snow. Snowshoeing, skiing, and skijoring rates assume more than 1 foot of snow.
- Skis are faster than snowshoes because they require less work.

## Elevation Time Factors

- Add **1 hour for every 300 meters (984 ft) of ascent**.
- Add **1 hour for every 600 meters (1,968 ft) of descent**.
- Uphill or downhill, movement rates decrease regardless of direction.

## Formations and Pace

- **Snow-free terrain:** wedge formation maximizes fields of fire and command and control.
- **Snow-covered terrain:** column/file formation is essential — it breaks trail and keeps the unit moving. A wedge in snow forces every soldier through undisturbed snow, slows everyone, and leaves more tracks for the enemy to follow. Use the wedge only when contact is imminent.
- **Pace:** slow and steady beats fast with frequent halts. Adjust movement rates and soldier spacing to prevent the accordion effect. Spacing depends on mission, terrain, and visibility; leave enough distance to climb without forcing the following soldier to change pace.
- **Slope surfaces:** talus (boulder fields) is often a relatively easy ascent route. Scree (small loose rock) is extremely difficult to climb but makes an excellent descent route — first analyze rockfall danger to troops below.

## Load Echeloning

- **Fighting load:** what the soldier carries into direct-fire contact — kept as light as possible.
- **Approach march load:** fighting load plus sustainment equipment, carried to the assault position and dropped before contact.
- **Sustainment load:** stays at the base or with vehicles.
- The commander decides what is carried and what is left behind **before** the march, not during it. See ATP 3-21.18 for load weights (fighting load 60–80 lb, approach march load 80–100 lb, emergency approach march load 100–125 lb).

## March Planning Checklist (Mountain)

1. Route reconnoitered: slope, snow depth, avalanche and rockfall hazard, water sources, trafficable routes.
2. Movement rate selected from the table for the actual mode and snow depth — not the 4 kph flat-ground rate.
3. Elevation time factors added (1 hr / 300 m up, 1 hr / 600 m down).
4. Trail-breaking rotation planned; lead element rotated before exhaustion.
5. Rest halts planned on level ground, out of the wind, with warming and hydration.
6. Loads echeloned; drop point for approach march loads identified.
7. Communications plan includes dead zones and a backup (runner, visual signal, retransmission site).
8. MEDEVAC/CASEVAC plan briefed; medical personnel placed with each march element.
9. Weather window identified; movement timed to be off exposed ridges before afternoon storms.
10. Buddy checks enforced at every halt (cold injury, altitude symptoms, equipment).
