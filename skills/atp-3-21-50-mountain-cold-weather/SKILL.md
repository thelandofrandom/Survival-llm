# Infantry Small-Unit Mountain and Cold Weather Operations

## Purpose

ATP 3-21.50 *Infantry Small-Unit Mountain and Cold Weather Operations* (U.S. Army, 29 July 2025, 200 pages) is the Army's current techniques publication for infantry platoons, squads, and companies fighting and moving in mountains and extreme cold. It supersedes the 27 August 2020 edition. This skill distills it into field-usable doctrine for a small-unit leader: how mountain and cold-weather terrain changes organization, command and control, offense, defense, movement, load, and fires — and how to keep soldiers alive and effective when altitude, cold, and steep ground are all working against them.

Core idea: **in mountains, the terrain dictates the tactics.** Avenues of approach are few and obvious, key terrain is almost always high ground commanding a pass or valley, and a unit's combat power is capped by acclimatization, calories, water, and cold-weather skill — not by firepower. The manual's through-line is that mountain operations are slower, colder, and more exhausting than anything on flat ground, and plans that ignore that fact produce casualties before contact.

This skill covers all six chapters: organization, command and control, offense, defense, tactical enabling operations and activities (movement, observation posts, combat outposts, tunnels and caves, bases, marches, soldier load), and augmenting combat power (information collection, direct fires, fire support, medical considerations).

## 1. The Environment — What Mountains Do to a Small Unit

Mountain terrain is defined by slope, elevation, and exposure, and each of those punishes the unprepared in a different way.

- **Observation and fields of fire** are superb from heights — and mutual. A ridgeline position that sees everything can be seen from everywhere. Valleys are canalized kill zones; passes and saddles are the only ways through, and whoever holds them controls movement.
- **Avenues of approach** are few and obvious: passes, saddles, ridgelines, and valley floors. Bypasses are rare and usually worse than the obvious route. Plan for the enemy to know where you are going.
- **Key and decisive terrain** is almost always high ground commanding a pass, a valley junction, or a water source. In mountains, key terrain is often *the only* terrain that matters.
- **Cover and concealment** are thin above the treeline. Rock gives cover from direct fire but not from plunging fire or observation; snow gives concealment only while it is falling or fresh.
- **Slope surfaces matter:** talus (boulder fields) often proves a relatively easy ascent route; scree (fist-sized or smaller loose rock) is extremely difficult to climb because the rocks loosen and give way — but scree makes an excellent descent route, provided leaders first analyze the rockfall danger to troops below.
- **Weather is the decisive factor.** Mountain weather is local, violent, and fast. Temperature drops with altitude, wind accelerates over ridges and through passes, and afternoon thunderstorms build over peaks with little warning. Forecast at the elevation you will occupy, not the valley floor.

**Cold is the greatest threat at altitude.** Freezing injuries (frostbite) and nonfreezing injuries (hypothermia, trench foot, chilblain) are the most common debilitating injuries in mountain operations, and most are preventable. The four essential requirements for survival in cold weather are **warmth, food, water, and shelter** — in that order of urgency. Exertion makes soldiers sweat; sweat in very cold temperatures can freeze and cause frostbite. Leaders enforce layering discipline, the buddy system (check each other's faces, ears, fingers, and toes), and regular warming rotations with warm, non-caffeinated liquids.

**Rule of thumb for cold-weather time:** add an additional one-third of the normal time needed to any task performed in extreme cold weather — from starting vehicles to operating weapons. A plan built on fair-weather task times will run a third late before it starts.

## 2. Organization — Small Units, Decentralized by Necessity

Mountain terrain compartmentalizes everything. Units that would fight as a coherent company on flat ground break into dispersed squads and fire teams separated by ridges, dead ground, and weather.

- **Decentralized small-unit operations are the norm, not the exception.** Squads and fire teams will operate semi-independently for long periods, out of sight and often out of radio contact. Leaders at the lowest level must understand the commander's intent well enough to act without orders.
- **Close combat in mountains** is fought at short range, on steep ground, often in poor visibility. Fire teams, not platoons, are the maneuver element that actually closes with the enemy.
- **Operational influences:** the environment constrains everything — movement rates, weapon ranges, communications, resupply, and casualty evacuation. Every plan must account for the fact that a mountain unit moves slower, tires faster, and is harder to reinforce or resupply than a unit on flat ground.
- **Task organization** must account for the technical skills the terrain demands: mountaineering, cold-weather survival, and rope work are not optional specialties in mountains — they are core infantry skills for the mission.

## 3. Command and Control — Leading When You Cannot See Your People

Competent leadership matters more in mountains than anywhere else, because the environment punishes bad decisions immediately and the leader often cannot see or reach the soldiers being led.

- **Mission command is the only workable model.** Centralized planning, decentralized execution. The commander gives intent, task, and purpose; subordinate leaders execute with initiative. A leader who waits for permission on a mountain dies waiting.
- **Command posts** in static positions (assembly areas, battle positions) may be fixed, but in mountains they are frequently displaced by weather, enemy observation, or terrain. Keep the CP small, mobile, and redundant — a CP that cannot move is a target.
- **Intelligence preparation of the operational environment (IPOE)** in mountains is terrain-first: analysis of terrain and weather dominates. The manual stresses studying slope, elevation, avalanche and rockfall hazard, water sources, and the few trafficable routes — the enemy's options are as constrained as yours.
- **Communications** are degraded by terrain masking, weather, and altitude. Plan for radio dead zones; use retransmission sites on high ground, runners, and visual signals as backups. Never build a plan that depends on a single communications link working.
- **Leader responsibilities in extreme conditions:** when soldiers find it hard to remember things, leaders show patience, review orders and drills, and keep minds busy. When soldiers withdraw into themselves, pair them in a buddy system with acclimatized soldiers and keep them talking. When soldiers grow irritable, leaders stay patient, keep a sense of humor, and vary duties. When soldiers shirk tasks, leaders remind them the job is to fight and keep weapons in fighting order — cold makes tasks take longer, but it does not make them impossible.

## 4. Offense — Attacking Uphill

The offense in mountains is an uphill fight against an enemy who holds the high ground, the observation, and the fields of fire. The manual is blunt about the cost: attacking uphill against prepared positions in mountains is the hardest mission in infantry warfare.

- **Characteristics of the offense** still apply — surprise, concentration, tempo, audacity — but all are harder to achieve. Surprise is difficult because avenues of approach are few and observable; concentration is difficult because terrain disperses forces; tempo is difficult because movement is slow.
- **Movement to contact** in mountains is deliberately slow and security-heavy. Security elements (observation posts, security patrols) go out early and stay out; the main body moves in bounds, covered by overwatching positions on the high ground it has already taken.
- **Hasty and deliberate attacks:** a hasty attack exploits a fleeting opportunity with the forces at hand; a deliberate attack is planned in detail, with reconnaissance, rehearsals, and fires. In mountains, most attacks are deliberate — the terrain rarely allows a hasty attack to succeed, because the attacker cannot mass quickly.
- **Variations of attacks** include the envelopment (the preferred form in mountains — attack the enemy from flanks and rear, using terrain the enemy cannot cover), the turning movement, the infiltration (acclimatized soldiers with proper skills and equipment infiltrating to attack lines of communication), and the penetration.
- **Plan and prepare:** reconnaissance is everything. Know the route, the enemy position, the fields of fire, the weather window, and the exfiltration route before committing. Rehearse on similar terrain. Stockpile ammunition, water, and warming supplies at the assault position — resupply during the attack will be slow or impossible.
- **Execute:** move at night or in poor visibility when possible; use covered approaches (dead ground, draws, reverse slopes); assault the last covered position, then cross the final open ground fast and violently. Control the crest — whoever holds the crest holds the fight.
- **Assess:** after the attack, consolidate immediately, reorganize, redistribute ammunition, treat casualties, and prepare for the counterattack, which will usually come from higher ground you have not yet taken.

## 5. Defense — Holding Ground That Fights Back

The defense in mountains favors the defender enormously: the defender holds the high ground, the observation, and the fields of fire, and the attacker must climb into them.

- **Characteristics of the defense** — preparation, security, disruption, massing effects, flexibility — are all easier for the defender in mountains and harder for the attacker. A small, well-prepared force on good ground can hold off a much larger attacker.
- **Area defense** is the base form: platoon battle positions in depth, mutually supporting, sited to cover the avenues of approach. Positions are primary, alternate, supplementary, and subsequent — a position that cannot be shifted when the enemy finds it is a position that will be destroyed.
- **Battle positions** are sited for fields of fire down the avenues of approach, with overhead cover against plunging fire and indirect fire, and with covered routes between positions so soldiers can move without being observed.
- **Defense of a linear obstacle** (a river, a ridgeline, a cliff line) uses the obstacle itself as the primary defense, with positions sited to cover the crossing points — the only places the enemy can come through.
- **Perimeter defense** is used when the unit is isolated or surrounded: all-around security, with positions sited for 360-degree fields of fire and a central reserve.
- **Reverse-slope defense** is the classic mountain defense: positions on the reverse (sheltered) side of a crest, out of the enemy's direct observation and fire, with the crest itself as a shield. The enemy crests blind and is engaged at close range from positions he cannot see. It is the single most effective defensive technique in mountains — and it requires strict fire discipline, because the defender cannot see the enemy until he is very close.
- **Plan and prepare:** dig in, stockpile, camouflage, and rehearse. In mountains, preparation time is never wasted — a position improved for an hour is a position that holds for a day.
- **Execute:** engage the enemy at maximum effective range on the approaches; break up his formations with indirect fire on the canalized routes; destroy him at close range on the crest or in the killing area you have chosen.
- **Assess:** after repelling an attack, reorganize, redistribute, treat casualties, and prepare for the next attack — which will come from a different direction, because the enemy has learned the first one.

## 6. Tactical Enabling Operations — Movement, Posts, Caves, and Bases

### Dismounted movement rates

A soldier can only move as fast as lungs and legs allow. The normal planning rate for dismounted troops on hard-packed, gently rolling terrain is **4 kilometers per hour**; in snow or hills it drops sharply. Movement rates from Table 5-2:

| Movement mode | Unbroken trail | Broken trail |
|---|---|---|
| On foot, no snow cover | 2–3 kph cross-country | 3–4 kph trail walking |
| On foot, <1 ft of snow | 1.5–3 kph | 2–3 kph |
| On foot, >1 ft of snow | 0.5–1 kph | 2–3 kph |
| Snowshoe | 1.5–3 kph | 3–4 kph |
| Skiing | 1.5–5 kph | 5–6 kph |
| Skijoring | n/a | 8–24 kph (15 kph max recommended for safety) |

**Elevation adjustment:** add 1 hour for every 300 meters (984 ft) of ascent and 1 hour for every 600 meters (1,968 ft) of descent. An unbroken trail means undisturbed snow — slow and exhausting; a broken trail means packed or cleared snow. Trail breaking is time-intensive and requires multiple fire teams or squads rotating the lead.

**Formations:** in snow-free terrain a wedge maximizes fields of fire and command and control; in snow, the column/file formation is essential — it breaks trail and is the only formation that keeps the unit moving. A wedge in snow forces every soldier through undisturbed snow and leaves more tracks for the enemy to follow. Use the wedge only when contact is imminent. In all terrain, a slow, steady pace beats a fast pace with frequent halts — it prevents the accordion effect and limits fatigue.

### Observation posts and combat outposts

- **Observation posts (OPs)** provide early warning and observation. Site them for observation of the avenues of approach, with covered routes to and from the main position, and with a withdrawal route planned before occupation. An OP that cannot withdraw is a sacrifice, not a sensor.
- **Combat outposts** are larger, stronger positions forward of the main defense, designed to fight, not just observe. They delay and disrupt the enemy, force him to deploy early, and give the main body time to prepare. They must be strong enough to survive until the main body is ready — and they must have a planned withdrawal.
- **Tunnels and caves** are used for shelter, storage, and fighting positions in mountains. They offer superb protection from weather and indirect fire, but they are traps if the entrance is blocked or the enemy finds it — always maintain a second exit and never occupy a cave you have not fully reconnoitered.
- **Bases** (base camps, patrol bases) in mountains are sited for defense, water, and access to routes. They are built for all-around security and for the ability to sustain the unit through weather that may cut resupply for days.

### Marches and soldier load

March planning, preparation, and execution in mountains follow ATP 3-21.18, but the mountain environment makes every factor worse: distances are longer in time if not in kilometers, loads are heavier (cold-weather clothing and equipment), and the march rate is lower. **Load echeloning** is critical: the fighting load (what the soldier carries into contact) is kept as light as possible; the approach march load (fighting load plus sustainment) is carried to the assault position and dropped before contact; sustainment loads stay at the base or with vehicles. The commander decides what is carried and what is left behind — and the decision is made before the march, not during it. → `references/movement-rates-and-load.md`

## 7. Augmenting Combat Power — Fires, Collection, and the Human Factor

### Information collection

Soldier sensors — observation, listening, patrolling — are the primary collection means in mountains, because technical sensors are degraded by terrain and weather. Every soldier is a sensor; the information collection plan tells each one what to look for and how to report it. Position, ground patrols, observation posts, remote sensors, and aerial surveillance are layered so that no single failure blinds the unit.

### Direct fires

Mountain terrain changes ballistics: steep angles, thin air, and cold affect trajectory and weapon performance. Leaders must understand that weapons behave differently at altitude and in extreme cold — lubricants thicken, batteries die, optics fog, and metal becomes brittle. Keep weapons warm, dry, and clean; test-fire at the altitude you will fight at. Direct-fire weapons are sited for fields of fire down the avenues of approach, with overlapping coverage so no approach is uncovered.

### Fire support

Indirect fires in mountains are complicated by masking (ridges block trajectories), by the difficulty of observing targets, and by the danger of friendly fire on reverse slopes. Aviation fires are limited by weather, altitude, and landing-zone availability. Plan fires early, register targets on the approaches, and never assume a fire mission will be available when you need it — weather can ground aircraft and mask artillery for hours.

### Medical: altitude and cold injuries

The medical picture in mountains is dominated by altitude and cold, and the two can occur simultaneously with confusingly similar symptoms.

- **Acute mountain sickness (AMS)** is the most common altitude illness: headache, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, fatigue, sleep disturbances. Incidence is over 20 percent above 2,400 meters (8,000 feet) and rises with altitude.
- **High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE):** severe shortness of breath, frothing at the mouth, cyanosis (blue skin). **High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE):** severe headache, stumbling, confusion, incoherent speech. Both are life-threatening. **The most effective treatment for all altitude sickness is descent and rest.**
- **Altitude bands and acclimatization:** most soldiers can operate up to 2,400 meters (8,000 feet) with minimal effects. Acclimatization for 2,400–4,200 meters (8,000–13,800 feet) usually takes **1 to 2 weeks**; above 4,200 meters add **another 2 weeks**. Acclimatization above 5,200 meters (17,000 feet) is generally not possible for many personnel. There are **no shortcuts** — any attempt to trim or bypass the process usually results in injuries. Soldiers continue to train during acclimatization (crevasse crossing, foot marches, weapons firing, rock climbing). Stateside acclimatization is possible, but de-acclimatization is likely if transport to theater exceeds a few days, and troops must acclimate in theater.
  - Low altitude, 0–1,000 m (0–3,300 ft): no effect.
  - Moderate altitude, 1,000–2,400 m (3,300–8,000 ft): little to no effect; effects usually start around 2,400 m.
  - High altitude, 2,400–4,200 m (8,000–14,000 ft): slower movement and performance; judgment and cognitive skills may be affected; AMS; gain no more than **300 meters (1,000 feet) per day above 3,000 meters (10,000 feet)**; mandatory and immediate evacuation for HAPE/HACE at 3,700 meters (12,000 feet) and above; 50–80 percent ineffective at 4,200 meters.
  - Very high altitude, 4,200–6,000 m (14,000–19,800 ft): mandatory acclimatization (1 to 2 days for every 1,000 feet above 10,000 feet); HAPE/HACE increase in severity and incidence; limited time at elevation; highly experienced and trained personnel only; specialized equipment.
  - Extremely high altitude, above 6,000 m (19,800 ft): weeks of acclimatization; no air assets above 5,500 meters (18,000 feet).
- **Cold injuries:** frostbite and snow blindness are the common injuries at altitude. Altitude sickness and cold injuries can occur simultaneously and their signs can be confused — **evacuate any soldier who is coughing and stumbling immediately to medical support at lower altitude.** With command emphasis and proper equipment, clothing, and training, most cold-weather injuries are preventable.
- **MEDEVAC vs. CASEVAC:** medical evacuation moves casualties on dedicated, properly marked medical platforms with en route care; casualty evacuation moves casualties on nonmedical vehicles or aircraft without en route care. Plan both, rehearse both, and place medical personnel with each march element. In mountains, evacuation is slow — plan for prolonged field care. → `references/altitude-and-cold-injuries.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a mountain or cold-weather infantry task, answer with the manual's logic: (1) name the terrain level and the governing constraint (altitude, cold, slope, weather); (2) give the procedure as a numbered sequence with the numbers that matter (movement rates, ascent/descent time factors, acclimatization days, altitude bands); (3) state the safety check or abort criterion (descent for altitude illness, withdrawal route for an OP, refreezing rule for frostbite); (4) cite the chapter. Never recommend an attack, a movement rate, or a load above the unit's acclimatization and training level without flagging the gap. If the user asks about offense or defense, lead with the terrain's effect on that operation before giving the tactical answer.

## Operating Rules

1. **The terrain sets the schedule.** Acclimatization, weather, and snowpack are not negotiable; a plan that fights them is a casualty report waiting to be written.
2. **Decentralize or fail.** Squads will fight out of sight and out of contact — intent must be clear enough to act on without orders.
3. **Slow and steady moves; fast and halting exhausts.** Movement rates are planning facts, not aspirations — 4 kph on flat ground, far less in snow, plus an hour per 300 meters of climb.
4. **Hold the crest, own the reverse slope.** The reverse-slope defense is the mountain defender's best weapon; the crest is the attacker's prize.
5. **Descend on doubt.** AMS, HAPE, HACE, bad weather, suspect snow — descent is the treatment, the abort, and the rescue all at once.
6. **Cold is the enemy that never sleeps.** Warmth, food, water, shelter — and the buddy system that checks all four on every soldier, every halt.
