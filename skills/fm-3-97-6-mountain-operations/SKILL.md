# FM 3-97.6 Mountain Operations

## Purpose

FM 3-97.6, *Mountain Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 28 November 2000, 140 pages) — the Army's turn-of-the-century mountain operations manual, written for commanders, staffs, and subordinate leaders conducting operations below division level in mountainous terrain. It covers the mission of the infantry brigade and its supporting arms and services, plus the specialized techniques and training combat in mountains requires. Its material applies without modification to both nuclear and non-nuclear warfare. The manual is organized as intelligence (the physical environment and its effects on people and equipment), command and control, firepower and force protection, maneuver, and logistics and combat service support, with appendices on mountain illnesses and injuries and on forecasting weather in the mountains.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 3-97.6 itself superseded FM 90-6 (30 June 1980). For current Army doctrine, FM 3-97.6 has in turn been superseded in spirit by **TC 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (2025)** and **ATP 3-90.97, *Mountain Warfare and Cold Weather Operations* (2016)** — those are the current references, and where they disagree with FM 3-97.6 on a procedure or a number, follow the current manual. FM 3-97.6 remains valuable because it is the most complete single-volume treatment of mountain operations as a combined-arms problem: its chapters on intelligence, command and control, firepower, maneuver, and logistics treat the mountain as an operational environment, not just a climbing problem, and its appendices on altitude illness and field weather forecasting are still sound. The durable core is the terrain judgment, the acclimatization doctrine, the cold-injury prevention, and the maneuver principles — the weapons, communications, and organizational details are dated.

## 1. The Mountain Environment

**1.1. Terrain dominates everything.** Mountains restrict deployment, canalize movement, deny mutual support between adjacent units, and make rapid employment and shifting of reserves difficult. Small units gain many occasions for sudden and bold action, and opportunities to deceive the enemy are many. The manual divides mountain terrain into levels by the skills and equipment movement requires: at the lowest level, dismounted movement on foot with no special equipment; higher levels demand fixed ropes, then assault climbers and fixed alpine paths. The steeper the ground, the more the operation becomes a mountaineering problem rather than a maneuver problem.

**1.2. Weather is a weapon and a hazard.** Temperature falls with altitude, wind increases, and weather at different elevations in the same region can differ sharply. Mountain weather changes fast and locally: heavy snowfall increases avalanche hazard and can force changes to plans, and avalanches have taken the lives of more soldiers in mountain operations than all other terrain hazards combined. Commanders must use general forecasts together with locally observed conditions. Appendix B gives a field forecasting method: a portable aneroid barometer, thermometer, wind meter, and hygrometer, with marked or abnormal changes within a 12-hour period suggesting a weather change; and cloud reading — shape (cumulus vs. stratus) indicates atmospheric stability, height indicates the distance of an approaching storm. Cumulus clouds usually mean instability and violent weather with heavy rain or snow and strong gusty winds; stratus clouds usually mean a stable atmosphere but can signal an approaching storm with light, steady precipitation. → `references/environment-and-weather.md`

**1.3. Nutrition and water at altitude.** Soldiers need **4,500 calories or more per day** in the mountains, eaten as small, frequent meals with snacks often, and with increased fat in the diet — chronic fatigue (energy depletion) resembles and aggravates hypothermia, and the body without fuel cannot maintain temperature. Dehydration is a constant threat: drink **3 to 4 quarts of water per day when static and up to 8 quarts during increased activity**, avoid caffeine and alcohol, and force drinking — when soldiers become thirsty they are already dehydrated, and the thirst sensation is blunted by hypoxia. In cold climates sweat evaporates so rapidly or is absorbed so thoroughly by clothing layers that water loss is not readily apparent. Monitor the deepness of the yellow hue in the urine. All water from natural sources must be purified or chemically sterilized, no matter how pure it appears — giardiasis comes from drinking unpurified water; boil water **3 to 5 minutes** or use approved purification tablets or filters. → `references/mountain-illness.md`

## 2. Altitude — Hypoxia, Acclimatization, and the Illnesses

**2.1. What altitude does.** As soldiers ascend, the proportion of oxygen in the air decreases; without proper acclimatization this causes altitude sickness and reduced physical and mental performance, and soldiers cannot maintain the same physical performance at high altitude that they can at low altitude, regardless of fitness level. The manual's altitude bands:

| Band | Meters | Feet | Effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Sea level–1,500 | Sea level–5,000 | None |
| Moderate | 1,500–2,400 | 5,000–8,000 | Mild, temporary altitude sickness may occur |
| High | 2,400–4,200 | 8,000–14,000 | Altitude sickness and decreased performance increasingly common |
| Very High | 4,200–5,400 | 14,000–18,000 | Altitude sickness and decreased performance the rule |
| Extreme | 5,400+ | 18,000+ | With acclimatization, soldiers can function for short periods |

**2.2. Mental effects come first.** Decreased perception, memory, judgement, and attention are the most noticeable mental effects; above 3,000 meters (10,000 feet) the senses, mood, and personality can change. Within hours of ascent many soldiers experience euphoria, joy, and excitement accompanied by errors in judgement, leading to mistakes and accidents; after 6 to 12 hours euphoria often changes to depression, irritability, or listlessness. Use the buddy system during early exposure to identify soldiers more severely affected. Night vision is significantly reduced at approximately 2,400 meters (8,000 feet) or higher. Commanders should trade speed for accuracy — allow extra time to accomplish tasks to minimize errors and injuries.

**2.3. Hypoxia-related illnesses.** Hypoxia seldom strikes alone: the combination of improper nutrition, hypoxia, and cold is much more dangerous than any of them alone. The three significant altitude illnesses:

- **Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS).** Headache, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, irritability, dizziness. Onset from rapid ascent above 2,400 meters (8,000 feet). Treatment: stop and rest; symptoms normally subside in 3–7 days if soldiers do not continue to ascend. If symptoms do not disappear, rapid descent of **150 to 300 meters (500 to 1,000 feet)** or greater is necessary. Re-ascent only after symptoms resolve. Watch for HAPE or HACE developing.
- **High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE).** Wheezing and coughing (possibly with pink sputum), gurgling sound in the chest, difficulty breathing, coma. Usually begins within the first 2–4 days after rapid ascent, generally during the second night of sleep at high altitude; most often does not occur until above 3,500 meters (12,000 feet). Treatment: rapid evacuation; seek qualified medical assistance; observe for HACE. Death may occur if rapid descent is not initiated.
- **High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE).** The most severe high-altitude illness: severe headache, nausea, vomiting, staggering walk or sway, confusion, disorientation, drowsiness, coma, usually followed by death. Treatment: immediate evacuation, preferably by air; seek qualified medical assistance.

**2.4. Other altitude effects.** Subacute mountain sickness (prolonged deployments above 3,600 meters / 12,000 feet: sleep disturbance, loss of appetite, weight loss, fatigue — a failure to acclimatize adequately). Carbon monoxide poisoning from inefficient fuel combustion in enclosed, poorly ventilated spaces at altitude. Sleep disturbances (frequent apnea and fragmented sleep, lasting weeks below 5,400 meters and possibly never stopping higher; reported as low as 1,500 meters). Poor wound healing at higher elevations — injuries may require descent for effective treatment.

**2.5. Acclimatization — the doctrine.** Altitude acclimatization involves physiological changes that permit the body to adapt to low oxygen saturation. Once acquired it is maintained as long as the soldier remains at that altitude, but is lost upon returning to lower elevations, and acclimatization to one altitude does not prevent illness if ascent to higher altitudes is too rapid. Four factors affect it: altitude, rate of ascent, duration of stay, and level of exertion. For most soldiers at high to very high altitudes, **70 to 80 percent of the respiratory component of acclimatization occurs in 7 to 10 days, 80 to 90 percent of overall acclimatization by 21 to 30 days**, and maximum acclimatization may take several months to years; some soldiers may not acclimatize at all, and there is no absolute way to identify them except by previous altitude experience. Highly fit, motivated individuals may go too high too fast and become victims — slow and easy climbing, limited activity, and long rest periods are critical.

- **Staged ascent.** Ascend to a moderate altitude and remain there **3 days or more** to acclimatize before ascending higher; several staging stops are better.
- **Graded ascent.** Limit daily altitude gain; the altitude at which soldiers sleep is the critical element. Two nights at 2,700 meters (9,000 feet) and limiting sleeping altitude to **no more than 300 meters (1,000 feet) per day** above the previous night's sleeping altitude significantly reduces altitude sickness.
- A combination of staged and graded ascent is the safest and most effective method. Where there is insufficient time, commanders may consider acetazolamide to accelerate acclimatization, but only on the advice of physicians trained in high-altitude or wilderness medicine, and soldiers must be acclimatized before commitment to combat. High-carbohydrate diets (whole grains, vegetables, peas and beans, potatoes, fruits, honey, refined sugar) aid acclimatization. → `references/mountain-illness.md`

**2.6. Cold injuries.** After altitude illness, cold injuries — freezing and nonfreezing — are generally the greatest threat. The common cold-weather injuries are frostbite (freezing), hypothermia (nonfreezing), trench/immersion foot (nonfreezing), and snow blindness. Altitude sickness and cold injuries can occur simultaneously, with signs confused with each other; coughing, stumbling individuals should be immediately evacuated to medical support at lower levels. With command emphasis and proper equipment, clothing, and training, all cold-weather injuries are preventable. Review cold-weather injury prevention, train in shelter construction, dress in layers, and use the buddy system. → `references/mountain-illness.md`

## 3. Command, Control, and Training

**3.1. Assessment.** Commanders assess mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available, time available, and civil considerations. In the mountains the assessment must answer whether the unit has former mountain warfare instructors, military mountaineering qualifications, and the right equipment — superficial knowledge of mountain warfare and ignorance of weather conditions are recurring causes of failure.

**3.2. Leadership.** Mountain warfare demands leaders skilled in the human dimension: morale, esprit, and the buddy system matter more than in flat terrain, because the environment itself is an enemy. Leaders must understand the stratification of mountain warfare — the skills required rise steeply with the terrain level — and must not commit unacclimatized or untrained soldiers to ground that demands skills they do not have.

**3.3. Communications.** Combat net radio is degraded by terrain masking; mobile subscriber equipment, wire and field phones, audio/visual/physical signals, and messengers all have a role. Plan redundant means — no single system works everywhere in mountains.

**3.4. Training.** Initial training assessment, then physical conditioning, mountain living, navigation, weapons and equipment, camouflage and concealment, fortifications, military mountaineering, driver training, Army aviation, reconnaissance and surveillance, and team development. Mountaineering training is stratified: basic movement skills for all, advanced skills for specialists, and assault-climber qualification for the few. → `references/maneuver-and-teams.md`

## 4. Firepower and Protection

**4.1. Firepower in the mountains.** Field artillery, mortars, air support, and electronic warfare are all affected by altitude, temperature, and terrain masking. Artillery ranges and effects change with altitude and air density; observation of fire is difficult where the enemy can be seen only in glimpses between ridges; mortars gain value because their high-angle fire reaches reverse slopes and defiles that flat-trajectory weapons cannot. Air support is limited by weather, altitude, and the scarcity of landing zones.

**4.2. Protection of the force.** Air defense artillery must cope with terrain masking of radar and the difficulty of siting on high ground; engineers build and maintain routes, create obstacles, and improve survivability in rock and frozen ground; NBC protection is complicated by cold, altitude, and the difficulty of decontamination in snow.

## 5. Maneuver

**5.1. Movement and mobility.** Mounted movement is canalized by roads and passes; dismounted movement is slow, exhausting, and altitude-limited. Mobility depends on acclimatized soldiers, animal transport where available, helicopters (with lift efficiency markedly reduced by density altitude), and fixed installations — ropes, ladders, and cableways — built by specialist teams.

**5.2. Special purpose teams.** The manual's key organizational idea: a small number of trained specialists multiply the mobility of the whole force.

- **Assault climbers** lead the attack on near-vertical ground, placing fixed ropes and securing the route for following troops.
- **Installation teams** build fixed ropes, ladders, and cableways and supervise their use.
- **Evacuation teams** (Chapter 5) move casualties over steep and snow-covered ground where litter bearers alone cannot go.
- Guides, observers, and scouts are drawn from the same pool of trained mountaineers. Properly used, these soldiers drastically improve mobility; improperly used — committed as ordinary riflemen — their skill is wasted.

**5.3. Offensive operations.** Planning must account for the time altitude and terrain add to every movement; preparation includes acclimatization, route reconnaissance, and installation of fixed ropes on the approach. Forms of maneuver are the same as elsewhere, but envelopment and infiltration gain value because frontal attack up steep ground against a prepared enemy is costly. **Motti tactics** — the Finnish-derived technique of cutting an enemy column into isolated pockets (mottis) on a road or trail and destroying them one at a time — are specifically discussed as suited to mountain roads and defiles. Exploitation and pursuit are limited by the same terrain that limited the attack.

**5.4. Defensive operations.** The defense is organized around key terrain — passes, ridge lines, and dominating heights — with the **reverse slope defense** as the characteristic mountain technique: the main position sits behind the crest, protected from direct fire and observation, with forward observers and patrols on the crest itself. Retrograde operations and stay-behind operations are both harder and more valuable in mountains, where a small force can delay a much larger one. → `references/maneuver-and-teams.md`

## 6. Logistics and Combat Service Support

**6.1. Planning.** Logistics is the limiting factor in mountain operations more often than the enemy. Supply routes are few, exposed, and weather-dependent; every class of supply must be planned for the altitude and the season. Time and space factors are distorted — distances are measured in hours of movement, not kilometers.

**6.2. Supply and transportation.** Supply routes must be secured and maintained against weather, avalanche, and enemy action. Transportation relies on a mix of wheeled and tracked vehicles on the roads, helicopters, pack animals, and man-carry — and the mix changes with the terrain level. Maintenance is harder in cold and altitude; lubricants thicken, batteries weaken, and engines lose power.

**6.3. Personnel and health support.** Personnel support must account for altitude attrition — soldiers who cannot acclimatize must be replaced, not pushed. Combat health support planning emphasizes evacuation: mountain evacuation teams move casualties down steep ground, and the evacuation chain must be planned before the operation, not improvised after casualties occur. Treatment at altitude is limited; many conditions, including HAPE and HACE, are treated by descent itself.

## Output Contract

When answering a mountain operations question from FM 3-97.6, give the terrain judgment first, then the altitude and acclimatization numbers (altitude bands, staged/graded ascent rules, 4,500 calories, 3–8 quarts of water), then the illness recognition and treatment (AMS/HAPE/HACE, cold injuries), then the maneuver or logistics implication. State the altitude band and the acclimatization state the advice assumes. Where current doctrine (TC 3-97.61, ATP 3-90.97) differs, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter or appendix the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Acclimatize before you commit.** Staged or graded ascent; never commit unacclimatized soldiers to high ground or combat.
2. **Feed and water on schedule.** 4,500+ calories a day, 3–8 quarts of water, forced drinking — thirst is already too late.
3. **Descent is treatment.** AMS, HAPE, and HACE are treated by going down; HAPE and HACE kill without rapid evacuation.
4. **Cold injury is preventable.** Layers, shelter, buddy system, command emphasis — every cold injury is a leadership failure.
5. **Terrain decides the tactics.** Reverse slopes, key passes, motti tactics, and specialist teams exist because the mountain dictates them.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** TC 3-97.61 (2025) and ATP 3-90.97 supersede FM 3-97.6 where they conflict; FM 3-97.6 is the deep combined-arms reference, not the authority.
