# Mountain Illness — FM 3-97.6

## Altitude Bands and Effects

| Band | Meters | Feet | Effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Sea level–1,500 | Sea level–5,000 | None |
| Moderate | 1,500–2,400 | 5,000–8,000 | Mild, temporary altitude sickness may occur |
| High | 2,400–4,200 | 8,000–14,000 | Altitude sickness and decreased performance increasingly common |
| Very High | 4,200–5,400 | 14,000–18,000 | Altitude sickness and decreased performance the rule |
| Extreme | 5,400+ | 18,000+ | With acclimatization, soldiers can function for short periods |

Mental effects: decreased perception, memory, judgement, and attention. Above 3,000 m (10,000 ft) senses, mood, and personality can change — euphoria and errors in judgement within hours of ascent, then depression, irritability, or listlessness after 6–12 hours. Night vision significantly reduced at 2,400 m (8,000 ft) or higher. Use the buddy system during early exposure. Trade speed for accuracy: allow extra time for tasks.

## The Three Altitude Illnesses

**Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)** — cause: rapid ascent above 2,400 m (8,000 ft). Symptoms: headache and fatigue, insomnia, irritability, depression, coughing and shortness of breath, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, swelling of eyes and face. Treatment: stop and rest; symptoms normally subside in 3–7 days if soldiers do not continue to ascend. If symptoms do not disappear, rapid descent of 150–300 m (500–1,000 ft) or greater. Re-ascent only after symptoms resolve. Observe for HAPE or HACE. Prevention: acclimatization, staged and/or graded ascent, no strenuous activity during stops, increased carbohydrate intake, acetazolamide prescribed by a physician.

**High Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)** — cause: unacclimatized soldiers rapidly ascending above 2,400 m, or acclimatized soldiers ascending rapidly from high to higher altitude; fluid accumulation in the lungs. Usually begins within the first 2–4 days after rapid ascent, generally during the second night of sleep at high altitude; most often does not occur until above 3,500 m (12,000 ft). Symptoms: wheezing and coughing (possibly with pink sputum), gurgling sound in chest, difficulty breathing, coma. Treatment: rapid evacuation recommended; seek qualified medical assistance; observe for HACE. Death may occur if rapid descent is not initiated.

**High Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)** — the most severe high-altitude illness. Cause: excess fluid accumulation in the brain, same ascent pattern as HAPE; most often above 3,500 m (12,000 ft). Symptoms: severe headache, nausea, vomiting, staggering walk/sway, confusion, disorientation, drowsiness, coma, usually followed by death. Treatment: immediate evacuation, preferably by air; seek qualified medical assistance.

**Hypoxia** — deficiency of oxygen reaching the tissues; onset above 3,000–4,000 m (10,000–13,000 ft) on rapid ascent. Symptoms: impaired judgment, perception, and higher mental functions increasing with altitude. Treatment: evacuation to lower altitude. Prevention: acclimatization, slow ascent, limited activities, long rest periods.

**Subacute mountain sickness** — prolonged deployments (weeks/months) above 3,600 m (12,000 ft): sleep disturbance, loss of appetite, weight loss, fatigue; reflects failure to acclimatize adequately. **Carbon monoxide poisoning** — inefficient fuel combustion at altitude in enclosed, poorly ventilated spaces. **Sleep disturbances** — frequent apnea and fragmented sleep, lasting weeks below 5,400 m and possibly never stopping higher; reported as low as 1,500 m. **Poor wound healing** — lowered immune function at altitude; burns and cuts may require descent.

## Acclimatization

Altitude acclimatization is the physiological change that permits the body to adapt to low oxygen saturation. Once acquired it is maintained while the soldier remains at that altitude, but is lost on return to lower elevations; acclimatization to one altitude does not prevent illness if ascent higher is too rapid. Four factors: altitude, rate of ascent, duration of stay, level of exertion. **70–80% of the respiratory component of acclimatization occurs in 7–10 days; 80–90% of overall acclimatization by 21–30 days**; maximum acclimatization may take several months to years. Some soldiers never acclimatize; the only way to know is previous altitude experience. Highly fit, motivated individuals may go too high too fast and become victims.

- **Staged ascent:** ascend to a moderate altitude and remain there 3 days or more before ascending higher; several staging stops are better.
- **Graded ascent:** limit daily altitude gain; the sleeping altitude is critical — two nights at 2,700 m (9,000 ft), then limit sleeping altitude to no more than 300 m (1,000 ft) per day above the previous night's sleeping altitude.
- A combination is safest and most effective. Acetazolamide may accelerate acclimatization when time is short, but only on advice of physicians trained in high-altitude or wilderness medicine, and soldiers must be acclimatized before commitment to combat. High-carbohydrate diets aid acclimatization.

## Nutrition and Dehydration

**Chronic fatigue (energy depletion)** — cause: low blood sugar, insufficient caloric intake. Prevention: adequate food, 4,500 calories or more per day, small frequent meals, snack lightly and often, increase fat in diet. Symptoms: difficulty sleeping, fatigue, irritability, headache, difficulty thinking and acting coherently, stumbling and clumsiness. Energy depletion resembles and aggravates hypothermia.

**Dehydration** — cause: loss of fluid, salt, and minerals through sweating, breathing, and urine; in cold climates sweat evaporates rapidly or is absorbed by clothing so loss is not apparent; diminished thirst sensation from hypoxia; drinking inconvenient in cold weather. Prevention: 3–4 quarts of water per day when static, up to 8 quarts during increased activity; adequate rest; avoid caffeine and alcohol; keep canteens full; use flavored powdered drink mixes to encourage intake. Symptoms: tired and weak, parched mouth/tongue/throat, difficulty swallowing, darkening urine, constipation and painful urination, loss of appetite, rapid heartbeat, headache, dizziness, nausea with or without vomiting, difficulty focusing eyes. Treatment: sufficient hydration, rest; severe cases may require an IV; insulate and evacuate. Dehydration compounds cold and altitude effects.

**Giardiasis** — parasitical illness from drinking unpurified water. Symptoms: abdominal pain, weakness and nausea, frequent diarrhea and intestinal gas, loss of appetite. Prevention: drink only potable water; boil water 3–5 minutes; use approved purification tablets or filters; keep water containers clean. Treatment: proper hydration with potable water, evacuation and prescribed medications.

## Cold Injuries

After altitude illness, cold injuries — freezing and nonfreezing — are the greatest threat: frostbite (freezing), hypothermia (nonfreezing), trench/immersion foot (nonfreezing), snow blindness. Altitude sickness and cold injuries can occur simultaneously with confused signs; coughing, stumbling individuals should be immediately evacuated to medical support at lower levels. All cold-weather injuries are preventable with command emphasis, proper equipment, clothing, and training: review cold-weather injury prevention, train in shelter construction, dress in layers, use the buddy system.
