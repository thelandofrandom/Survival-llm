# Environment and Weather — FM 3-97.6

## Terrain

Mountains restrict deployment, canalize movement, deny mutual support between adjacent units, and make rapid employment and shifting of reserves difficult. Small units gain many occasions for sudden and bold action, and opportunities to deceive the enemy are many. The same tactical principles govern warfare in mountains as on any other terrain, but their application is constrained by the ground.

Movement is stratified by the skills and equipment the terrain demands: at the lowest level, dismounted movement on foot with no special equipment; higher levels require fixed ropes; the highest levels require assault climbers and fixed alpine paths. As the slope steepens, the operation becomes a mountaineering problem rather than a maneuver problem. Commanders must match the terrain level to the training and equipment actually available — committing untrained soldiers to ground that demands skills they do not have is a recurring cause of failure.

Terrain also presents the risk of snow avalanches. Avalanches have taken the lives of more soldiers engaged in mountain operations than all other terrain hazards combined. Snow can pose a serious obstacle in any season at altitude.

## Mountain Weather

Temperature falls with altitude, wind increases, and weather at different elevations in the same region can differ sharply due to variations in cloud height, temperature, winds, and barometric pressure. General reports and forecasts must be used in conjunction with locally observed weather conditions to produce reliable forecasts for a particular mountain area.

Heavy snowfall greatly increases avalanche hazards and can force changes to plans. Wind drives snow, creates cornices and windslab, and produces windchill that can injure exposed flesh in minutes. Soldiers must understand the windchill effect — the combined effect of temperature and wind — and commanders must plan for it.

## Field Weather Forecasting (Appendix B)

The Air Force provides the bulk of weather support, but in the mountains a unit can forecast for itself with instruments and observation.

**Measurable indicators** — a portable aneroid barometer, thermometer, wind meter, and hygrometer. Marked or abnormal changes within a 12-hour period in barometric pressure, wind velocity, wind direction, temperature, or moisture content of the air may suggest a potential change in the weather.

**Clouds** — shape and height are used to identify clouds. Shape provides information about the stability of the atmosphere; height above ground level indicates the distance of an approaching storm. Taken together, both indicate the likelihood of precipitation.

- **Cumulus clouds** ("puffy," tufts of cotton, thickness equal to or greater than width, sharp distinct edges) indicate instability at the altitude where they are found. Stormy weather associated with cumulus is usually violent, with heavy rains or snow and strong, gusty winds. Precipitating cumulus are cumulonimbus.
- **Stratus clouds** (layered, flattened, greater horizontal than vertical dimensions) usually indicate a stable atmosphere, but can indicate the approach of a storm. Stormy weather associated with stratus usually does not include violent winds, and precipitation is usually light but steady, lasting up to 12 hours or more.
- High clouds (cirrus, cirrostratus, cirrocumulus) at 6,000 m / 19,500 ft; middle clouds (altostratus, altocumulus) around 4,000 m / 13,000 ft; low clouds (stratocumulus, nimbostratus, cumulus, stratus) around 2,000 m / 6,500 ft. The heights are estimates and vary with geographical location.

**Applying the indicators** — no single indicator is decisive. Combine instrument readings, cloud type and height, wind direction and velocity, and the trend over 12 hours. A falling barometer with rising high cloud and a wind shift is the classic approach of a storm; a rising barometer with clearing cloud usually means improving weather.
