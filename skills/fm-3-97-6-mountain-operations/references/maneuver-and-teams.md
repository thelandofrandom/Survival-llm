# Maneuver and Teams — FM 3-97.6

## Movement and Mobility

Mounted movement is canalized by roads and passes; dismounted movement is slow, exhausting, and altitude-limited. Mobility depends on acclimatized soldiers, animal transport where available, helicopters (lift efficiency decreases markedly with increase in density altitude), and fixed installations — ropes, ladders, and cableways — built by specialist teams. Maximum utilization of available Army air vehicles must be effected to obtain tactical mobility and accomplish resupply and evacuation, even though lift efficiency falls with altitude.

## Special Purpose Teams

A small number of trained specialists multiplies the mobility of the whole force. The manual identifies three team types, plus supporting roles:

- **Assault climbers** — lead the attack on near-vertical ground, placing fixed ropes and securing the route for following troops. They are the spearhead on the steepest terrain level.
- **Installation teams** — build fixed ropes, ladders, and cableways and supervise their use. Their supervisors also serve as mountaineering advisors to commanders.
- **Evacuation teams** — move casualties over steep and snow-covered ground where litter bearers alone cannot go (see Chapter 5).
- **Guides, observers, and scouts** — drawn from the same pool of trained mountaineers; they reconnoiter routes, observe the enemy from dominating ground, and guide units through difficult terrain.

Properly used, these soldiers drastically improve mobility. Committed as ordinary riflemen, their skill is wasted. Commanders must protect and employ them as specialists.

## Offensive Operations

Planning must account for the time altitude and terrain add to every movement; preparation includes acclimatization, route reconnaissance, and installation of fixed ropes on the approach. Forms of maneuver are the same as elsewhere, but envelopment and infiltration gain value because frontal attack up steep ground against a prepared enemy is costly.

**Motti tactics** — the Finnish-derived technique of cutting an enemy column into isolated pockets (mottis) on a road or trail and destroying them one at a time — are specifically discussed as suited to mountain roads and defiles, where a small force can canalize and isolate a larger one. Exploitation and pursuit are limited by the same terrain that limited the attack: a force that cannot move fast cannot exploit fast.

Movement to contact and the attack must be planned with the understanding that observation is limited to glimpses between ridges, that fields of fire are short and canalized, and that the enemy on dominating ground can see and shoot down on every approach.

## Defensive Operations

The defense is organized around key terrain — passes, ridge lines, and dominating heights. The characteristic mountain technique is the **reverse slope defense**: the main position sits behind the crest, protected from direct fire and observation, with forward observers and patrols on the crest itself. The enemy cresting the ridge meets the main position at close range, on ground the defender has prepared, while the defender's own observation and fire are organized from the reverse slope.

Retrograde operations are harder and more valuable in mountains, where a small force can delay a much larger one at a defile or pass. Stay-behind operations exploit the same ground: a small force left behind in compartmented terrain can harass, canalize, and delay the enemy's main force so that when the decisive battle takes place, enemy troop strength is dissipated and he is compelled to fight under unfavorable conditions.

## Logistics and Evacuation

Logistics is the limiting factor in mountain operations more often than the enemy. Supply routes are few, exposed, and weather-dependent; every class of supply must be planned for the altitude and the season. Distances are measured in hours of movement, not kilometers.

Transportation relies on a mix of wheeled and tracked vehicles on the roads, helicopters, pack animals, and man-carry — and the mix changes with the terrain level. Maintenance is harder in cold and altitude: lubricants thicken, batteries weaken, engines lose power.

Combat health support planning emphasizes evacuation: mountain evacuation teams move casualties down steep ground, and the evacuation chain must be planned before the operation, not improvised after casualties occur. Treatment at altitude is limited; many conditions, including HAPE and HACE, are treated by descent itself. Personnel support must account for altitude attrition — soldiers who cannot acclimatize must be replaced, not pushed.
