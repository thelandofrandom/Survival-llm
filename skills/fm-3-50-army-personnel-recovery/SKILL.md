# FM 3-50 Army Personnel Recovery

## Purpose

FM 3-50, *Army Personnel Recovery* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 2 September 2014, 104 pages) — how the Army runs personnel recovery. It supersedes FM 3-50.1 (21 November 2011) and is consistent with joint personnel recovery doctrine (JP 3-50), applicable DOD directives and instructions, Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff instructions and manuals, Army policy and regulation, and federal law. The proponent is the United States Army Combined Arms Center.

Where JP 3-50 is the joint doctrine that assigns responsibility across the Services and the combatant commands, FM 3-50 is the Army's implementation: what Army commanders and staffs do, how they organize, what tools they use, how they plan through the military decisionmaking process (MDMP), and how PR changes across major combat operations, stability operations, and defense support of civil authorities (DSCA). Its principal audience is Army commanders and staffs responsible for planning, preparing, executing, and assessing PR.

The manual's definition: Army personnel recovery is the military efforts taken to prepare for and execute the recovery and reintegration of isolated personnel. Army forces work together with DOD and other U.S. Government organizations, and with multinational partners, to recover persons separated from their unit or in a situation where they must survive, evade, resist, or escape. The people at risk are not only Soldiers — the PR structure addresses Soldiers, Army civilians, contractors accompanying the force (CAAF), and other designated persons, in hostile or permissive environments.

## 1. Army Personnel Recovery Operations (Chapter 1)

**1.1. Foundations.** Army PR rests on the same system as joint PR: four functions (preparation, planning, execution, adaptation) and five execution tasks — **report, locate, support, recover, and reintegrate**. Commanders — supported by their staffs — exercise mission command of the PR tasks. That means commanders are familiar with the tasks and prepared to execute them; PR is not delegated away to a specialist and forgotten.

**1.2. Organizations.** At the joint force level, the PR organization links the joint force commander, the Joint Personnel Recovery Center (JPRC), and the component commanders. Inside the Army, the PR organizational structure is built from applicable doctrine, policy, procedures, people, organizations, equipment, and information systems. At every echelon the structure is visible in two activities: maintaining the unit's link to the chain of command, and maintaining personnel recovery within the unit. Senior leaders establish and maintain the structure; commanders and staffs build a foundation for prevention of, preparation for, and response to isolating events.

**1.3. The other Services.** DOD policy directs the Services to develop a PR capability, and each Service does it differently: the Marine Corps follows a tactical recovery of aircraft and personnel (TRAP) model; the Navy conducts PR to support its surface, subsurface, and air assets; the Air Force considers combat search and rescue a primary task and equips and trains for it; the Coast Guard conducts PR to support its surface and air assets. Army planners working jointly must know these differences — the force that recovers your people may not be an Army force.

**1.4. The four Army PR components.** Figure 1-3 depicts four general components for personnel recovery: **focal groups, options, tasks, and military methods of recovery**. These are the integrated components Army forces use to conduct PR.

**1.5. Focal groups.** The three entities most concerned with applying PR doctrinal principles are the **commander and staff, the unit or recovery force, and isolated persons**. These focal groups apply the principles at the operational level and the tactics, techniques, and procedures at the tactical level. Together they integrate activities using the PR components to accomplish missions. Leadership and accountability are essential: leaders at every echelon — from company and below, through battalion and brigade, to echelons above brigade — are responsible for the conduct of operations, including personnel recovery.

**1.6. What the commander and staff focal group does.** It develops and disseminates general PR guidance, periodically revising it as conditions change; subordinate leaders build their own PR guidance on that of higher headquarters. It produces **isolated Soldier guidance (ISG)** based on the PR guidance — ISG is more developed and refined than the commander's PR guidance, may exist for an area of operations, and is force-tailored by leaders at all echelons for each specific mission within the AO. When all three focal groups base their actions on the ISG, they facilitate recovery. It maintains personnel accountability at all times — accurate accountability and reporting narrow the focus of search operations and save time and lives.

**1.7. U.S. Government PR options.** Figure 1-3 also shows the U.S. Government PR options — the diplomatic and civil efforts that sit alongside the military methods. When cooperating with multinational partners, the level of preparedness and the formal PR architecture vary; liaison with multinational partners adds options but also adds coordination burden. Partners typically represented at an embassy include USAID, the Department of Agriculture, the Central Intelligence Agency, the Department of Commerce, DOD, the Drug Enforcement Administration, the Department of Justice, the Department of Homeland Security, the Department of the Treasury, and the Federal Aviation Administration — any of which may be involved in a recovery in a stability or DSCA context.

**1.8. The five tasks in Army terms.** **Report** is most effective when conducted soon after the command confirms isolation. **Recover** is sometimes a subordinate task of another mission. **Reintegrate** aims to return the isolated person to duty with minimum physical and psychological harm; commanders force-tailor reintegration to individual needs. Reintegration is complex and can be prolonged: initial actions secure recovered individuals, provide basic life support, and transport them; the reintegration team focuses on debriefing and ensures recovered persons receive needed medical and behavioral health care before returning to duty; the commander determines who has access to recovered persons; public affairs coordinates what is said publicly. A seamless transition from recovery force to reintegration team facilitates an effective return to duty. The PR coordination staff element (PRCS) is responsible for fully documenting each isolating event and its aftermath. Government agencies or contract organizations participate in the reintegration of civilians who work for the Army.

**1.9. Army PR proficiencies.** Army forces maintain readiness for PR through the shared PR proficiencies (Figure 1-6), built on the training principles of ADRP 7-0. Army initial entry training introduces all Soldiers to the Warrior Ethos and basic combat skills; individual and collective training in units provides further instruction; training and exercises in SERE contribute to individual preparation. The proficiencies the manual names are:

- **Structure** — senior leaders establish and maintain the PR organizational structure; at every echelon it is visible in maintaining the unit's link to the chain of command and maintaining PR within the unit.
- **Communicate** — the responsibility of all three focal groups. Integrated information systems and knowledge management move knowledge between headquarters and individuals. The quality of the information matters as much as the hardware: a command's ability to improve situational awareness about an isolated person directly correlates to a positive outcome. Communication is most important to the isolated person — they expect higher headquarters and recovery forces to have robust systems to identify and locate them, but that does not relieve the individual of the responsibility to know how to communicate with those organizations.
- **Navigate** — the isolated person's ability to determine position and move toward safety or a recovery site.
- **Preserve life** — survival medicine, water, food, shelter, and protection.
- **Endure hardship** — the psychological resilience to survive isolation, evasion, and captivity and return with honor.

## 2. Responsibilities and Supporting Tools (Chapter 2)

**2.1. Responsibilities.** Chapter 2 assigns PR responsibilities by echelon and by staff function, across the Army warfighting functions. The pattern is consistent: the commander owns PR, the staff integrates it, and every leader from company level up is accountable for the personnel in their charge. Responsibilities include maintaining accountability, producing and disseminating PR guidance and ISG, ensuring SERE training for personnel at risk, maintaining the PR architecture (coordination processes for Army headquarters, Figure 2-1), and executing the five tasks when an isolating event occurs.

**2.2. Supporting tools.** The tools include joint and Army policy and doctrinal principles, SOPs, and execution documents such as OPLANs and OPORDs; collaborative software used to conduct Army operations; and the **Personnel Recovery Mission Software (PRMS)** website used to obtain and manage ISOPREP data and evasion plans of action. For Army plans and orders, **Appendix 13 (Personnel Recovery) to Annex E (Protection)** contains PR information — the Army's equivalent of the joint PR appendix. Tools are only useful if the data in them is current: an ISOPREP that is out of date, or an EPA that was never written, is a gap the recovery force discovers at the worst possible moment. → `references/responsibilities-tools-planning.md`

## 3. Planning for Personnel Recovery (Chapter 3)

**3.1. PR and the military decisionmaking process.** Chapter 3 walks PR through every step of the MDMP, with a staff-actions table for each step: receipt of mission (Table 3-1), mission analysis (Table 3-2), course of action development (Table 3-3), course of action analysis / war game (Table 3-4), course of action comparison (Table 3-5), course of action approval (Table 3-6), and orders production, dissemination, and transition (Table 3-7). PR is not a separate planning effort bolted on at the end — it is integrated into each MDMP step, so that the plan that goes out already contains the recovery architecture.

**3.2. The PR appendix.** The product of PR planning is the PR appendix to the operation plan (Figure 3-1 gives an example). It answers, in advance, who is responsible, what forces are available, what the communications and authentication procedures are, what the recovery methods are, and what the authorities are. The appendix is built during mission analysis and refined through the rest of the MDMP.

**3.3. Categories of information for PR.** The manual lists the categories of information the staff must gather and maintain for PR — the who, what, where, and how of the recovery problem. Without them, the appendix is a template, not a plan.

**3.4. Evasion plans of action.** Table 3-8 gives sample EPA categories. The EPA is the isolated person's plan, written before the mission: what they will do if isolated, where they will go, how they will communicate, how they will authenticate, and what the recovery force should expect. The EPA and the ISOPREP are the two documents that turn an anonymous missing person into a known individual with a plan — and they are the reason PRMS exists. → `references/responsibilities-tools-planning.md`

## 4. Considerations for Major Combat Operations (Chapter 4)

**4.1. Characteristics.** Major combat operations (MCO) are large-scale combat against an organized enemy — offensive and defensive tasks in a contested environment. Isolation risk is high and recovery is contested: the enemy has air defenses, direction-finding, and the will to capture.

**4.2. Causes of isolation incidents.** The manual catalogs the causes — aircraft shoot-downs, vehicle ambushes, units cut off in maneuver, personnel separated during operations in complex terrain. Knowing the likely causes lets the staff pre-position recovery capability where the risk is highest.

**4.3. Conducting the operations process.** In MCO, PR is conducted through the operations process — plan, prepare, execute, assess — with the same rigor as any other operation. The recovery mission is planned, rehearsed where possible, and executed with the full weight of available combat power, because in MCO the recovery force is itself at risk. The manual's emphasis, drawn from Operation Iraqi Freedom, is that each situation's PR planning, preparation, and execution must be tailored to the specific operation — there is no generic MCO recovery plan.

## 5. Considerations for Stability (Chapter 5)

**5.1. Characteristics.** Stability operations are conducted in a permissive or semi-permissive environment, often alongside civilian agencies and multinational partners, with the objective of establishing security and conditions for governance. The threat is different: less organized combat, more kidnapping, criminality, and opportunistic violence.

**5.2. Isolation risk in stability.** Isolation risk in stability is driven less by combat and more by exposure — personnel moving through populated areas, working with local partners, and operating without the protection of a maneuver force. Figure 5-1 shows the risk curve; Figure 5-2 shows isolation risk as a function of exposure and threat.

**5.3. Isolation risk reduction.** Approaches include limiting exposure, varying routes and routines, maintaining communications, and ensuring personnel at risk have SERE training and current ISOPREP/EPA data. Risk reduction is a command decision: the mission requires exposure, and the commander balances mission against risk.

**5.4. Department of State and DOD interaction.** In stability operations, the Department of State is often the lead U.S. Government agency, and DOD supports. The interaction is governed by policy and by the situation on the ground; the embassy country team and the military commander must agree on who leads a recovery, who provides forces, and who talks to the host nation. Table 5-1 lays out partner responsibilities and relationships.

**5.5. Interaction with other partners.** Multinational partners, intergovernmental organizations, and nongovernmental organizations all operate in the stability environment. Each brings capabilities and constraints; habitual relationships built before the event are what make coordination work during it. → `references/operations-mco-stability-dsca.md`

## 6. Considerations for Defense Support of Civil Authorities (Chapter 6)

**6.1. Characteristics.** DSCA is support to civil authorities in the United States — disaster response, civil disturbance, and other domestic missions. The environment is domestic, the authorities are civilian, and the legal framework is different: the military supports, it does not lead, and it operates under civilian authority.

**6.2. PR capability phases.** Figure 6-1 shows the PR capability phases for DSCA: the capability is built and maintained before the mission, scaled during it, and stood down after. The commander and staff focus in DSCA is on maintaining accountability of personnel operating in a chaotic, dispersed environment — disaster zones, in particular, isolate people quickly and without enemy action.

**6.3. Commander and staff focus.** In DSCA, the PR problem is accountability and communications in a degraded environment, not enemy action. The same tools apply — ISOPREP, EPA, PRMS, the PR appendix — but the recovery force may be a civilian agency, and the isolated person may be a civilian employee or contractor. The commander's job is to ensure the architecture works across the military-civilian boundary. → `references/operations-mco-stability-dsca.md`

## Output Contract

When answering from FM 3-50, answer at the level the question asks: doctrine and organization questions get the components and focal groups; planning questions get the MDMP walk-through and the PR appendix; operations questions get the MCO, stability, or DSCA considerations as appropriate. Always name who owns the action — commander, staff, unit/recovery force, or isolated person — because the manual's central contribution is assigning responsibility by echelon. Distinguish Army implementation (FM 3-50) from joint doctrine (JP 3-50) and from individual skills (FM 21-76, ATP 3-50.21, FM 21-76-1).

## Operating Rules

1. **The commander owns PR.** At every echelon, from company and below to echelons above brigade, leaders are responsible for the conduct of operations, including personnel recovery.
2. **Accountability is the foundation.** Accurate accountability and reporting narrow the focus of search operations and save time and lives.
3. **ISG is the link.** When all three focal groups base their actions on the isolated Soldier guidance, recovery is facilitated.
4. **Plan PR inside the MDMP.** PR is integrated into each MDMP step; the product is the PR appendix (Appendix 13 to Annex E).
5. **Keep the data current.** An out-of-date ISOPREP or a missing EPA is a gap the recovery force discovers at the worst moment.
6. **Tailor to the operation.** MCO, stability, and DSCA have different threats, different partners, and different authorities — there is no generic recovery plan.
