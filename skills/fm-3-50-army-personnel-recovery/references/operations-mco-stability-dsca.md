# Operations — MCO, Stability, DSCA — FM 3-50

## Major Combat Operations (Chapter 4)

**Characteristics:** large-scale combat against an organized enemy — offensive and defensive tasks in a contested environment. Isolation risk is high and recovery is contested: the enemy has air defenses, direction-finding, and the will to capture.

**Causes of isolation incidents:** aircraft shoot-downs; vehicle ambushes; units cut off in maneuver; personnel separated during operations in complex terrain. Knowing the likely causes lets the staff pre-position recovery capability where the risk is highest.

**Conducting the operations process:** in MCO, PR is conducted through the operations process — plan, prepare, execute, assess — with the same rigor as any other operation. The recovery mission is planned, rehearsed where possible, and executed with the full weight of available combat power, because in MCO the recovery force is itself at risk. The manual's emphasis, drawn from Operation Iraqi Freedom, is that each situation's PR planning, preparation, and execution must be tailored to the specific operation — there is no generic MCO recovery plan.

## Stability Operations (Chapter 5)

**Characteristics:** conducted in a permissive or semi-permissive environment, often alongside civilian agencies and multinational partners, with the objective of establishing security and conditions for governance. The threat is different: less organized combat, more kidnapping, criminality, and opportunistic violence.

**Isolation risk in stability:** driven less by combat and more by exposure — personnel moving through populated areas, working with local partners, operating without the protection of a maneuver force. **Isolation risk reduction approaches:**
- Limit exposure; vary routes and routines.
- Maintain communications.
- Ensure personnel at risk have SERE training and current ISOPREP/EPA data.
- Risk reduction is a command decision: the mission requires exposure, and the commander balances mission against risk.

**Department of State and DOD interaction:**
- In stability operations, the Department of State is often the lead U.S. Government agency, and DOD supports.
- The embassy country team and the military commander must agree on who leads a recovery, who provides forces, and who talks to the host nation.

**Interaction with other partners:** multinational partners, intergovernmental organizations, and nongovernmental organizations all operate in the stability environment. Each brings capabilities and constraints; habitual relationships built before the event are what make coordination work during it.

## Defense Support of Civil Authorities (Chapter 6)

**Characteristics:** support to civil authorities in the United States — disaster response, civil disturbance, and other domestic missions. The environment is domestic, the authorities are civilian, and the legal framework is different: the military supports, it does not lead, and it operates under civilian authority.

**PR capability phases (Figure 6-1):** the capability is built and maintained before the mission, scaled during it, and stood down after.

**Commander and staff focus in DSCA:** maintaining accountability of personnel operating in a chaotic, dispersed environment — disaster zones, in particular, isolate people quickly and without enemy action. The same tools apply (ISOPREP, EPA, PRMS, the PR appendix), but the recovery force may be a civilian agency, and the isolated person may be a civilian employee or contractor. The commander's job is to ensure the architecture works across the military-civilian boundary.
