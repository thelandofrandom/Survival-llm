# Holistic Health and Fitness

## Purpose

FM 7-22 *Holistic Health and Fitness* (Department of the Army, October 2020, incorporating Change 1 — the working copy also carries Change 2, August 2025, which removed the Pose Method running skill and corrected the Karvonen formula) is the Army's doctrine for building Soldiers who are ready for any mission at any time. It replaces the old physical-training-only model with the H2F System: five domains — physical, nutritional, mental, spiritual, and sleep readiness — trained together, periodized together, and led by commanders. It is for leaders, H2F coaches and staff, and any individual who wants a complete, evidence-based fitness system rather than a workout plan.

Key grounding facts: most Soldiers need 7 to 9 hours of sleep every 24 hours to maximize health and sustain performance; the five H2F domains are physical, nutritional, mental, spiritual, and sleep readiness; training is periodized into microcycles (a week), mesocycles (several weeks), and macrocycles (months to a season), moving through Base, Build, Peak, Combat, and Recovery phases; and the Army Combat Fitness Test in this edition has six events — 3-repetition maximum deadlift, standing power throw, hand-release push-up, sprint-drag-carry, leg tuck, and 2-mile run.

## 1. The H2F System: Principles and Elements (Chapter 1)

**1.1 Principles.** Three principles govern the system:
- **Optimization:** train the whole person, not one quality. A Soldier who is strong but sleep-deprived, or fit but under-fueled, is not ready.
- **Individualization:** programs are adjusted to the individual — age, sex, training history, injuries, and job demands all change the prescription.
- **Immersion:** H2F is not a class or a gym session; it is embedded in the unit's daily life, schedule, and culture.

**1.2 Elements.** Five elements make the system work:
- **Governance:** policy, standards, and accountability — leaders own readiness.
- **Program:** the doctrinal description of how leaders implement the H2F System in their units, covering the five domains.
- **Personnel:** H2F staff — strength and conditioning coaches, athletic trainers, dietitians, occupational therapists, physical therapists, cognitive performance specialists, and chaplains — embedded with units.
- **Equipment and facilities:** the training spaces and tools that make the program real — from the field to the H2F performance center.
- **Leader education:** leaders are taught the system before they are expected to run it.

**1.3 The standard.** The doctrine is the standard to optimize Soldiers' performance as they move about the Army and the battlefield. It applies from the Future Soldier Program through Basic Combat Training, Advanced Individual Training, the first unit of assignment, and the sustaining phase — from first day to separation.

## 2. Phases (Chapter 2)

**2.1 Initial phase.** Future Soldier Program (pre-enlistment preparation); Basic Combat Training; Advanced Individual Training / One Station Unit Training; first unit of assignment. In the initial phase, the emphasis is on building the base: general physical preparedness, movement competency, and the habits of sleep, nutrition, and mental readiness. Leaders must teach and follow the other H2F domains — particularly sleep and nutrition — alongside physical training; recruits who are under-slept and under-fueled do not adapt, they break.

**2.2 Sustaining phase.** From the first unit of assignment to separation: Soldiers continue to build readiness, optimize performance, and mitigate the rate of loss of physical and mental preparedness for any mission at any time. The sustaining phase includes Warrant Officer Candidate School, Warrant Officer Basic Course, Basic Officer Leader Courses, Active and Reserve Component training, individual training, and condensed training for units with limited time.

**2.3 The through-line.** Readiness is not built once and kept; it is built, peaked, used, and rebuilt — the periodization model of Chapter 5. A Soldier's career is a series of training cycles, not a single fitness test.

## 3. The Five Domains (Chapter 3)

**3.1 Physical readiness.** The ability to perform warrior tasks and battle drills and high-physical-demand tasks — lift, carry, move, fight — under load and under stress. Built through the program design of Chapter 6 and the physical-readiness training of Chapter 7. The Army Combat Fitness Test is the assessment, not the training program.

**3.2 Nutritional readiness.** Foundational nutrition (a healthy eating pattern of nutrient-dense whole foods — vegetables from all subgroups, whole fruits, lean protein, healthy fats, complex carbohydrates), health promotion and chronic-disease prevention, body composition, operational nutrition (eating in the field), event fueling and post-event recovery, and nutrition in extreme environments. The manual's rule: food is fuel and recovery, not just calories.

**3.3 Mental readiness.** Cognitive capability (attention, memory, decision-making under stress), emotional capability (regulation, stress management), and interpersonal capability (communication, teamwork, leadership). Mental readiness is trained like physical readiness — with deliberate practice, not lectures.

**3.4 Spiritual readiness.** Meaning, purpose, and connection — developed through general practices (reflection, values clarification, community) and religious practices for those who hold them, with attention to free exercise, religious liberty, the Establishment Clause, and pluralism. The Chaplain Corps leads spiritual-readiness development; commanders enable it; the individual owns it.

**3.5 Sleep readiness.** The domain leaders most often neglect and the one with the fastest payoff. Most Soldiers need **7 to 9 hours of sleep every 24 hours** to maximize health and sustain performance. The relationship between sleep duration and cognitive readiness is a continuum — more sleep always produces improved performance. There is no clear threshold below which effectiveness is compromised and above which it is sustained; the question is always how to get more sleep, not whether the current amount is "enough."

## 4. Physiology (Chapter 4)

**4.1 Energy pathways.** Three systems, each dominant at a different duration and intensity:
- **Phosphagen pathway:** phosphocreatine provides energy for very short duration (5–10 seconds), high-intensity, high-power activities — 3–5 second sprints, the standing power throw, maximum-repetition lifts. It depletes rapidly and requires long rest to re-energize; if Soldiers do not rest enough, loss of ATP degrades performance.
- **Glycolytic pathway:** the middle-distance system — high intensity for tens of seconds to a few minutes, producing lactate. Training it improves the ability to sustain hard efforts.
- **Oxidative phosphorylation:** the aerobic system — long-duration, lower-intensity work. Glycogen is its fuel; when glycogen runs out, performance collapses ("hitting the wall"). Soldiers avoid depleting glycogen before prolonged aerobic activity — for example, avoiding glycogen depletion from the anaerobic sprint-drag-carry before the 2-mile run.

**4.2 VO2 max.** The highest amount of oxygen that can be used during maximal aerobic effort — the ceiling of endurance performance. It is trainable, and it is the single best predictor of aerobic readiness. Training raises it; detraining lowers it; age lowers the ceiling but training raises the floor.

**4.3 Anatomy — bone, muscle, nervous system, neuroendocrine, cardiorespiratory.** Bone adapts to load (Wolff's law) — progressive loading strengthens it; sudden overload breaks it. Muscle adapts to the training imposed: heavy loads build strength, high repetitions build endurance, power work builds explosiveness. The nervous system is the first adapter — early strength gains are mostly neural (better recruitment, better coordination), which is why technique and practice matter as much as load. The neuroendocrine system (testosterone, cortisol, growth hormone) responds to training stress and to recovery — chronic under-recovery suppresses the anabolic response. The cardiorespiratory system delivers oxygen; its adaptation is the heart's stroke volume and the muscles' ability to extract oxygen.

**4.4 The adaptation rule.** The body adapts to the stress imposed — and only to that stress. Specificity is absolute: running builds running, lifting builds lifting, and neither substitutes for the other. Progressive overload (gradually increasing the training demand) is what drives adaptation; sudden jumps drive injury.

## 5. Periodization (Chapter 5)

**5.1 Training cycles.** Periodization is the planned variation of training volume and intensity over time, so the Soldier peaks for the mission rather than burning out before it:
- **Microcycle:** typically one week — the basic building block.
- **Mesocycle:** several microcycles, usually 2–6 weeks, focused on one quality.
- **Macrocycle:** months to a season — the full arc from base to peak.

**5.2 Integrated periodization — the phases.** Base → Build → Peak 1 → Combat and Peak 2 → Recovery:
- **Base:** general physical preparedness, movement competency, aerobic foundation. High volume, moderate intensity.
- **Build:** specific qualities for the mission — strength, power, loaded movement. Intensity rises, volume falls.
- **Peak 1:** sharpening — the highest intensity, lowest volume, timed to a test or deployment milestone.
- **Combat and Peak 2:** sustainment under operational demand; training maintains what the base and build created.
- **Recovery:** planned reduction in load so the body supercompensates — adaptation happens in recovery, not in training. Skipping recovery is skipping adaptation.

**5.3 The planning rule.** Every training week has a purpose inside a mesocycle, and every mesocycle has a purpose inside the macrocycle. Training without a phase is exercise; training with a phase is preparation.

## 6. Program Design (Chapter 6)

**6.1 Soldier assessment.** Program design starts with assessment: movement screening, strength and endurance testing, body composition, and — critically — sleep, nutrition, and stress status. A program designed for a Soldier who is sleeping 5 hours a night is a different program from one designed for a Soldier sleeping 8.

**6.2 Components of physical fitness.** Structural requirements (body composition, bone and joint health), physiological capacity (strength, power, endurance, VO2 max), and physical components (the trainable qualities). The program addresses all of them, in proportion to the Soldier's job demands.

**6.3 Training sessions.** A session has a structure: preparation (warm-up, movement preparation), strength, endurance, and recovery (cool-down). Exercise order matters — power before strength, strength before endurance, skill work before fatigue. Training strategies are chosen by the phase: heavy days and light days are planned, not accidental.

**6.4 Endurance training load and heart-rate monitoring.** Heart-rate monitoring depends on the measurement device and on knowing the target heart rate for the workout and the unit training plan. The **Karvonen formula** (corrected in Change 2) sets target heart rate from heart-rate reserve:

Target HR = ((HRmax − HRrest) × %intensity) + HRrest, where HRmax ≈ 220 − age.

Example: a 25-year-old with a resting HR of 60 training at 70% intensity — HRmax ≈ 195; HR reserve = 135; 70% of 135 = 94.5; target HR ≈ 155 bpm. Resting heart rate is itself a readiness signal: daily spikes above the normal range may indicate overtraining or the need to reduce intensity. Poor sleep, nutrition, hydration, and stress control all raise resting heart rate.

## 7. Physical Readiness (Chapter 7)

**7.1 Principles.** Precision (correct movement, correct load), progression (gradual overload), and integration (all qualities trained together, not in isolation). Ruck marching is trained progressively — load, distance, and pace increase over weeks, never all at once. Army Water Survival Training progresses from basic survival swimmer to combat survival swimmer, with safety as the controlling condition.

**7.2 The ACFT (this edition's six events).** The Army Combat Fitness Test in FM 7-22 (2020) is six events, in order: 3-repetition maximum deadlift; standing power throw; hand-release push-up; sprint-drag-carry; leg tuck; 2-mile run. The test is the assessment of the program, not the program itself — training only the test events produces a Soldier who can pass a test and fail a mission. (Note: later Army policy replaced the leg tuck with the plank; this manual's edition uses the leg tuck.)

**7.3 Safety.** Every training session has safety controls: qualified supervision, proper equipment, progressive loading, and the authority to stop. The leader who pushes through pain signals is not building toughness — he is building a profile (a medical restriction) and a casualty.

## 8. Nutritional Readiness (Chapter 8)

**8.1 Foundational nutrition.** A healthy eating pattern built on nutrient-dense whole foods: a variety of vegetables from all subgroups (dark green, red and orange, legumes, starchy); fruits, especially whole fruits; lean protein; healthy fats; complex carbohydrates. The pattern matters more than any single food or supplement.

**8.2 Body composition.** Managed through the eating pattern and training load together — not through crash dieting, which destroys performance and readiness. The Army body-composition standard is a readiness standard, and the healthy way to meet it is sustained, moderate change.

**8.3 Operational nutrition and event fueling.** In the field and before/after demanding events: fuel before (carbohydrate for glycogen), fuel during prolonged activity (carbohydrate and fluids), recover after (carbohydrate plus protein, fluids, and electrolytes). Post-event recovery nutrition is part of the event, not an afterthought — the first hour after hard work is when the body is most receptive to refueling.

**8.4 Environment and therapeutic nutrition.** Heat, cold, and altitude all change nutrition and hydration needs; injuries and medical conditions change them further. Hydration is the constant: poor hydration degrades performance, raises resting heart rate, and impairs cognition long before it produces thirst.

## 9. Mental Readiness (Chapter 9)

**9.1 Capabilities.** Cognitive capability (attention, working memory, decision-making under stress); emotional capability (recognizing and regulating emotion, managing stress); interpersonal capability (communication, teamwork, leadership under pressure). These are trained capabilities, not fixed traits.

**9.2 Training mental readiness.** Deliberate practice under stress — scenario training, controlled exposure to pressure, after-action review of decisions, and the deliberate teaching of stress-management techniques (controlled breathing, self-talk, goal-setting, visualization). The goal is a Soldier who can think clearly when the body is exhausted and the situation is ambiguous.

## 10. Spiritual Readiness (Chapter 10)

**10.1 The domain.** Spiritual readiness is meaning, purpose, and connection — the "why" that sustains a Soldier through hardship. It is developed through general practices (reflection, values clarification, service, community) and religious practices for those who hold them (worship, prayer, study, dietary and clothing practices).

**10.2 Leader and chaplain roles.** Commanders and leaders create the conditions: time, access, and an environment where spiritual practice is respected. The Chaplain Corps (unit ministry team, garrison chaplain's office) leads development and provides resources. The manual addresses free exercise and religious liberty, the Establishment Clause, and pluralism directly — spiritual readiness is inclusive by doctrine, and the assessment of it is the individual's own.

## 11. Sleep Readiness (Chapter 11)

**11.1 Fundamentals.** Most Soldiers need 7 to 9 hours of sleep every 24 hours. Sleep is when the body recovers, consolidates learning, and restores cognitive function — it is not time off from readiness, it is the production of readiness. Sleep continuity (unbroken sleep) matters as much as duration.

**11.2 Promoting healthy sleep.** Sleep environment (dark, cool, quiet); pre-sleep routine (consistent wind-down, no screens or stimulants late); sleep schedule (consistent bed and wake times, even on weekends where the mission allows). Leadership's role: set conditions, lead by example, educate and encourage, prioritize and plan. Leaders who treat sleep as a weakness produce units that perform like they are drunk — because sleep-deprived performance is measurably equivalent to intoxication.

**11.3 Planning for insufficient sleep.** When the mission makes 7–9 hours impossible, the manual gives an overall strategy: bank sleep before the deficit (sleep extension before a known short-sleep period), use naps strategically (short naps restore alertness), manage the sleep debt on return (recovery sleep is real and works), and use interventions — caffeine timed correctly, light exposure, and activity — to sustain readiness. When to seek expert help: persistent insomnia, loud snoring with witnessed apneas, excessive daytime sleepiness, or sleep problems that do not resolve with the basics — these are medical issues, not discipline issues.

## 12. Special Conditioning, Coaching, and Schedules (Chapters 12–14)

**12.1 Special conditioning (Chapter 12).** The Rehabilitation and Reconditioning Program (RCP): rehabilitation (returning the injured Soldier to function, under medical supervision) and reconditioning (returning the rehabilitated Soldier to full duty and fitness). The rule: better to press on too slowly than too soon — re-injury costs months, patience costs weeks. Pregnancy and postpartum physical training has its own sessions, exercises, and education blocks — progressive, supervised, and individualized.

**12.2 H2F coaching (Chapter 13).** Building Soldier readiness through holistic health, proactive health, physical exercise, surroundings, personal development, nutrition, recharge, family/friends/colleagues, spirit, and the power of the mind. Coaching is the delivery mechanism for the whole system — the coach translates doctrine into the individual Soldier's week.

**12.3 Schedules (Chapter 14).** The manual's longest chapter: complete H2F training schedules for every phase — Future Soldier Program, Basic Combat Training, Fitness Training Unit ACFT Improvement, Advanced Individual Training and One Station Unit Training, first unit of assignment, and the sustaining phase (Build 1, Build 2, Peak 1, Peak 2, Combat, Transition), plus Reserve Component and remotely located Soldiers. The schedules are the implementation — the doctrine made into a calendar.

## Output Contract

When asked about fitness, readiness, or the H2F System, answer from this manual's structure: the five domains first, then the physiology that explains training, then periodization, then the domain chapters. Give the numbers the manual gives — 7 to 9 hours of sleep, the Karvonen formula, the 5–10 second phosphagen window, the six ACFT events — and say when a number is an approximation (HRmax ≈ 220 − age). Never present a workout plan as the H2F System — the system is five domains, periodized, led, and coached. If the question needs current ACFT policy (the leg-tuck-to-plank change), say this manual's edition uses the leg tuck and note the change explicitly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Five domains, not one.** Physical, nutritional, mental, spiritual, sleep — a program that trains one is not H2F.
2. **Sleep is readiness production, not time off.** 7–9 hours is the standard; more sleep always improves performance.
3. **Adaptation happens in recovery.** Skipping recovery is skipping adaptation.
4. **Specificity is absolute.** Train the quality you need; running does not build lifting and lifting does not build running.
5. **Progressive overload, never sudden jumps.** Sudden load increases drive injury, not adaptation.
6. **The test is the assessment, not the program.** Training only the ACFT events produces a Soldier who can pass a test and fail a mission.
7. **Recondition slowly.** Better to press on too slowly than too soon after injury.
