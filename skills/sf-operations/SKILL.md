# Special Forces Operations

## Purpose

FM 31-21 *Special Forces Operations* (U.S. Army, February 1969, 142 pages, with Change 1 of 30 November 1971) is the Vietnam-era doctrinal manual for U.S. Army Special Forces. It is the capstone statement of how Special Forces understood their mission at the height of the Cold War: organizing, training, and employing indigenous resistance forces in unconventional warfare (UW), plus stability operations and other special missions.

> **HISTORIC DOCTRINE — READ THIS FIRST.** This manual is more than half a century old. Its organizations (the 1969 Special Forces group, the A/B/C detachment structure, the Special Forces Operational Base), its communications and infiltration means, its references to the unified command structure, and many of its tactics reflect the technology and the wars of the 1960s. **Do not treat it as current tactics, techniques, and procedures.** Current SF doctrine lives in FM 3-18, ATP 3-05-series publications, and related current manuals. What remains durable in FM 31-21 — and the reason it belongs in this vault — is its **principles and organization**: how a small detachment builds a resistance movement from nothing, how area commands are structured, how guerrilla warfare actually works, and how a force survives by being inseparable from the population. Use it for principles, organization, and historical understanding. Verify anything operational against current doctrine.

This skill distills the manual's three parts: Part One (introduction — role, mission, organization), Part Two (unconventional warfare — fundamentals, command and control, employment, infiltration/exfiltration, area development, intelligence and security, combat employment), and Part Three (stability operations and additional military operations).

## 1. The Role, Mission, and Capabilities of Special Forces

Special Forces exist to conduct unconventional warfare and related special operations. The manual states the mission in terms that still describe the core of the SF identity:

- **Organize, train, equip, and advise indigenous forces** — the resistance is the weapon; the detachment is the catalyst, trainer, and link to outside support.
- **Conduct unconventional warfare** in enemy-held, enemy-controlled, or politically sensitive territory, in support of theater objectives.
- **Conduct stability operations** — advising, assisting, and training host-nation forces, and supporting civic action and psychological operations.
- **Conduct other special operations** as directed by the unified commander, including direct action when no resistance potential exists or urgency demands U.S. personnel alone.

Capabilities the manual claims for SF: language and area expertise; the ability to live, move, and fight with indigenous forces for extended periods; proficiency across the full range of military skills (weapons, demolitions, communications, medicine, engineering); and the ability to operate with minimal outside support. Limitations are stated honestly: SF detachments are small, lightly equipped, dependent on outside logistical support for sustained operations, and vulnerable if committed against regular forces in a stand-up fight. The detachment's protection is not firepower — it is security, mobility, and the population.

## 2. Organization — The Group, the Detachments, and the Operational Base

The 1969 organization is a pyramid of detachments, and understanding it is the key to the whole manual:

- **Special Forces Group** — the parent organization, commanded by a colonel, with a group headquarters and staff (personnel, intelligence, operations, logistics, communications, medical, and civil affairs/psychological operations functions). The group is the unit that trains, deploys, and sustains detachments.
- **Special Forces Battalion** — an intermediate headquarters within the group, controlling several companies.
- **Special Forces Company** — controls multiple operational detachments.
- **Operational Detachment A (the "A-team")** — the basic operational unit: a small team (in this era, a 12-man detachment built around a commander, executive officer, and enlisted specialists in operations/intelligence, weapons, demolitions/engineering, communications, and medicine). It is designed to deploy into denied territory, make contact with a resistance movement, and build it into a fighting force. Every other element exists to support the A-team.
- **Operational Detachments B and C** — higher-echelon control detachments: B controls several A-teams in a sector; C controls several B detachments across a wider area. They provide command, control, communications, and logistical coordination between the deployed teams and the base.
- **Special Forces Operational Base (SFOB)** — the rear base (in theater or outside it) that commands, controls, sustains, and communicates with deployed detachments. It is the detachment's lifeline and its link to the theater commander.

The manual stresses flexibility: groups are "accommodated by modification" to the theater — the structure bends to the mission, the area, and the resistance potential, and a commander who treats the table of organization as scripture will fail. Detachments operate under **mission-type orders** — time-phased objectives with freedom to operate within the scope of assigned tasks — because distances, topography, and enemy counter-guerrilla activity make detailed control from the base impossible.

## 3. Fundamentals of Unconventional Warfare

Unconventional warfare, in the manual's definition, is military and paramilitary operations conducted in enemy-held, enemy-controlled, or politically sensitive territory — primarily through indigenous resistance forces. Its components:

- **Guerrilla warfare** — armed combat by irregular forces against the enemy.
- **Evasion and escape (E&E)** — returning isolated friendly personnel to friendly control; SF maintains the capability to support E&E inside unconventional warfare operational areas (UWOAs).
- **Subversion** — actions, violent or nonviolent, designed to reduce the military, moral, political, economic, or psychological strength of the enemy or the regime it supports. Subversion undermines a government or occupying power systematically; it is the resistance's political war, and SF personnel must be trained to conduct and support it.

The manual's governing insight: **a resistance movement has a political character that may not be apparent when support is first extended.** The sponsoring power must evaluate the long-range as well as the short-range effects of any support rendered. Arming the wrong movement, or the right movement for the wrong reasons, creates an enemy later. This is a strategic judgment, not a tactical one, and the manual puts it squarely on the commander and the sponsoring authority.

### Guerrilla warfare principles

Guerrilla units cannot achieve the combat power of regular units, and the manual never pretends otherwise. Their strength is **surprise and elusiveness**; their survival depends on acknowledging their limitations:

- **Offensive tactics** depend on detailed, timely intelligence and a well-conceived, rehearsed plan. The guerrilla attacks only when he has the advantage — and disappears before the enemy can react.
- **Defensive tactics** rest on protective surveillance. The guerrilla's best assurance against surprise is the population: civilian supporters augment local security and report enemy movement. The force is always prepared for rapid withdrawal to alternate areas, and it avoids static defensive combat — difficult terrain that restricts enemy ground and air observation and movement is its shield.
- **Security and area control** are achieved more easily in terrain that limits the enemy's ability to mount coordinated operations quickly. The guerrilla trades ground for time and lives.
- **Mobility, surprise, and the population** are the three legs. Remove any one and the force dies.

## 4. Command and Control of Unconventional Warfare

UW command has two organizations that must be understood separately:

- **The external organization** — the SF chain from the theater UW command through the SFOB and the B/C control detachments to the deployed A-teams. It provides direction, support, and the link to the conventional force.
- **The internal organization** — the resistance itself: the area command, sector commands, guerrilla units, the underground, and the auxiliary (the civilian support network). The **UW area command** is normally a combined command controlling subordinate sector commands, coordinating guerrilla operations, and linking the resistance to the SF detachment.

Control relationships shift as operations mature. Early on, deployed detachments answer to the SFOB, directly or through intermediate control headquarters. When UWOAs fall within the area of influence of advancing major tactical commands, operational control of guerrilla forces usually transfers to that tactical command — with SF personnel attached to the tactical headquarters as liaison and advisors. The manual warns that such delegation is generally not made below division level: guerrilla forces are a theater asset, not a battalion's extra company.

**Communications** are the commander's greatest vulnerability and greatest need. The manual's communications architecture — sole-user telephone, radio nets, and courier systems — is obsolete in its hardware, but the principle is permanent: without secure, redundant communications, the detachment is isolated and the resistance is uncontrollable. Plan communications as a primary mission task, with alternate and contingency means.

## 5. Employment — With Resistance Forces, Unilaterally, and With Air and Naval Support

### Employment with indigenous resistance forces

This is the primary employment. The detachment infiltrates, makes contact, assesses the resistance potential, and begins the long work of organization, training, and employment. The detachment commander is an advisor, trainer, and leader — but the resistance leaders must own the movement, or it collapses when the detachment leaves.

### Unilateral employment (direct action)

SF elements operate without indigenous forces when: SF skills are specifically required; no significant resistance potential exists in a critical area; or urgency, security, or policy considerations require U.S. personnel alone. Unilateral operations are short, sharp, and expensive in risk — the manual treats them as the exception, not the norm.

### Air support

Air support provides infiltration, resupply, close air support, and exfiltration. The manual covers fixed-wing and rotary-wing support, including the dependence on airfields, weather, and the enemy air defense picture. A guerrilla force that cannot be resupplied from the air cannot be sustained.

### Naval support

Naval forces provide infiltration and exfiltration by sea, gunfire support, and logistics. Ship-based aircraft extend the reach of air support into coastal areas.

### Communications in UW

Dedicated nets link the detachment to the SFOB and to subordinate resistance elements. Security of communications is a survival issue: enemy direction-finding and interception can locate a detachment faster than any patrol.

## 6. Infiltration and Exfiltration

Infiltration methods are air, water, and land. The manual treats infiltration as a full operation in itself, with its own planning, rehearsal, and abort criteria:

- **Air infiltration:** parachute (the classic UW entry), helicopter, and fixed-wing landing — each with its own signature, weather limits, and reception requirements.
- **Water infiltration:** surface craft and subsurface means; dependent on coastline, tides, and enemy coastal surveillance.
- **Land infiltration:** on foot or by vehicle through gaps in enemy lines; the slowest, most vulnerable method, and the one most dependent on local guides and the population.

Exfiltration mirrors infiltration, with the added problem that a force that has been operating and fighting has casualties, prisoners, and equipment to move — and an enemy that is now alert. Plan the exfiltration before the infiltration; a team that knows how it got in but not how it gets out is trapped, not deployed.

## 7. Organization and Development of the Unconventional Warfare Operational Area (UWOA)

This is the heart of the manual — the seven-phase model of building a resistance movement:

- **Phase I — Psychological Preparation.** Condition the population to support resistance and to resist the enemy's control. This is political work before military work.
- **Phase II — Initial Contact.** SF elements make contact with resistance leaders, assess potential, and establish the relationship.
- **Phase III — Infiltration.** The detachment enters the area.
- **Phase IV — Organization.** Build the area command, sector commands, guerrilla units, underground, and auxiliary. Establish communications, security, and logistics.
- **Phase V — Buildup.** Train, equip, and expand the force; stockpile supplies; conduct limited operations to test the force and the enemy.
- **Phase VI — Combat Employment.** Full guerrilla operations in support of theater objectives.
- **Phase VII — Demobilization.** Disband or transition the force when the need ends — or when a legitimate government exists that can assume control. Demobilization is a planned operation, not an afterthought: it covers disposition of personnel, weapons, and the political settlement.

Within the UWOA, the detachment must manage:

- **Special Forces–resistance relations:** trust is the currency. The detachment commander advises; he does not command the resistance. Cultural respect, language, shared hardship, and keeping promises are the tools.
- **Psychological operations:** PSYOP in the UWOA targets the population, the enemy, and the resistance itself — building support, undermining enemy morale, and sustaining the will to fight.
- **Communications in the UWOA:** nets that link area command to sectors and to the SFOB, with security procedures and courier backups.
- **Logistical support:** the UWOA lives on caches, airdrops, captured supplies, and the population's support. Logistics planning must precede operations; a guerrilla force without ammunition is a crowd.
- **Administration:** records, pay (or its equivalent), discipline, and the mundane machinery that keeps a force coherent.
- **Training program:** a structured program that takes civilians and turns them into guerrillas — weapons, tactics, demolitions, communications, medicine, and leadership — run on a schedule the area can sustain.

## 8. Intelligence and Security in the UWOA

Intelligence production within the UWOA is the detachment's daily work: the resistance's greatest intelligence asset is the population, and its greatest vulnerability is the same population's exposure to enemy penetration.

- **Intelligence production:** collect, evaluate, and disseminate. The guerrilla's intelligence is tactical and immediate — enemy patrols, checkpoints, supply movements — and it must flow fast enough to act on.
- **Counterintelligence:** the underground and the auxiliary are penetrated by the enemy's agents as a matter of course. Compartmentation, need-to-know, and constant vetting are survival disciplines.
- **Security:** physical security of the detachment, the area command, and the caches; communications security; and the security of the population that supports the movement. A compromised cache or a captured courier can unravel months of work in a day.

## 9. Combat Employment of Guerrilla Forces

Guerrilla combat operations follow the principles of §3: attack with surprise and overwhelming local advantage, then disappear. The manual covers raid, ambush, interdiction, and harassment operations; the use of guerrilla forces in support of conventional operations (interdicting lines of communication, seizing key terrain ahead of the advance, disrupting enemy reserves); and the coordination problems that arise when guerrilla and conventional forces operate in the same area — including the transfer of operational control discussed in §4.

**Demobilization** closes the manual's UW arc. When the mission ends, the force must be disbanded or transitioned in an orderly way: weapons collected or accounted for, personnel returned to civilian life or integrated into legitimate security forces, and the political settlement honored. A guerrilla force abandoned armed and unpaid becomes the next war's problem.

## 10. Stability Operations and Additional Military Operations

- **Stability operations (Ch. 10):** SF employment in support of host-nation stability — advising and training indigenous military and paramilitary forces, civic action, and psychological operations. The manual treats this as a distinct mission set with its own logistical support requirements and, critically, its own selection and training of personnel: stability operations demand language, cultural, and political skills at least as much as combat skills.
- **Additional military operations (Ch. 11):** the concept of employment of SF in other missions — the manual sketches missions, organization, command and control, operations, and logistics for tasks outside the UW and stability frameworks. Read it as the manual's acknowledgment that SF's real employment is always wider than the doctrine's categories.

## Output Contract

When asked about SF operations from this manual, lead with the historic-doctrine flag, then answer from the manual's principles: (1) name the mission type (UW with resistance, unilateral, stability, other); (2) identify the organization involved (detachment, area command, SFOB) and the phase of development; (3) give the procedure or principle with its rationale; (4) state what is obsolete (communications hardware, specific organizations, references to 1960s command structures) and what is durable (phases, principles, the population relationship). Never present a 1969 procedure as current TTPs without the flag.

## Operating Rules

1. **Flag the era, every time.** Historic doctrine: principles and organization are the value; hardware, structures, and TTPs are dated.
2. **The population is the center of gravity.** Without it there is no intelligence, no security, no logistics, and no resistance.
3. **Mission-type orders.** Distance and enemy action make detailed control impossible; give the objective and the freedom to achieve it.
4. **Build before you fight.** Organization, training, and logistics precede combat employment — a force thrown into combat in Phase IV will not survive to Phase VI.
5. **Plan the exit before the entry.** Infiltration without exfiltration, and combat employment without demobilization, are both traps.
6. **Security is compartmentation.** The underground that talks to everyone is penetrated by everyone.
