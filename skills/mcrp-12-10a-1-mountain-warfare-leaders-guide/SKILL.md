# Small Unit Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare

## Purpose

MCRP 12-10A.1 W/CH-1 *Small Unit Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare* (U.S. Marine Corps, May 2026, 240 pages) is the Marine Corps' hands-on guide for the squad and platoon leader in mountains and cold weather. Where MCTP 12-10A gives the MAGTF the operational picture, this publication is the leader's field book: weather reading, leadership in a mountainous cold-weather environment, movement and navigation, route planning and selection, crossing water obstacles, avalanches, movement over snow, casualty evacuation, mountain patrolling, fires, logistics and sustainment, mountain safety and health, cold-weather clothing and equipment, defensive positions and field fortifications, patrol bases in snow, and camouflage, cover, and concealment.

Core idea: **the small-unit leader is the mountain warfare system.** In compartmented terrain, the squad leader and platoon commander make the decisions that determine whether the unit moves, fights, and survives — and they make them with the knowledge in this book, applied on the ground, in the weather, under load. The manual's through-line is practical competence: read the weather, pick the route, cross the water, avoid the avalanche, move over snow, evacuate the casualty, and keep the Marines alive to fight.

This skill covers all sixteen chapters: mountain weather, leadership in a mountainous cold-weather environment, movement and navigation, route planning and selection, crossing water obstacles, avalanches, movement over snow, casualty evacuation in mountain warfare, mountain patrolling, fires, logistics and sustainment, mountain safety and health considerations, cold-weather clothing and equipment, defensive positions and field fortifications, patrol bases in a snow-covered environment, and camouflage, cover, and concealment.

## 1. Mountain Weather — Read the Sky Before You Move

Mountain weather is local, violent, and fast — and the small-unit leader is the unit's weather forecaster. Chapter 1 teaches the leader to read temperature, wind, humidity, pressure, and clouds, and to turn those readings into decisions about whether to move, where to move, and when to stop.

- **Temperature decreases with increasing altitude.** Forecast at the elevation you will occupy, not the valley floor. Expect the summit to be far colder and far windier.
- **Wind chill** is the combination of air temperature and wind speed on exposed skin. The manual gives the formula: Wind chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75(V^0.16) + 0.4275T(V^0.16), where T is temperature in °F and V is wind speed in mph. The chart's warning is blunt: **frostbite occurs in 15 minutes or less** at the coldest wind-chill values. Leaders check the chart before exposing skin.
- **Humidity and dew point:** air holds water vapor; the warmer the air, the more it holds. When air cools to its saturation point (dew point), water droplets form — fog, cloud, and precipitation follow. Humidity matters because wet cold is far more dangerous than dry cold.
- **Pressure and altitude:** air pressure decreases as altitude increases, measured in millibars or inches of mercury. A barometer is used for forecasting weather and determining altitude; an altimeter measures altitude by measuring barometric pressure. A rising barometer generally means improving weather; a falling barometer means deteriorating weather. Leaders track pressure trends, not just the current reading.
- **Clouds:** cirrus clouds (ice crystals, usually 20,000–35,000 feet, thin and feathery) can build hundreds of miles ahead of a front — an early warning. Cumulonimbus clouds (heavy, dark, towering, anvil-shaped) mean thunderstorms — get off exposed ridges and summits before they build. Cloud type, height, and movement are the leader's forecast.
- **Local winds:** in fair weather, air currents flow down streams and hillsides in the early morning and start flowing uphill as the day warms. Valley winds reverse with the sun. A leader who knows the local wind pattern can predict fog, cloud, and storm movement.
- **Decision rule:** treat every forecast as a guess with a 6–12 hour horizon. Re-evaluate at every halt. If the weather is turning, the route changes — the mountain does not negotiate. → `references/mountain-weather.md`

## 2. Leadership in a Mountainous Cold-Weather Environment

Leadership in mountains is harder than leadership anywhere else, because the environment attacks the leader's own judgment as well as the Marines'.

- **The leader's first job is the Marines' welfare.** Cold, altitude, fatigue, and fear degrade judgment, memory, and morale. The leader watches for withdrawal, irritability, confusion, and shirking — and intervenes early, with patience, buddy pairing, task rotation, and humor.
- **Skill sets and training requirements** (Appendix A) define what each Marine must know: mountain warfare skill sets, capabilities, and training requirements by level. Leaders must know what their Marines can and cannot do — and never assign a task above a Marine's training level.
- **Training** happens at the Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center (MCMWTC) and through unit programs. The human body's adjustment to altitude is a lengthy process that cannot be shortened; training must focus on lower-body and core strength, endurance, and the specific skills of mountain movement.
- **Environmental factors in an emergency** are fire, water, and shelter — the leader's priorities when a Marine is in trouble are warmth, food, water, and shelter, in that order.
- **Leaders provide initial treatment for high-altitude and cold-weather injuries** — the leader is the first medical responder, and the first minutes matter. Rugged terrain, high altitude, and cold weather increase the stress imposed on Marines and on leaders alike.

## 3. Movement and Navigation — Moving on Foot, Ski, and Snowshoe

- **Movement** in mountains is slow, deliberate, and navigation-dependent. Leaders navigate by map, compass, altimeter, and GPS — and know how to use all of them, because any one can fail.
- **Route selection** (Chapter 4) uses eight factors: terrain, weather, avalanche or rockslide potential, and five more covered in the chapter — the leader weighs all eight before committing the unit to a route. Route planners must identify and avoid avalanche and rockslide hazards, using local avalanche maps, local knowledge, current weather patterns, and past weather patterns. **The adversary might initiate an avalanche or rockslide** to destroy a unit — route selection is a security decision, not just a movement decision.
- **Navigation tools:** map, compass, altimeter, GPS, and terrain association. Whiteout, fog, and spindrift can erase navigation in minutes — carry all four tools and know how to use them.
- **Ski poles:** used to maintain balance when moving with a heavy pack over slippery, uneven ground. Grip by sliding the hand up through the wrist strap and grabbing the handgrip and strap together. On a traverse, the uphill ski pole is held along the shaft rather than by the grip.
- **Movement over snow** (Chapter 7): snowshoes and skis. The military snowshoe has advantages and disadvantages the leader must know; Marines must be able to explain them and employ snowshoes effectively. Skiing is faster than snowshoeing because it requires less work. Movement on skis uses a rhythm of **15 minutes of movement with a 2-minute break** — breaks also give troops the opportunity to adjust equipment, hydrate, and check each other.
- **Water discipline and rest discipline** apply on snow as on dry ground — more so, because the cold hides dehydration and the exertion hides fatigue.

## 4. Route Planning and Selection — Eight Factors, One Decision

The eight factors to consider when selecting a route are terrain, weather, avalanche or rockslide potential, and the additional factors detailed in Chapter 4 (including enemy, water, vegetation and concealment, and sustainment). The leader's route plan must:

- **Identify and avoid avalanche and rockslide hazards** in the area of operations, using local avalanche maps, local knowledge, current weather patterns, and past weather patterns.
- **Skirt or avoid avalanche-prone slopes, scree, cliffs, avalanche paths, and other natural hazards** — these terrain features may be part of the enemy's plan as well as nature's.
- **Consider the adversary's ability to initiate an avalanche or rockslide** in an attempt to destroy the unit.
- **Plan for the worst route, not the best:** the route that works in good weather may be a death trap in a storm. Build in an alternate route and a decision point.
- **Use trained mountain leaders for route planning** — the manual directs it explicitly.

## 5. Crossing Water Obstacles — Streams, Rivers, and Ice

Water in mountains is cold, fast, and dangerous — and crossing it is one of the most hazardous things a small unit does.

- **Crossing streams and rivers:** reconnoiter first — depth, current, bottom, and the downstream hazard (what happens if someone falls in). Cross in the morning when glacial streams run lowest. Never cross above a waterfall, strainer, or rapid you cannot survive.
- **Individual crossings** use a staff for balance and as a probe to locate footing. The staff is also used to probe depth and bottom conditions.
- **Fording in summer and winter** differ: winter fording means ice, cold water, and hypothermia risk — limit exposure time and warm the Marines immediately afterward.
- **Safety swimmers:** ensure a suitable spot downstream for safety swimmers to preposition. Strong swimmers are positioned downstream with a throw bag to rescue anyone swept downstream. As a last resort, safety swimmers enter the water. In swift water, safety swimmers use a belay line. In a training scenario, swimmers wear flotation. If a Marine is unconscious and caught on a line, the safety swimmer enters the water to attempt a rescue. Safety swimmers can be stationed downstream even when the unit is crossing a stream where the current flows toward a hazard such as a rock jam or waterfall — in that case, the safety swimmer can make a field-expedient rescue.
- **Swimming:** in fast, shallow water, Marines cross on their backs with feet downstream.
- **Crossing frozen waterways:** test the ice before committing the unit. If a Marine falls through the ice, exit the hole by shallow swimming onto the ice — **do not push up on the edge**, because it may break. Spread weight, roll onto the ice, and crawl clear.
- **Loosen pack straps** so the pack can be shed if a Marine falls in. A pack that cannot be shed is an anchor. → `references/water-crossings.md`

## 6. Avalanches — The Mountain's Artillery

A snow avalanche occurs when a mass of snow, ice, and incorporated debris slides down a slope at high speed. Avalanche formation is a complex interaction among terrain, snowpack, and weather. Leaders must avoid planning routes in potential avalanche areas, check weather and avalanche forecasts, equip Marines with safety gear, recognize red-flag conditions, and know what to do when an avalanche happens.

### Types

- **Loose-snow avalanche (point release):** starts with a small amount of snow with little cohesion and picks up more as it descends. From a distance, it appears to start at a point and fan out into a triangle. Usually small and involving only upper layers, but can become large and destructive. Occurs most often on **steep slopes of 35 degrees or higher**, during or shortly after a snowstorm, or during warming events (rain, rising temperatures, solar radiation). Moving snow in a loose-snow slide can trigger larger, deeper slab releases.
- **Slab avalanche:** one or more layers of cohesive snow break away as a unit. The slab splits into smaller blocks as it travels. Slab failure is commonly initiated when the bond between the slab and the bed surface fails. Slab thickness ranges from less than an inch to **35 feet or more**; width from a few yards to well over a mile. Anatomy: **crown** (the breakaway wall at the top, a tension fracture through the depth of the slab), **bed surface** (the surface the slab slides over), **flanks** (left and right sides), and **stauchwall** (the lowest downslope fracture surface). Speed ranges from **65 mph for a wet slab to 150 mph for a dry slide**. Most slab avalanches release on slopes with angles between **35 and 40 degrees**.

### Hazard evaluation — the data triangle

To determine whether an avalanche is possible, evaluate how four variables interact:

1. **Terrain** — is the terrain capable of producing avalanches? Seven factors: slope angle, slope aspect (orientation), terrain roughness (anchoring), slope shape, vegetation, elevation, and path history. Slope angle is critical: as angle increases, so does the stress on the slab. Avalanches can occur on any size slope.
2. **Snowpack** — is the snowpack stable? Could the snow slide?
3. **Weather** — is the weather contributing to instability? New snow, wind loading, warming, and rain all increase hazard.
4. **Human factor** — without people, there is no hazard. Consider the alternatives and their possible consequences.

### Post-avalanche actions — the 15-minute rule

- **If caught in an avalanche:** try to swim out using swimming and rolling action to stay on the surface of the snow. Fight to stay on top — the snow sets like concrete when it stops.
- **Survival odds:** within **15 minutes of being buried there is a 92 percent chance of survival**. The odds of being rescued alive go down **3 percent per minute after 15 minutes**. The rescue must happen fast — the unit is the rescue team, because no outside help will arrive in time.
- **Search organization:** keep shovels, probes, and first aid equipment readily available. Organize a hasty probe line to search the most likely burial areas. Continue the hasty probe line search until avalanche search replacements arrive. Delegate tasks to each platoon: hasty search teams, avalanche guards, and probe lines. Mark the last-seen point immediately — it is the center of the search.
- **The avalanche guard** watches the slope above for a second slide while the search runs. A rescue that is buried by a second avalanche has failed twice. → `references/avalanche-rescue.md`

## 7. Movement Over Snow — Skis and Snowshoes

- **Snowshoes:** the military snowshoe is the basic over-snow movement tool. Marines must know its advantages and disadvantages and employ it effectively. Snowshoes are slower than skis but simpler and more reliable in broken terrain and deep, soft snow.
- **Skis:** faster than snowshoes because they require less work. Ski movement uses a rhythm of 15 minutes of movement with a 2-minute break. Ski poles aid balance with a heavy pack on slippery ground.
- **Trail breaking:** in snow over 30 cm (12 inches), the lead 2–3 persons pack the trail; rotate them to the rear every 15–30 minutes (see MCTP 12-10A).
- **Cold-weather equipment in snow:** keep weapons, optics, and batteries warm and dry; snow in a weapon's action freezes it shut.

## 8. Casualty Evacuation in Mountain Warfare

- **Casualty evacuation in mountains is slow, hard, and dangerous.** The terrain that injured the Marine is the terrain the evacuation must cross.
- **Plan the evacuation before the movement:** routes, methods, personnel, and equipment. The evacuation plan is part of the patrol order, not an afterthought.
- **Methods:** litter carry (improvised litters are almost always worth building), rope lowers and hauls, and helicopter evacuation where LZs and weather permit. A casualty who cannot be carried must be stabilized and sheltered in place — prolonged field care is the norm, not the exception.
- **Cold is the second casualty:** a wounded Marine loses heat fast. Insulate from the ground, cover with dry layers, and warm with body heat or warming devices. Hypothermia kills wounded Marines who would otherwise survive.
- **Altitude illness and cold injury** can strike the evacuation team as well as the casualty — rotate the carriers, watch for symptoms, and descend if anyone deteriorates.

## 9. Mountain Patrolling

- **Patrolling in mountains** is slower, more exposed, and more navigation-dependent than patrolling on flat ground. Patrols move in file, break trail, and maintain security on the high ground.
- **Search teams** are organized for reconnaissance and for casualty and avalanche search — the same skills serve both.
- **Patrol bases** (Chapter 15) in a snow-covered environment are sited for defense, water, and access to routes, with all-around security and the ability to sustain the unit through weather that may cut resupply for days. Snow is both the building material (snow walls, snow caves) and the enemy (cold, wet, avalanche).
- **Camouflage, cover, and concealment** (Chapter 16): winter camouflage — white overgarments, snow-colored equipment, and movement discipline. Snow is bright and tracks are visible; a patrol that does not manage its tracks is a patrol that can be followed. Move on hard snow, in shade, and during snowfall when possible.

## 10. Fires, Logistics, and Sustainment

- **Fires** (Chapter 10): indirect fires in mountains are complicated by masking, observation, and friendly-fire risk on reverse slopes. Plan fires early, register targets on the approaches, and never assume a fire mission will be available when needed. Ground-to-air signals by air control party teams coordinate close air support.
- **Logistics and sustainment** (Chapter 11): the limiting factor on mountain operations. Calories (6,000+ per day at altitude), water (4 quarts stationary, 8 quarts moving, plus 2 in high mountains), warmth, and resupply by the means the terrain allows — foot, pack animal, or helicopter. A unit that cannot be fed, watered, and warmed cannot fight.
- **Cold-weather clothing and equipment** (Chapter 13): the layered system, enforced by leaders and checked by buddies. Clothing is a system — a single missing layer or a wet layer can kill.
- **Defensive positions and field fortifications** (Chapter 14): dug into frozen ground (explosives may be needed), sited for fields of fire down the avenues of approach, with overhead cover against plunging fire and indirect fire, and covered routes between positions.
- **Mountain safety and health** (Chapter 12): recognition and treatment of altitude and cold-weather injuries (see §11 below).

## 11. Mountain Safety and Health — Altitude and Cold Injuries

- **Altitude illness:** AMS (headache, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, fatigue, sleep disturbances — over 20% incidence above 2,400 m / 8,000 ft), HAPE (severe shortness of breath, frothing, cyanosis), HACE (severe headache, stumbling, confusion, incoherent speech). **Descent and rest is the most effective treatment for all altitude sickness.**
- **Cold injuries:** frostbite (frostbite occurs in 15 minutes or less at the coldest wind-chill values), hypothermia, trench foot, immersion foot, chilblain, snow blindness. Prevention: layering, buddy system, warming rotations, dry socks, and command emphasis. Most cold-weather injuries are preventable.
- **Recognition and treatment** of altitude and cold-weather injuries is a leader skill — the leader is the first medical responder. See ATP 3-21.50 for the full altitude bands and acclimatization schedule.

## Output Contract

When asked about a small-unit mountain task, answer with the leader's logic: (1) name the governing hazard (weather, avalanche, water, cold, altitude) and the chapter that covers it; (2) give the procedure as a numbered sequence with the numbers that matter (15-minute avalanche survival window, 35–40° slab release slopes, 65–150 mph slide speeds, wind-chill frostbite time, 15/2-minute ski rhythm); (3) state the safety check or abort criterion (route around avalanche terrain, second slide watch during rescue, downstream safety swimmers before any crossing); (4) cite the chapter. Never recommend a route, crossing, or slope above the unit's training level without flagging the gap and the training needed. If the user asks about avalanches, lead with the 15-minute survival window and the search organization before giving the hazard evaluation.

## Operating Rules

1. **Read the weather, then decide.** Temperature, wind, pressure, clouds — the forecast is a 6–12 hour guess, re-evaluated at every halt.
2. **Fifteen minutes is the avalanche clock.** 92% survival if found within 15 minutes, minus 3% per minute after. The unit is the rescue team.
3. **Route around avalanche terrain.** Loose snow releases at 35°+, slabs at 35–40°, at 65–150 mph. No route is worth a slide.
4. **Never cross water without a downstream safety plan.** Safety swimmers, throw bag, belay line — and never cross above a hazard you cannot survive.
5. **The leader is the first medic.** Altitude and cold injuries are recognized and treated by the leader, in the first minutes, on the ground.
6. **Snow is shelter and enemy both.** Patrol bases, snow walls, and snow caves protect — and the same snow buries, wets, and freezes.
