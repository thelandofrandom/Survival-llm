# Avalanche Rescue — MCRP 12-10A.1

Source: MCRP 12-10A.1, Chapter 6 (Avalanches) and Chapter 4 (Route Planning). Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## What an Avalanche Is

A snow avalanche occurs when a mass of snow, ice, and incorporated debris slides down a slope at high speed. Avalanche formation is a complex interaction among terrain, snowpack, and weather. Leaders must avoid planning routes in potential avalanche areas, check weather and avalanche forecasts, equip Marines with safety gear, recognize red-flag conditions, and know what to do when an avalanche happens.

## Types

### Loose-Snow Avalanche (Point Release)
- Starts with a small amount of snow with little cohesion; picks up more as it descends.
- From a distance, appears to start at a point and fan out into a triangle.
- Usually small, involving only upper layers — but can become large and destructive.
- Occurs most often on **steep slopes of 35 degrees or higher**, during or shortly after a snowstorm, or during warming events (rain, rising temperatures, solar radiation).
- Moving snow in a loose-snow slide can trigger larger, deeper slab releases.

### Slab Avalanche
- One or more layers of cohesive snow break away as a unit; the slab splits into smaller blocks as it travels.
- Failure is commonly initiated when the bond between the slab and the bed surface fails.
- Thickness: less than an inch to **35 feet or more**. Width: a few yards to well over a mile.
- Anatomy: **crown** (breakaway wall at the top, tension fracture through the depth of the slab), **bed surface** (the surface the slab slides over), **flanks** (left and right sides), **stauchwall** (lowest downslope fracture surface, a diagonal wedge-like shear fracture).
- Speed: **65 mph for a wet slab to 150 mph for a dry slide**.
- Most slab avalanches release on slopes with angles between **35 and 40 degrees**.

## Hazard Evaluation — The Data Triangle

Four variables interact to determine whether an avalanche is possible:

1. **Terrain** — is the terrain capable of producing avalanches? Seven factors: slope angle, slope aspect (orientation), terrain roughness (anchoring), slope shape, vegetation, elevation, and path history. Slope angle is critical: as angle increases, so does stress on the slab. Avalanches can occur on any size slope.
2. **Snowpack** — is the snowpack stable? Could the snow slide?
3. **Weather** — is the weather contributing to instability? New snow, wind loading, warming, and rain all increase hazard.
4. **Human factor** — without people, there is no hazard. Consider the alternatives and their possible consequences.

Learning to recognize avalanche terrain is the critical starting point. Leaders should recognize that avalanches can occur on any size slope.

## Route Planning Around Avalanches

- Identify and avoid avalanche and rockslide hazards using local avalanche maps, local knowledge, current weather patterns, and past weather patterns.
- Skirt or avoid avalanche-prone slopes, scree, cliffs, avalanche paths, and other natural hazards.
- The adversary might initiate an avalanche or rockslide to destroy a unit — route selection is a security decision.
- The eight factors to consider when selecting a route are terrain, weather, avalanche or rockslide potential, and the additional factors detailed in Chapter 4.

## If Caught in an Avalanche

- Try to **swim out** using swimming and rolling action to stay on the surface of the snow. Fight to stay on top — the snow sets like concrete when it stops.
- Create an air pocket in front of your face if buried; stay calm and conserve air.

## The 15-Minute Rule

- Within **15 minutes of being buried there is a 92 percent chance of survival**.
- The odds of being rescued alive go down **3 percent per minute after 15 minutes**.
- The unit is the rescue team — no outside help will arrive in time.

## Search Organization

1. **Mark the last-seen point immediately** — it is the center of the search.
2. Keep **shovels, probes, and first aid equipment readily available**.
3. Organize a **hasty probe line** to search the most likely burial areas.
4. Continue the hasty probe line search until avalanche search replacements arrive.
5. **Delegate tasks** to each platoon: hasty search teams, avalanche guards, and probe lines.
6. Post an **avalanche guard** to watch the slope above for a second slide while the search runs. A rescue buried by a second avalanche has failed twice.
7. Treat the rescued Marine for hypothermia, trauma, and asphyxiation; evacuate to medical support.
