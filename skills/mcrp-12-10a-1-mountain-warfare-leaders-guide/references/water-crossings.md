# Water Crossings — MCRP 12-10A.1

Source: MCRP 12-10A.1, Chapter 5 (Crossing Water Obstacles). Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Before Any Crossing

- Reconnoiter first: depth, current, bottom, and the **downstream hazard** — what happens if someone falls in.
- Cross in the **morning** when glacial streams run lowest.
- **Never cross above a waterfall, strainer, or rapid you cannot survive.**
- Loosen pack straps so the pack can be shed if a Marine falls in. A pack that cannot be shed is an anchor.

## Crossing Streams and Rivers

- **Individual crossings** use a staff for balance and as a probe to locate footing. The staff is also used to probe depth and bottom conditions.
- **Team crossings** link arms or use a rope; belayed crossings use a fixed line with the crosser clipped in.
- Face upstream, brace against the current, move deliberately — one foot at a time, staff planted before the next step.
- Swimming is the last resort.

## Safety Swimmers

- Ensure a suitable spot **downstream** for safety swimmers to preposition.
- Strong swimmers are positioned downstream with a **throw bag** to rescue anyone swept downstream.
- As a **last resort**, safety swimmers enter the water to attempt a rescue.
- In swift-moving water, safety swimmers use a **belay line** to rescue individuals.
- In a training scenario, swimmers wear flotation.
- If a Marine is unconscious and caught on a line, the safety swimmer enters the water to attempt a rescue.
- Safety swimmers can be stationed downstream even when the unit is crossing a stream where the current flows toward a hazard such as a rock jam or waterfall — in that case, the safety swimmer can make a field-expedient rescue.

## Swimming a Crossing

- In fast, shallow water, Marines cross **on their backs with feet downstream**.
- Feet downstream protects the head from rocks and lets the Marine fend off obstacles with the feet.
- Keep the pack on only if it floats and can be shed; otherwise, float it separately or ferry it across.

## Fording in Summer

- Summer fording is faster and warmer, but glacial streams run highest in the afternoon as snow melts — cross in the morning.
- Check for flash-flood risk after rain upstream; a stream that is fordable in the morning can be deadly by afternoon.

## Fording in Winter

- Winter fording means ice, cold water, and hypothermia risk — limit exposure time and warm Marines immediately afterward.
- Dry off, change into dry clothing, and get moving to generate heat. A wet Marine in winter who stops moving is a hypothermia casualty.
- Temperatures below 0 °F for more than 4 or 5 hours require special equipment care — operators must allow 10 to 15 minutes for equipment to warm or adjust.

## Crossing Frozen Waterways

- **Test the ice before committing the unit.** Ice thickness, quality, and uniformity vary — clear ice is stronger than white or cloudy ice; moving water underneath weakens ice.
- Cross in file, spaced out, with a rope between Marines if the ice is suspect.
- If a Marine falls through the ice: **exit the hole by shallow swimming onto the ice. Do not push up on the edge**, because it may break. Spread weight, roll onto the ice, and crawl clear.
- Rescue from the ice: approach the hole by crawling or sliding to spread weight; extend a rope, pole, or ladder; pull the Marine out horizontally, not vertically.
- Warm the rescued Marine immediately — dry clothing, insulation from the ground, warm liquids, and evacuation if hypothermic.
