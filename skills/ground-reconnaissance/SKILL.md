# Ground Reconnaissance Operations

## Purpose

MCRP 2-10A.6 *Ground Reconnaissance Operations* (U.S. Marine Corps, 4 April 2018; formerly MCWP 2-25, 407 pages) is the Marine Corps' doctrine for ground reconnaissance — how reconnaissance units find the enemy, report what they find, and shape the commander's decisions without getting decisively engaged. It covers the full spectrum: the units, the command-and-control architecture, mission development, planning, the reconnaissance missions themselves, methods of maneuver (insertion), communications, and intelligence reporting.

Core idea: **reconnaissance is the commander's eyes, and eyes that fight are blind.** A reconnaissance unit's product is information, not enemy casualties. Every decision in the manual — stealth over firepower, reporting over engaging, exfiltration over holding ground — flows from that one principle. The manual's second core idea is that reconnaissance is a system, not a patrol: the Surveillance and Reconnaissance Coordination Center (SARCC), the Reconnaissance Operations Center (ROC), the mission planning folder, and the RAIDERREP reporting format are the machinery that turns a team's observations into decisions.

This skill distills chapters 1–9 and appendices A–J: full-spectrum operations, units and organizations, command and control, mission development, planning overview, reconnaissance operations, methods of maneuver, communications, and intelligence operations and reporting.

## 1. Full-Spectrum Operations — Where Reconnaissance Fits

Ground reconnaissance supports every type of Marine Corps operation: offensive, defensive, other tactical operations, stability, counterinsurgency, reconnaissance and security, military engagement and security cooperation, and crisis response. The manual frames the battlespace in three zones:

- **Deep operations** — beyond the close fight, against enemy reserves, command and control, and logistics. Reconnaissance here is strategic in effect: it finds the targets that shape the battle before it is joined.
- **Close operations** — the fight itself. Reconnaissance here is tactical and immediate: enemy positions, obstacles, and avenues of approach in front of the main effort.
- **Rear operations** — security of lines of communication, bases, and sustainment. Reconnaissance here is preventive: finding the threat before it finds the rear.

**Employment concepts:** reconnaissance supports three kinds of operations — access operations (gaining entry to denied areas), maneuver operations (finding the way for the main force), and target operations (finding and designating targets for fires). Each carries its own risk assessment: deep reconnaissance accepts higher risk for higher payoff; close reconnaissance trades risk against the immediate needs of the maneuver commander.

**Reconnaissance push vs. reconnaissance pull** — the manual's two basic methods, and a distinction every commander must understand:

- **Reconnaissance push** uses reconnaissance elements conservatively: they confirm what the commander already believes and fill specific gaps. The main force's plan is largely set, and reconnaissance refines it. The manual cites I MEF in Desert Storm (1991) as characterized by reconnaissance push.
- **Reconnaissance pull** lets reconnaissance *drive* the plan: the main force follows where reconnaissance finds weakness, gaps, and opportunity. Pull is more aggressive, more demanding on the reconnaissance force, and more rewarding when the enemy situation is genuinely uncertain.

The choice is a commander's decision, made before the mission, and it changes everything about tasking, risk tolerance, and reporting priority.

## 2. Units and Organizations — Who Does Reconnaissance

The Marine Corps fields several reconnaissance-capable organizations, each with a different character:

- **Reconnaissance Battalion** — the division-level ground reconnaissance force. Its mission is to conduct ground reconnaissance and surveillance in support of the division, and to perform other tasks (including limited offensive operations) as directed. Organized into reconnaissance companies, which break into reconnaissance platoons and teams. Sustainment is a constant constraint: reconnaissance units operate forward, lightly, and far from support.
- **Reconnaissance Company** — the battalion's maneuver element; deploys platoons and teams on specific missions.
- **Reconnaissance Platoon** — the basic maneuver element: small, mobile, capable of independent action for limited periods. Characteristics: stealth, mobility, endurance, and communications. Capabilities include area, zone, and route reconnaissance, surveillance, and target acquisition. Limitations are honest: limited firepower, limited sustainment, and vulnerability if detected and fixed.
- **Force Reconnaissance Company** — the MEF-level deep reconnaissance force, with capabilities beyond the battalion: deep insertion (including amphibious, airborne, and subsurface), direct action in limited circumstances, and support to special operations. Its platoons are the Marine Corps' deepest ground reconnaissance asset.
- **Radio Reconnaissance Platoon (Radio Battalion)** — signals intelligence support to reconnaissance.
- **Ground Sensor Platoon (Intelligence Battalion)** — remote sensor employment.
- **Light Armored Reconnaissance (LAR) Battalion** — mounted reconnaissance with speed, firepower, and survivability the dismounted teams lack — and a signature the dismounted teams do not have.
- **Scout Sniper Platoon (Infantry Battalion)** — surveillance and precision fires in support of the infantry battalion.

**Joint and other-service assets** the manual lists as available in support: Army Special Forces, Ranger Regiment, special operations aviation (rotary- and fixed-wing), SEAL teams, Special Warfare Combatant-craft Crewmen, special operations combat controllers, and MARSOC. The point is that ground reconnaissance does not operate alone — it is one node in a joint reconnaissance enterprise, and the reconnaissance commander must know what else is available.

**Reconnaissance battalion corpsmen** get their own section: the corpsman is a combat multiplier and a sustainment necessity, and the manual details his roles and responsibilities because a reconnaissance team without medical capability is a team that cannot take a casualty and keep moving.

## 3. Command and Control — The SARCC, the ROC, and the Reporting Chain

Reconnaissance C2 is a system of centers, and the manual spends a full chapter on it because a reconnaissance report that never reaches the commander is worthless.

- **G-2/S-2 (intelligence) section** — owns the intelligence effort: develops requirements, tasks collection, and fuses reporting.
- **Intelligence Operations Center (IOC)** — the staff fusion node.
- **Surveillance and Reconnaissance Coordination Center (SARCC)** — the coordination hub for all surveillance and reconnaissance in the MAGTF. The SARCC deconflicts collection, tasks assets, and ensures coverage. There is a senior SARCC at the MAGTF/GCE level, a regimental SARCC, and a battalion SARCC — each with defined functions. The SARCC's liaison cell connects to supported units.
- **Reconnaissance Operations Center (ROC)** — the reconnaissance unit's own operations center: it plans, controls, and sustains deployed teams, maintains communications, and passes reporting up the chain. The manual details ROC responsibilities, personnel, and operations.
- **Supported unit's Combat Operations Center (COC)** — receives reporting and coordinates fire support and maneuver with the reconnaissance effort.
- **Fire Support Coordination Center (FSCC)** — coordinates fires in support of reconnaissance, including the critical problem of firing in support of a team that must remain undetected.

**Command and support relationships** matter more in reconnaissance than in most operations: a reconnaissance platoon may be in general support, direct support, or attached to a maneuver unit, and each relationship changes who tasks it, who sustains it, and who owns its reporting. The manual's key personnel section (unit commander, executive officer, staff, subordinate commanders, liaison officers) defines who does what when the plan meets the enemy.

**Communications, computers, and intelligence (C2/C4I):** reconnaissance depends on communications more than any other ground arm. A team that cannot report is a team that does not exist for the commander. The manual stresses redundant means, disciplined reporting windows, and the integration of intelligence systems with communications systems.

## 4. Mission Development — From Requirement to Tasking

Reconnaissance missions begin with the intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR) process. The commander states what he needs to know; the G-2/S-2 turns that into requirements; the SARCC tasks the assets.

- **Mission-type orders** are the norm: the reconnaissance leader is told the objective, the commander's intent, and the reporting requirements, and is given freedom in execution — because the ground and the enemy will change the plan.
- **Characteristics of ground reconnaissance missions:** stealth, depth, duration, and reporting discipline. A mission that is compromised is a mission that has failed even if it "succeeded" tactically.
- **The Mission Planning Folder (MPF)** is the mission's bible. It contains the operation order, the team-specific order, annexes, coordination checklists, and the confirmation brief. The manual details its development and contents (Appendix C gives the full structure). A team deploys with the folder, and the folder is the record of what was tasked, coordinated, and agreed.
- **Operations security (OPSEC):** personnel security, mission classification, and **isolation** — the period before deployment when the team is cut off from outside contact to protect the mission. Isolation is a security measure with a human cost; the manual treats it as a planning factor, not an afterthought.
- **Coordination:** with adjacent units, supporting arms, aviation, and the SARCC — done before insertion, because coordination after insertion is usually impossible.

## 5. Planning Overview — The Five Phases

The manual structures every reconnaissance mission in five phases:

1. **Planning phase** — arrange for reconnaissance, make reconnaissance, complete the plan, issue the order, supervise. (The manual's troop-leading steps, adapted to reconnaissance: the leader plans, reconnoiters, completes the plan, issues the order, and supervises — with the ROC and SARCC in the loop throughout.)
2. **Insertion and infiltration phase** — insertion is the delivery (air, sea, land); infiltration is the movement from the insertion point to the objective area. The two are planned separately and rehearsed separately.
3. **Execution phase — actions on the objective** — the team observes, reports, and, if tasked, acts (designating targets, conducting limited raids, or guiding follow-on forces).
4. **Exfiltration and extraction phase** — exfiltration is the team's own movement out; extraction is recovery by another asset (helicopter, vehicle, boat). Both are planned before insertion, with alternates.
5. **Recovery phase** — debrief and the **RAIDERREP** (see §8). Recovery is not administration: the debrief is where the mission's intelligence value is captured, and a bad debrief wastes a good mission.

## 6. Reconnaissance Operations — The Missions

The manual's core tactical content is the taxonomy of reconnaissance missions:

- **Area reconnaissance** — a detailed examination of a specific area: enemy, terrain, and obstacles within defined boundaries. Used when the commander needs to know everything about a piece of ground.
- **Zone reconnaissance** — a broader, less detailed examination of a larger area, usually to find the enemy or confirm his absence. Zone reconnaissance trades detail for coverage.
- **Route reconnaissance** — examination of a specific route: its trafficability, obstacles, enemy positions, and suitability for the main force's movement. The route reconnaissance report is a product the maneuver commander moves on.
- **Force-oriented reconnaissance** — focused on a specific enemy force rather than terrain: its composition, disposition, and activity.
- **Bridge reconnaissance** — a specialized mission: the condition, capacity, and enemy control of bridges — a chokepoint problem that can decide a maneuver.
- **Leader's reconnaissance** — the leader personally reconnoiters the objective or route before committing the force. Short, fast, and high-value.
- **Amphibious and underwater reconnaissance** — beach and hydrographic reconnaissance in support of amphibious operations: advance force operations, pre-assault operations (beach and obstacle reconnaissance), and post-assault operations. The manual covers planning considerations specific to the amphibious environment — surf, tides, beach gradients, and obstacles.
- **Riverine operations** — reconnaissance in river and littoral environments, with their own planning and environmental considerations.
- **Surveillance operations** — establishing observation posts and hide sites, and maintaining continuous observation of a named area of interest. The manual covers site selection, occupation, security, reporting, link-up, and contingencies. A surveillance mission is a marathon: the team must be sustainable, concealed, and disciplined for days.
- **Combat assessment** — reporting damage types and levels after fires: did the strike achieve its effect? The manual's damage-assessment appendix (Appendix G) gives the framework for specific targets.
- **Battlespace shaping** — reconnaissance missions that shape the battlespace for follow-on operations: deception, target development, and the preparation of the environment.
- **Hunter-killer operations** — reconnaissance paired with a strike element to find and destroy specific targets. The manual's principles of hunter-killer employment stress the reconnaissance element's role: find, fix, and hand off — the killer does the killing.
- **Urban terrain operations** — surveillance and reconnaissance in cities, with the KOCOA evaluation (key terrain, observation and fields of fire, cover and concealment, obstacles, avenues of approach), urban hide sites and observation posts, camouflage, communications, and the industrial/residential patterns that shape urban movement.
- **Specialized limited-scale raids and other offensive operations** — reconnaissance units can conduct raids, but the manual is explicit about capabilities and limitations: a reconnaissance unit that raids is a reconnaissance unit that is no longer reconnoitering, and the commander must accept that trade.
- **Special missions** — pathfinder operations, sniper operations, and stability and contingency operations.

## 7. Methods of Maneuver — Getting In, Moving, Getting Out

Insertion is the most dangerous phase of a reconnaissance mission: the team is at its most vulnerable, its signature is largest, and detection during insertion usually ends the mission.

- **Surface waterborne:** the Combat Rubber Reconnaissance Craft (CRRC) — crew responsibilities, tactical loads, sea conditions, surf limitations, distance, and navigation. The manual gives the training qualifications required, because waterborne insertion without qualification is a drowning, not a mission.
- **Combat swimming:** scout swimmers, qualification and capability certification, and the limits of what a swimmer can carry and how far he can go.
- **Helocast:** insertion by jumping from a helicopter into water. Safety personnel qualifications, considerations, and training requirements are detailed — helocast is a high-risk insertion method with a long safety tail.
- **Subsurface waterborne:** combatant diving — organization and duties, dive operations planning, equipment, underwater navigation, limited-visibility diving, contaminated-water diving, altitude diving, cold-weather diving, and closed- versus open-circuit operations. Submarine operations (debarkation, withdrawal) and proficiency/requalification requirements complete the section.
- **Helicopterborne:** missions, characteristics, the Special Patrol Insertion and Extraction System (SPIES), fast-rope insertion, rappelling, pickup and landing zones (selection and marking), and the planning factors for helicopter operations.
- **Airborne:** military free-fall, the multi-mission parachute system, tandem offset resupply, low-level static line, rates of descent, organization and duties, equipment, navigation, low-visibility operations, drop-zone surveys, and special-purpose drop zones (rough terrain, open sea, high altitude, cold weather).
- **Dismounted ground operations:** foot movement — planning considerations, contingencies, training and self-discipline, camouflage. Stay-behind operations (remaining in place as the enemy advances) get their own treatment.
- **Mounted ground operations:** capabilities, advantages (speed, firepower, sustainment) and disadvantages (signature, terrain limits) of mounted reconnaissance, planning considerations, vehicle preparation and inspections, fundamentals of movement (methods of travel, movement techniques and formations), camouflage, and mobility platforms.
- **Specialized ground operations:** military mountaineering, over-the-snow mobility, and pack animals — each a niche capability with its own training burden.

## 8. Communications — The Lifeline

Reconnaissance communications are covered in a full chapter because they are the mission's center of gravity:

- **Networks and architecture:** primary, alternate, and contingency nets; frequency management; the ROC's networks and the platoons' networks.
- **Radios:** HF, VHF, and UHF — each with its range, reliability, and signature characteristics. Elements of success: discipline, brevity, and redundancy.
- **MAGTF Secondary Imagery Dissemination System (MSIDS):** how imagery moves from the team to the commander.
- **Reports:** communication windows (the scheduled times a team transmits — and the discipline of transmitting only then, to limit enemy direction-finding), messages and reports, brevity words and prowords, report formats, and data file naming conventions.
- **Imagery collection and transmission:** imagery labels, gathering equipment, and the **objective sketch** — the reconnaissance team's hand-drawn product, with its types and the information it must contain. In an age of digital imagery, the manual still teaches the sketch because a sketch is what a team produces when its electronics fail.
- **Electronic warfare:** methods of prevention and operating procedures in an EW environment — the enemy will try to find, jam, and deceive reconnaissance communications, and the team must have a plan for each.
- **Geographic environments:** desert, jungle, cold weather, mountain, and urban — each changes propagation, equipment performance, and technique.

## 9. Intelligence Operations and Reporting Dissemination — The RAIDERREP

The final chapter closes the loop: reconnaissance exists to produce intelligence, and intelligence is worthless until it is reported, evaluated, and disseminated.

- **The intelligence cycle:** direction, collection, processing, dissemination — and the manual's principles of intelligence operations: intelligence is focused downward, intelligence drives operations, intelligence activities require centralized management, the G-2/S-2 facilitates use of intelligence, intelligence must be tailored and timely, and utilization is the final step of the cycle.
- **Intelligence functions:** support the commander's estimate, develop the situation, provide indications and warning, support force protection, support targeting, support combat assessment.
- **The commander's role:** focus the intelligence effort, participate in the intelligence process, use intelligence in decisionmaking, support the intelligence effort, and evaluate the results.
- **Requirements:** intelligence requirements (IRs) and **priority intelligence requirements (PIRs)** — the questions the commander must have answered to make decisions. PIRs have defined characteristics (they are tied to decisions, they are prioritized, and they drive collection).
- **Collection:** geospatial intelligence, human intelligence, signals intelligence, and measurement and signature intelligence — and the reconnaissance team's place in each.
- **Processing and production:** intelligence preparation of the battlespace (IPB) and the production of usable intelligence from raw reporting.
- **Evaluating intelligence:** relevance, reliability, accuracy — the three tests every report must pass.
- **Reporting and debriefing:** intelligence reporting levels, and the debrief — the structured post-mission interview that extracts everything the team saw, heard, and inferred.
- **The RAIDERREP** (reconnaissance, action, information, dissemination, and exploitation report): the mission's capstone product, produced when information satisfies collection requirements. It identifies the who, what, when, where, why, and how of the operation, organized by warfighting function. The RAIDERREP is the document that turns a patrol into intelligence.

## Output Contract

When asked about a reconnaissance task, answer with the manual's structure: (1) name the mission type (area/zone/route/force-oriented/surveillance/etc.); (2) identify the unit and the C2 chain (SARCC → ROC → team); (3) give the planning sequence (mission development → five phases → reporting); (4) state the insertion method and its limits, the communications plan, and the exfiltration plan; (5) name the reporting product (RAIDERREP, objective sketch, or Level I report). If the user asks for a reconnaissance plan, include the abort criteria and the reporting windows. Never recommend a reconnaissance unit accept a decisive engagement without flagging that it destroys the mission's purpose.

## Operating Rules

1. **Information is the product.** A reconnaissance unit that fights has stopped reconnoitering.
2. **Stealth is survivability.** Signature discipline — movement, noise, light, communications — is a tactical task, not a preference.
3. **Report on schedule, report completely.** A late report is a missed decision; an incomplete report is a guess.
4. **Plan the exfiltration before the insertion.** Both are planned before the team leaves, with alternates.
5. **Reconnaissance push or pull — decide before tasking.** The choice changes the mission.
6. **The debrief is the mission.** Capture everything, or the patrol's value dies with its memory.
