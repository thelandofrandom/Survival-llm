## The intelligence cycle

Direction → Collection → Processing → Dissemination — and back to direction. The reconnaissance team's place is collection; the G-2/S-2 owns direction and processing; dissemination is the shared responsibility that closes the loop.

## Principles of intelligence operations

1. Intelligence is focused downward — the lowest echelon that must act gets the intelligence first.
2. Intelligence drives operations — the plan follows what is known, not what is hoped.
3. Intelligence activities require centralized management — the SARCC and G-2/S-2 deconflict collection so assets are not wasted or duplicated.
4. The G-2/S-2 facilitates use of intelligence — the staff makes intelligence usable, not just available.
5. Intelligence must be tailored and timely — a perfect report that arrives after the decision is worthless.
6. Utilization is the final step of the cycle — intelligence that is not used is not intelligence, it is trivia.

## Intelligence functions

- Support the commander's estimate.
- Develop the situation.
- Provide indications and warning.
- Support force protection.
- Support targeting.
- Support combat assessment.

## PIRs and IRs

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intelligence requirement (IR) | A question the commander needs answered |
| Priority intelligence requirement (PIR) | An IR tied directly to a decision the commander must make — the highest-priority questions |

**PIR characteristics:** tied to a decision, prioritized against other PIRs, specific enough to drive collection, and time-sensitive. A PIR that does not drive a decision is not a PIR — it is a curiosity.

## Collection disciplines

| Discipline | What it provides | Reconnaissance role |
|---|---|---|
| Geospatial intelligence (GEOINT) | Imagery and mapping of the battlespace | Ground truth that confirms or corrects imagery |
| Human intelligence (HUMINT) | Information from people | The team's observations and any direct contact |
| Signals intelligence (SIGINT) | Enemy communications and emissions | Reporting of observed enemy communications activity |
| Measurement and signature intelligence (MASINT) | Signatures (acoustic, seismic, chemical, etc.) | Sensor employment and reporting |

## Evaluating intelligence — three tests

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Does this answer a requirement the commander actually has? |
| Reliability | Is the source trustworthy? |
| Accuracy | Is the information correct, confirmed by another source? |

A report that fails any test is flagged, not discarded — but it is never presented as fact.

## The RAIDERREP

The **RAIDERREP** (reconnaissance, action, information, dissemination, and exploitation report) is the mission's capstone product, produced when information satisfies collection requirements. It identifies the **who, what, when, where, why, and how** of the operation, organized by warfighting function. It serves as the extremely detailed record of the mission and the basis for follow-on tasking. Structure per the manual: mission identification, the reconnaissance findings by warfighting function, actions taken, information obtained, dissemination made, and exploitation recommended.

## Debrief checklist (Appendix J)

- [ ] Debrief conducted immediately on recovery, while memory is fresh
- [ ] Team leader and all team members present; recorder assigned
- [ ] Mission planning folder and RAIDERREP updated with debrief results
- [ ] Objective sketches, imagery, and captured documents/materials collected and catalogued
- [ ] Enemy order of battle, positions, and activity confirmed against the PIRs
- [ ] Terrain, obstacles, and route findings recorded for the next mission
- [ ] Lessons (what worked, what did not) recorded for the unit, not just the team
- [ ] Follow-on tasking identified before the team stands down

## Objective sketches

The reconnaissance team's hand-drawn product. Types include the hasty sketch (drawn under time pressure, key features only), the detailed sketch (measured, with grid references and scales), and the panoramic sketch (a wide view of the objective area). Every objective sketch must contain: orientation (north arrow and scale), the observer's position, the objective and its key features, distances and bearings, and the date-time group. A sketch is what a team produces when its electronics fail — and it is often more useful than a photograph, because it shows what the observer judged important.

## Communications — report discipline

- **Communication windows:** scheduled transmission times. Transmit only then, to limit enemy direction-finding.
- **Brevity words and prowords:** standardized voice procedure; every transmission short, formatted, and confirmed.
- **Report formats:** standardized formats for spot reports, situation reports, and intelligence reports — the receiver must be able to parse the report without asking questions.
- **Data file naming conventions:** standardized so imagery and reports can be found and fused later.
- **Primary, alternate, contingency:** every net has three means. A team with one radio has no communications plan — it has a hope.

## Electronic warfare procedures

- **Prevention:** emission control, directional antennas, low power, short transmissions, and frequency discipline.
- **Operating in an EW environment:** alternate frequencies, prearranged brevity codes, courier backup, and the discipline to go silent rather than transmit into a jam.
- **Geographic environments:** desert (long-range propagation, heat effects on equipment), jungle (attenuation, humidity), cold weather (battery failure, brittle materials), mountain (line-of-sight limits, reflections), urban (concrete attenuation, multipath).

## Mission planning folder (Appendix C) — contents

Operation order; team-specific operation order; annexes (intelligence, communications, logistics, fire support, medical); coordination checklists; confirmation brief. The folder is developed by the ROC with the team, and the team deploys with it. It is the record of what was tasked, coordinated, and agreed — and the starting point for the debrief.
