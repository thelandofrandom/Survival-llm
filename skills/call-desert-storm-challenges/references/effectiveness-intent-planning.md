# Effectiveness, Intent, and Planning — ARI Research Report 1633 (1993)

Distilled from Halpin and Keene, *Desert Storm Challenges: An Overview of Desert Storm Survey Responses*, ARI Research Report 1633, January 1993. Findings on 7-point scales unless noted.

## Effectiveness

Reconnaissance rated highest at 5.4 (334 respondents), then friendly indirect fire 5.2, deception 5.2, deep-operations interdiction of the second echelon 5.2, and close air support 5.1. Logistics rated 4.7 (377); counter-reconnaissance 4.3. The outlier was **FASCAM flank support at 2.5 (102 respondents, a majority rating it 1)** — the lowest-rated effectiveness item by a wide margin.

## Leadership

Officer leadership rated 5.7 (2,297 respondents); NCO leadership 5.5 (2,304). Leadership was the highest-rated area in the survey. EPW and refugee handling rated 5.1; personnel evacuation 4.9; Reserve units 4.6; replacements 4.5.

## Disruptive Factors

"Other" factors rated 5.0 (203) — the highest disruptive score. The chemical threat rated 3.6; staff sleep loss 3.5; unit sleep loss 3.3; command-post displacement 2.8; enemy indirect fire 2.3; direct fire 1.9; maneuver 1.7; deception 1.5. Enemy action disrupted operations far less than sleep loss, the chemical threat, and the miscellaneous "other" problems respondents wrote in.

## Task Difficulty

Lower scores mean harder. Dealing with uncertainty was hardest at 3.9; synchronizing the operation 4.6; integrating the staff 5.3; allocating time 5.3; visualizing the battlefield 5.3; deciding the course of action was easiest at 6.8. The hard part was not deciding — it was synchronizing and dealing with uncertainty.

## Commander's Intent

More than 1,500 respondents addressed commander's intent, and endorsed it strongly. Units operated from FRAGOs and intent, not detailed OPORDs — intent was called the "single most important aspect of the OPORD/FRAGO" and must be known at least two levels down. Some intent was vague or misinterpreted, but the consensus was that intent, not the plan, carried the operation.

## The Estimate Process

**84% (1,396 of 1,667) said the estimate process was adequate.** It was abbreviated by cutting IPB event templating, wargaming a single course of action, and having the commander and S-3 build the plan together; a fragmentary SOP followed the LD crossing. The report notes bluntly: "no process was used after combat operations initiated."

## Resources

Orders gave adequate time for 85% (1,717 staff respondents); sufficient time ran 79–82%. But sufficient assets ran only 65% overall — and **41% for CSS commanders** — and sufficient support 62% (49% for CSS). The gap between time and assets is the story: units had time to plan but not always the means to execute.

## Movement in Deep Operations

EPWs slowed the advance for 32% of combat commanders; supplies 31%; communications 25%; CSS units 24%. Enemy actions slowed only 7%. Weather was named by 15% of commenters. The advance was slowed by its own tail and its prisoners, not by the enemy. EPW handling was also the second-highest surprise category in the companion TR 970 (107 responses).

## Synchronization

Rehearsal and battle drills were the keys to synchronization. Problems: coordinating adjacent-unit fires, airspace deconfliction, returning through the breach, fuel, the speed mismatch between combat and combat-service units, and communications.

## Task Organization

Frequent arbitrary attachment and detachment was disruptive. Moving mobile CSS forward to the FSBs and forming logistics task forces were key; the separate brigade was called "ideal" except for its lack of mobile air-defense artillery.
