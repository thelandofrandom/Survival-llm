# Intelligence, Communications, Weapons, and Logistics — ARI Research Report 1633 (1993)

Distilled from Halpin and Keene, *Desert Storm Challenges: An Overview of Desert Storm Survey Responses*, ARI Research Report 1633, January 1993. Findings on 7-point scales unless noted.

## Intelligence — Timely Up, Slow Down

Intelligence flowed up far better than down: timely from lower to higher echelons 82–88%, but from higher to lower only 54–63%. Friendly information was timely 67–71%, accurate 74–75%, complete 59–66%. The speed of pursuit degraded timeliness and accuracy, and units often did not know where other friendly units were.

**IPB in detail.** Roughly 70% thought the intelligence received was adequate to prepare for combat operations; roughly 25% said it was not. 29% (705) commented on IPB, PIR, or other intelligence areas. The recurring problems: dissemination, particularly the flow from higher to lower; intelligence adequate for preparation but inadequate during execution; intelligence from higher that lacked the resolution and detail to be of use at lower echelons; overestimation of enemy strength, capability, and resolve; and incompleteness — inadequate intelligence on minefield and obstacle locations, enemy dispositions, enemy strength, and BDA. Combat Commanders rated the Situation Template 4.1 (245 responding). IPB product ratings (combat / CSS / staff): Terrain Analysis ~4.7 / 4.4 / 4.5; Battlefield Area Evaluation 4.5 / 4.2 / 4.4; Weather Analysis 4.2 / 3.9 / 4.1; Event Template 3.9 / 3.8 / 3.9; Decision Support Template 3.9 / 4.0 / 3.9. PIR supported the commander's intent for 90% of combat commanders (366), 75% of CSS commanders (87), and 89% of staff (1,366). Adequate intelligence to prepare for combat: 69% combat (401), 71% CSS (104), 72% staff (1,626). Respondent recommendations included getting someone on the ground to check the analysis once IPB is "done," orienting echelons above division on the needs of the tactical commander, and stopping the practice of holding information at corps and division — "pass it down, once the current battle starts."

## Communications — Age, Range, Quantity

1,812 respondents commented on communications; 2,016 (82%) responded to the general question or follow-ups. Top issues: the age of the equipment (e.g., the VRC-10), range, and insufficient quantity. Units scrounged radios, observed planned silence, used the PRC-57 as backup, and fell back on couriers, relays, signal flags, and retransmission stations. MSE drew mixed reviews; TACSAT was insufficient.

## The Air War

Air superiority enabled fast, unimpeded movement, continuous CAS in fair weather, and unimpeded logistics. It also let units ignore camouflage and digging — and helicopters served as command-and-control and relay platforms and lifted morale.

## Airspace Command and Control (A2C2) — Mixed

ATACMS clearance was too slow, the FSCL was misused, airspace control areas were not coordinated, and friendly helicopters flew through friendly lines during direct and indirect fire.

## Training

Rehearsals rated 6.1 (combat), 5.5 (CSS), 5.5 (staff); battle drills 6.0/5.6. Units ran full-up rehearsals with giant sand tables and terrain models, HMMWVs standing in for armor, and replicas of enemy positions — the most concrete preparation the survey measured, and the highest-rated training of any kind. Logistics rehearsals were added — but only one major logistics rehearsal/MAPEX took place before the attack, and the report flags that as a problem: the sustainment system that had to move the force was the least rehearsed part of it.

## Weapons

Organic weapons vs. APCs rated 6.0; vs. armor 5.6; vs. bunkers 5.7. The M1A1 and the Bradley 25mm were very effective against bunkers and point targets; DPICM against tanks; the .50-caliber against trenches; the TOW 2A extremely effective against armor. Notable failures: Cobra-launched TOW 2A repeated malfunctions; M1A1 optics; Cobra 20mm jamming. Mass fires were used by 76% at initiation, 66% in exploitation, 61% in pursuit — and field artillery could not keep up with M1A1 speed.

## Logistics

Support was adequate for ~75% (68% of combat commanders, 74% of staff); positioned correctly for 64–70%. PLL (prescribed load list) was adequate for only ~52–55%. Controlled substitutions ran 74–87%. TDA equipment was mostly modern electronics, computers, FAX, and navigation gear. Host-nation support was essential — HET (heavy equipment transport) most mentioned, plus food, fuel, water, and linguists. The hardest classes were fuel, water, and spare parts. Transport and the lack of a "push" supply system forced units to beg, borrow, and scrounge. The supply distribution system was the lowest-rated CSS item.

## Security and EOD

MP patrols covered the MSRs for 95% of respondents and rode with convoys for 78%. But MPs were sufficiently equipped for EPWs for only 59%, and handover problems ran 44%. EPW volume far exceeded the system's capacity, yet the report says MPs did a great job despite having no secure FM radios, no EPW transport, and too few MPs.

**EOD.** 389 comments addressed explosive ordnance disposal, and as with EPWs, many said the system was overwhelmed — though many more offered substantive fixes. Keeping EOD with the DISCOM appeared adequate during the defense; during the offense, EOD expertise was needed forward, and respondents recommended employing EOD as teams forward with engineer units. Units needed more EOD or EOD-qualified troops: the massive amount of unexploded USAF ordnance throughout Iraq put friendlies at risk, and the tremendous amount of unexploded ordnance on the battlefield was a danger to dismounts and wheeled vehicles. EOD after the war was effective in clearing, but engineers did most of the work; EOD likely did not receive satisfactory intelligence on a continuous basis until well after the offensive.

## Lessons-Learned Candidates

The most frequent candidate was GPS — the single most-named item in the entire survey. Others: the desert wedge formation; rehearsals; division-level breach doctrine for a complex obstacle; a Fire Support Element for the Aviation Squadron plus a forward air controller; fighting four simultaneous battles — close, deep, rear, and intermediate deep (10–30 km forward of the FLOT); informing NCOs; not using non-standard fire-support coordination measures; and not combining Regular Army with USAR/NG units.

## Conclusions (p. 55)

Commander's intent was strongly endorsed; rehearsals were emphasized; supply and distribution, personnel evacuation and reporting, communications, and intelligence flow were frequently mentioned as problem areas. The report closes by describing the data available — it does not itself draw the lessons.

## In Their Words — Respondent Comments

On intelligence: "The system is broken. We received more info from our own digging and borrowing from the British than we did from the U.S. system." (LTC, Div G-3) — and, from the other side: "Never in the history of warfare have commanders had such high quality accurate and timely intel." (MAJ, Div G-2 Ops) On dissemination: "Stop holding information at corps and division, pass it down, once the current battle starts. Brigade commanders should be allowed to fight it." (CPT, Btry Cdr) On EOD: "Keeping EOD with DISCOM appears adequate during the defense. During the offense, their expertise is needed forward." (MAJ, Div Asst Eng) — and "EOD were good and essential." (MAJ, Eng Bde S-3) These are survey comments, not findings — but they show the spread of opinion the averages hide.
