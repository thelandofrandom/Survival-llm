# CALL: Desert Storm Challenges — An Overview of Desert Storm Survey Responses

## Purpose

ARI Research Report 1633, *Desert Storm Challenges: An Overview of Desert Storm Survey Responses* (Stanley M. Halpin and S. Delane Keene, U.S. Army Research Institute, January 1993, 100 pages) — the companion to ARI Technical Report 970 on the combat training centers. It describes what 2,463 Desert Storm participants reported about the challenges of the war, organized by theme. It is explicitly **not lessons learned**: the report "provides a description of data available from which lessons learned may be derived," with more detailed analyses deferred. Read it as survey data — what commanders and staffs said they experienced — not as verified findings.

**The survey.** 10,000 surveys distributed (5,000+ in Saudi Arabia in April 1991, the rest in CONUS through July 1991); **2,463 usable returns**: 1,864 officers (52% captain, 21% major), 478 NCOs including 111 sergeants major, 58 warrant officers; 415 Combat Commander forms, 132 CSS Commander forms, 1,916 Staff forms; 55% at division level or lower, the rest corps and echelons above corps; 12 divisions and separate brigades, 62 corps and EAC elements. 43% had at least one NTC rotation, 36% JRTC, 39% CMTC, 39% BCTP. 400 had prior combat experience (226 Vietnam, 47 Grenada, 117 Panama, 20 other including Lebanon and the Korean DMZ). Findings are on 7-point scales unless noted.

## The Respondents

Who answered: 1,864 officers (52% captain, 21% major), 478 NCOs including 111 sergeants major, and 58 warrant officers. By form: 415 Combat Commander, 132 CSS Commander, 1,916 Staff. 55% served at division level or lower, the rest at corps and echelons above corps; the sample covers 12 divisions and separate brigades and 62 corps and EAC elements. 43% had at least one NTC rotation, 36% JRTC, 39% CMTC, 39% BCTP. 400 had prior combat experience: 226 Vietnam, 47 Grenada, 117 Panama, 20 other including Lebanon and the Korean DMZ.

**Limitations — read these first.** All responses carry equal weight regardless of rank, position, or experience, so a private's impression counts the same as a division commander's (hearsay risk). Some respondents filled the wrong form — combat commanders filled Staff forms and vice versa; one brigade commander returned both. There are no light/heavy, G-2/G-4, or brigade/EAC breakdowns, and opinions are not matched to actual battle experience. The report describes the data; it does not verify it.

**Companion report.** This is the companion to ARI Technical Report 970, *How Well Did the Combat Training Centers Prepare Units for Combat?* (see the `call-combat-training-centers-desert-storm` skill), which asked the same population what surprises their CTC experience had not prepared them for. The two reports share the survey: 10,000 questionnaires, 2,463 returns.

## 1. Effectiveness

Reconnaissance rated highest at 5.4 (334 respondents), then friendly indirect fire 5.2, deception 5.2, deep-operations interdiction of the second echelon 5.2, and close air support 5.1. Logistics rated 4.7 (377); counter-reconnaissance 4.3. The outlier was **FASCAM flank support at 2.5 (102 respondents, a majority rating it 1)** — the lowest-rated effectiveness item by a wide margin. Ratings are 7-point means; the n varies by item because not every respondent answered every question.

## 2. Leadership

Officer leadership rated 5.7 (2,297 respondents); NCO leadership 5.5 (2,304). Leadership was the highest-rated area in the survey. EPW and refugee handling rated 5.1; personnel evacuation 4.9; Reserve units 4.6; replacements 4.5.

## 3. Disruptive Factors

"Other" factors rated 5.0 (203) — the highest disruptive score. The chemical threat rated 3.6; staff sleep loss 3.5; unit sleep loss 3.3; command-post displacement 2.8; enemy indirect fire 2.3; direct fire 1.9; maneuver 1.7; deception 1.5. Enemy action, in other words, disrupted operations far less than sleep loss, the chemical threat, and the miscellaneous "other" problems respondents wrote in.

## 4. Task Difficulty

Lower scores mean harder. Dealing with uncertainty was hardest at 3.9; synchronizing the operation 4.6; integrating the staff 5.3; allocating time 5.3; visualizing the battlefield 5.3; deciding the course of action was easiest at 6.8. The hard part was not deciding — it was synchronizing and dealing with uncertainty.

## 5. Commander's Intent — the Single Most Important Element

More than 1,500 respondents addressed commander's intent, and endorsed it strongly. Units operated from FRAGOs and intent, not detailed OPORDs — intent was called the "single most important aspect of the OPORD/FRAGO" and must be known at least two levels down. Some intent was vague or misinterpreted, but the consensus was that intent, not the plan, carried the operation.

## 6. The Estimate Process — Abbreviated, and Adequate

**84% (1,396 of 1,667) said the estimate process was adequate.** It was abbreviated by cutting IPB event templating, wargaming a single course of action, and having the commander and S-3 build the plan together; a fragmentary SOP followed the LD crossing. The report notes bluntly: "no process was used after combat operations initiated." → `references/survey-and-methods.md`

## 7. Resources — Time Was Enough; Assets Were Not

Orders gave adequate time for 85% (1,717 staff respondents); sufficient time ran 79–82%. But sufficient assets ran only 65% overall — and **41% for CSS commanders** — and sufficient support 62% (49% for CSS). The gap between time and assets is the story: units had time to plan but not always the means to execute.

## 8. Movement in Deep Operations — What Slowed the Advance

EPWs slowed the advance for 32% of combat commanders; supplies 31%; communications 25%; CSS units 24%. Enemy actions slowed only 7%. Weather was named by 15% of commenters. The advance was slowed by its own tail and its prisoners, not by the enemy. EPW handling was also the second-highest surprise category in the companion TR 970 (107 responses) — the two surveys agree that prisoners, not the enemy, were the friction.

## 9. Synchronization

Rehearsal and battle drills were the keys to synchronization. Problems: coordinating adjacent-unit fires, airspace deconfliction, returning through the breach, fuel, the speed mismatch between combat and combat-service units, and communications.

## 10. Task Organization

Frequent arbitrary attachment and detachment was disruptive. Moving mobile CSS forward to the FSBs and forming logistics task forces were key; the separate brigade was called "ideal" except for its lack of mobile air-defense artillery.

## 11. Intelligence — Timely Up, Slow Down

Intelligence flowed up far better than down: timely from lower to higher echelons 82–88%, but from higher to lower only 54–63%. Friendly information was timely 67–71%, accurate 74–75%, complete 59–66%. The speed of pursuit degraded timeliness and accuracy, and units often did not know where other friendly units were.

**IPB in detail.** Roughly 70% thought the intelligence received was adequate to prepare for combat operations; roughly 25% said it was not. 29% (705) commented on IPB, PIR, or other intelligence areas. The recurring problems: dissemination, particularly the flow from higher to lower; intelligence adequate for preparation but inadequate during execution; intelligence from higher that lacked the resolution and detail to be of use at lower echelons; overestimation of enemy strength, capability, and resolve; and incompleteness — inadequate intelligence on minefield and obstacle locations, enemy dispositions, enemy strength, and BDA. Combat Commanders rated the Situation Template 4.1 (245 responding). IPB product ratings (combat / CSS / staff): Terrain Analysis ~4.7 / 4.4 / 4.5; Battlefield Area Evaluation 4.5 / 4.2 / 4.4; Weather Analysis 4.2 / 3.9 / 4.1; Event Template 3.9 / 3.8 / 3.9; Decision Support Template 3.9 / 4.0 / 3.9. PIR supported the commander's intent for 90% of combat commanders (366), 75% of CSS commanders (87), and 89% of staff (1,366). Adequate intelligence to prepare for combat: 69% combat (401), 71% CSS (104), 72% staff (1,626). Respondent recommendations included getting someone on the ground to check the analysis once IPB is "done," orienting echelons above division on the needs of the tactical commander, and stopping the practice of holding information at corps and division — "pass it down, once the current battle starts." → `references/intel-comms-weapons-logistics.md`

## 12. Communications — Age, Range, Quantity

1,812 respondents commented on communications; 2,016 (82%) responded to the general question or follow-ups. Top issues: the age of the equipment (e.g., the VRC-10), range, and insufficient quantity. Units scrounged radios, observed planned silence, used the PRC-57 as backup, and fell back on couriers, relays, signal flags, and retransmission stations. MSE drew mixed reviews; TACSAT was insufficient.

## 13. The Air War

Air superiority enabled fast, unimpeded movement, continuous CAS in fair weather, and unimpeded logistics. It also let units ignore camouflage and digging — and helicopters served as command-and-control and relay platforms and lifted morale.

## 14. Airspace Command and Control (A2C2) — Mixed

A2C2 drew mixed reviews: ATACMS clearance was too slow, the FSCL was misused, airspace control areas were not coordinated, and friendly helicopters flew through friendly lines during direct and indirect fire.

## 15. Training — Rehearsals Were the Highest-Rated Training

Rehearsals rated 6.1 (combat), 5.5 (CSS), 5.5 (staff); battle drills 6.0/5.6. Units ran full-up rehearsals with giant sand tables and terrain models, HMMWVs standing in for armor, and replicas of enemy positions — the most concrete preparation the survey measured, and the highest-rated training of any kind. Logistics rehearsals were added — but only one major logistics rehearsal/MAPEX took place before the attack, and the report flags that as a problem: the sustainment system that had to move the force was the least rehearsed part of it.

## 16. Weapons — Effective, With Notable Failures

Organic weapons vs. APCs rated 6.0; vs. armor 5.6; vs. bunkers 5.7. The M1A1 and the Bradley 25mm were very effective against bunkers and point targets; DPICM against tanks; the .50-caliber against trenches; the TOW 2A extremely effective against armor. Notable failures: Cobra-launched TOW 2A repeated malfunctions; M1A1 optics; Cobra 20mm jamming. Mass fires were used by 76% at initiation, 66% in exploitation, 61% in pursuit — and field artillery could not keep up with M1A1 speed.

## 17. Logistics — Adequate, Barely, and Only With Help

Support was adequate for ~75% (68% of combat commanders, 74% of staff); positioned correctly for 64–70%. PLL (prescribed load list) was adequate for only ~52–55%. Controlled substitutions ran 74–87%. TDA equipment was mostly modern electronics, computers, FAX, and navigation gear. Host-nation support was essential — HET (heavy equipment transport) most mentioned, plus food, fuel, water, and linguists. The hardest classes were fuel, water, and spare parts. Transport and the lack of a "push" supply system forced units to beg, borrow, and scrounge. The supply distribution system was the lowest-rated CSS item. → `references/intel-comms-weapons-logistics.md`

## 18. Security — MPs Did the Job Without the Tools

MP patrols covered the MSRs for 95% of respondents and rode with convoys for 78%. But MPs were sufficiently equipped for EPWs for only 59%, and handover problems ran 44%. EPW volume far exceeded the system's capacity, yet the report says MPs did a great job despite having no secure FM radios, no EPW transport, and too few MPs.

**EOD.** 389 comments addressed explosive ordnance disposal, and as with EPWs, many said the system was overwhelmed — though many more offered substantive fixes. Keeping EOD with the DISCOM appeared adequate during the defense; during the offense, EOD expertise was needed forward, and respondents recommended employing EOD as teams forward with engineer units. Units needed more EOD or EOD-qualified troops: the massive amount of unexploded USAF ordnance throughout Iraq put friendlies at risk, and the tremendous amount of unexploded ordnance on the battlefield was a danger to dismounts and wheeled vehicles. EOD after the war was effective in clearing, but engineers did most of the work; EOD likely did not receive satisfactory intelligence on a continuous basis until well after the offensive.

## 19. Lessons-Learned Candidates

The most frequent candidate was GPS — the single most-named item in the entire survey. Others: the desert wedge formation; rehearsals; division-level breach doctrine for a complex obstacle; a Fire Support Element for the Aviation Squadron plus a forward air controller; fighting four simultaneous battles — close, deep, rear, and intermediate deep (10–30 km forward of the FLOT); informing NCOs; not using non-standard fire-support coordination measures; and not combining Regular Army with USAR/NG units.

## 20. Conclusions (p. 55)

The report's conclusions: commander's intent was strongly endorsed; rehearsals were emphasized; supply and distribution, personnel evacuation and reporting, communications, and intelligence flow were frequently mentioned as problem areas. The report closes by describing the data available — it does not itself draw the lessons. Its last word is a description of the data, not a prescription: the lessons are for the reader to derive.

## 21. In Their Words — Respondent Comments

The report's value is partly in the respondents' own words. On intelligence: "The system is broken. We received more info from our own digging and borrowing from the British than we did from the U.S. system." (LTC, Div G-3) — and, from the other side: "Never in the history of warfare have commanders had such high quality accurate and timely intel." (MAJ, Div G-2 Ops) On dissemination: "Stop holding information at corps and division, pass it down, once the current battle starts. Brigade commanders should be allowed to fight it." (CPT, Btry Cdr) On EOD: "Keeping EOD with DISCOM appears adequate during the defense. During the offense, their expertise is needed forward." (MAJ, Div Asst Eng) — and "EOD were good and essential." (MAJ, Eng Bde S-3) These are survey comments, not findings — but they show the spread of opinion the averages hide.

## Output Contract

When answering a question from this report, give the finding first with its numbers (respondent counts, percentages, 7-point ratings), then the theme it belongs to, then the caveat: this is survey data describing what participants reported, explicitly not lessons learned, with all responses weighted equally regardless of rank or experience. Cite the section and the scale. Where a finding touches current doctrine or equipment (GPS, FASCAM, IFF, EPW handling, MSE), say what the report recorded and note that current practice may differ.

## Operating Rules

1. **Survey data, not lessons.** Report what 2,463 respondents said; never present a survey response as a verified lesson learned.
2. **Numbers are the evidence.** Give counts, percentages, and ratings — a finding without its n is a rumor.
3. **Weight the caveats.** Equal weighting, wrong-form returns, no breakdowns, no matching to battle experience — say so when a finding is thin.
4. **Intent carried the operation.** Commander's intent, FRAGOs, and rehearsals are the report's strongest positive themes; FASCAM flank support (2.5), communications, and supply distribution are its weakest.
5. **The tail slowed the advance.** EPWs, supplies, comms, and CSS — not enemy action (7%) — were what slowed deep operations.
6. **Check the n before quoting a rating.** Every rating in this report carries a respondent count; a 5.4 from 334 people and a 2.5 from 102 are not the same kind of evidence.
