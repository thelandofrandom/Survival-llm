# FM 3-97.61 Military Mountaineering (2002)

## Purpose

FM 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 26 August 2002, 328 pages; originally published as TC 90-6-1) is the Army's 2002 mountaineering manual — the full course in moving, fighting, and surviving in mountains, from reading the rock and the weather through knots, anchors, climbing, rope installations, snow and ice, glaciers, and rescue. It is written for the small-unit leader and the mountaineer, in 11 chapters and 3 appendices.

**Supersession note — read this first.** This 2002 edition is superseded. The current edition is **TC 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (November 2025)**, already a skill in this vault as `military-mountaineering` — it supersedes the 2012 edition, which in turn replaced this 2002 manual. Where the two disagree on a procedure, a rating, or a piece of equipment, follow TC 3-97.61 (2025). The 2002 manual remains valuable for four reasons: its weather chapter (cloud types, fronts, forecasting, and the weather-recording routine) is more detailed than the current edition's; its rock classification and route classification sections are fuller; its knot chapter teaches 26 knots with step-by-step tying instructions and checkpoints; and its glacier and crevasse-rescue treatment, avalanche chapter, and time-distance formulas are the most complete in the vault for planning mountain movement. Use the 2025 edition as the authority and the 2002 edition as the deep field reference.

## 1. Mountain Terrain, Weather, and Hazards
**1.1. Reading the rock.** Rock type predicts hazard.

**1.2. Route classification.** In North America the **Yosemite Decimal System (YDS)** rates difficulty: Class 1, hiking trail; Class 2, off-trail scramble; Class 3, climbing, ropes for beginners (moderate scrambling); Class 4, belayed climbing (moderate to difficult scrambling, some exposure); Class 5, free climbing — roped up, belay, intermediate protection. Class 5 subdivides from 5.0–5.4 (little difficulty, advanced rock scrambling) through 5.5 (moderate, three points of contact), 5.6 (medium, vertical or overhangs), 5.7 (great difficulty, considerable experience), 5.8 (very great difficulty), 5.9 (extremely great difficulty, exposed positions), 5.10 (extraordinary difficulty, acrobatic technique and refined security technique), to 5.11–5.14 (greater increases, only talented and dedicated climbers). Classes from 5.10 up take a/b/c/d subdivisions; 5.9 and below take +/−. Aid climbing grades run **A0** (French-free — using gear to pass one or two moves) through **A1** (easy aid, reliable placements), **A2** (moderate, awkward placements, no serious fall danger), **A3** (hard aid — test every placement, falls of 40–60 feet possible), **A4** (serious aid — placements hold body weight only, falls up to two-thirds of a rope length), **A5** (extreme aid — no placement can be trusted to stop a fall), to **A6** (extremely severe — continuous A5 with A5 belays; if the leader falls, the whole team probably falls).

**1.3. Cross-country movement and the tactical problem.** Movement is often restricted by terrain and weather; erratic weather can halt a unit that is otherwise ready.

**1.4. Mountain air and weather.** Pressure is low and drops with altitude; the air is thinner, drier, and filters less ultraviolet radiation. The jet stream can exceed 100 knots at altitude. Wind force **quadruples each time wind speed doubles**. Valley winds and the foehn — warm, dry wind descending the lee side — are peculiar to mountains. The **adiabatic lapse rate** is the rate air cools as it rises (and warms as it descends). **Avalanche hazards:** slope stability is the key factor; triggers include new snow, wind loading, temperature change, and the climber himself; snow pits reveal layering and weak layers. Two types: loose-snow and slab (a cohesive layer breaking away over a weak layer). Cloud levels: low, 0–6,500 feet (cumulus, stratus); mid, 6,500–20,000 feet (altocumulus, altostratus, with the prefix "alto"); high, above 20,000 feet (cirrus, cirrostratus, cirrocumulus); vertical-development clouds (cumulonimbus) can grow to 40,000 feet or more and bring thunderstorms. Temperature: for each **100-meter rise in altitude, temperature drops approximately 1°C**; above approximately **8,000 feet**, snow can be expected any time of year; on cold, clear, calm mornings, temperature inversions trap cold air in valleys.

**1.5. Weather forecasting and recording.** A forecast must reach the small-unit leaders who will act on it, and it must be for the elevation they will occupy.

**1.6. Hazards.** Subjective hazards (human): falling (carelessness, over-fatigue, heavy equipment), bivouac site selection, equipment failure — ropes are not total security; they can be cut on a sharp edge or break. Objective hazards (terrain): altitude (above **6,500 feet** endurance and judgment fall off), visibility (fog, rain, darkness, blowing snow), gullies (rock, snow, and debris channel down them), rockfall (blocks and scree at the base of a climb mark recurring rockfall), avalanches, hanging glaciers and seracs (avoid if at all possible), crevasses.
## 2. Mountain Living — Water, Food, Altitude
**2.1. Water.** Mountain water should never be assumed safe. About 75 percent of the human body is liquid. A loss of two quarts of body fluid (2.5 percent of body weight) decreases physical efficiency by 25 percent; A loss of 12 quarts (15 percent of body weight) is usually fatal. Drink **one quart every four hours** as a minimum, more during rigorous activity; **three to six quarts per day** in total.

**2.2. Nutrition.** Three major food components are required: carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.

**2.3. Hygiene and sanitation.** Personal hygiene matters more, not less, in the high mountains — infection spreads fast in a cold, tired unit.

**2.4. Acclimatization and altitude illness.** A person is acclimatized when he can perform physically and mentally at altitude. The process begins immediately on arrival; disappearance of acute mountain sickness (AMS) symptoms in four to seven days does **not** mean complete acclimatization — adjustment continues for weeks or months, and complete acclimatization is possible to about **14,000 feet** for most soldiers with proper ascent, nutrition, and activity. Rapid ascent produces symptoms in a predictable pattern: to 6,000 feet in under 24 hours, 10–20 percent get mild symptoms; to 10,000 feet, 75 percent; to 12,000–14,000 feet, over 50 percent moderate and 12–18 percent severe; to 17,500 feet, severe, incapacitating symptoms in almost all. Vigorous activity during ascent or within the first 24 hours increases incidence and severity. Behavioral effects in the unacclimatized: errors in simple mental tasks, lost concentration and memory, lethargy, irritability, impaired night vision and up to 30 percent constriction of peripheral vision at 6,000 feet, loss of appetite, sleep disturbance, irregular breathing, slurred speech, headache. **Judgment and self-evaluation are impaired the same as in a person who is intoxicated.** A soldier who cannot walk a straight line, loses balance, or has an incapacitating headache should be evacuated to a lower altitude — a descent of at least **1,000 feet for at least 24 hours**.

**2.5. AMS, HAPE, HACE.** **Acute mountain sickness:** headache is the most noticeable symptom and may be severe; treatment is rest, fluids, and no further ascent — if untreated or if ascent continues, AMS can progress. **High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE):** fluid filling the lungs, caused by rapid ascent, encountered above **8,000 feet**, can occur in healthy soldiers and can kill. Symptoms: progressive dry cough with frothy white or pink sputum, later blood; cyanosis (blue face, hands, feet); labored breathing, dizziness, fainting; progressive shortness of breath, pulse 120–160; crackling, cellophane-like noises (rales) in the lungs; unconsciousness and death if untreated. Prevention: good nutrition, hydration, and gradual ascent — **no more than 1,000–2,000 feet per day to the sleeping altitude**, with a rest day (no gain in altitude, no heavy exertion) for every 3,000 feet gained. Treatment: **immediate descent of 2,000–3,000 feet minimum** if possible (a descent of 300 meters may help; never delay manual descent to await air evacuation), rest as a litter patient, supplemental oxygen if available, and evacuation to lower altitude. **High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE):** fluid in the brain from rapid ascent above 8,000 feet — mild personality changes, paralysis, stupor, loss of coordination; prevention is the same gradual ascent; treatment is immediate descent and evacuation.

**2.6. Cold, heat, and solar injuries.** Cold injuries divide into freezing (frostbite) and nonfreezing (trench foot, immersion foot).
## 3. Mountaineering Equipment
**3.1. Footwear and clothing.** The hot-weather boot is the all-round platform; extreme cold needs mission-specific footwear (Level 2 and 3 mountaineers). Clothing is a layering system (ECWCS): socks are the most under-appreciated item — keep feet dry, change socks, never wear cotton against the skin; underwear must move moisture away from the body; insulating layers trap air; outer layers shed wind and water; gaiters protect the lower leg from snow and ice; handwear must balance warmth against the dexterity to work rope and weapons; headwear matters — about **25 percent of heat loss is through the head**; the Kevlar ballistic helmet works for most basic mountaineering; eyewear must protect against glare and snowblindness — the supply system does not currently provide adequate eye protection, so units must source it.

**3.2. Climbing software (rope and webbing).** Ropes and cords are the most important pieces of mountaineering equipment.

**3.3. Climbing hardware.** Carabiners — the most versatile piece of equipment.

**3.4. Snow and ice hardware.** Ice ax — one of the most important tools for the mountaineer (walking aid, self-arrest, step cutting, anchor).

**3.5. Sustainability equipment.** Snow saw, snow shovel, wands (mark routes, crevasses, snow bridges, caches), avalanche rescue equipment (beacon, probe, shovel), packs, stoves (define the purpose before choosing — melting snow for water is the primary mountain stove task), tents (define the mission before choosing), skis (wide and short, pivoting binding), snowshoes (the traditional aid to snow travel), ski poles, sleds (squad-size Ahkio and others), headlamps.

**3.6. Choice of equipment and packing.** Personal gear includes an emergency survival kit; standard gear is what can be worn or carried individually; mountaineering and specialized gear is mission-specific; a day pack covers a day away from the bivouac; a squad or team safety pack covers a squad-sized element. **The Ten Essentials** — regardless of what else is carried, the individual always carries them (navigation, sun protection, insulation, illumination, first-aid, fire, repair kit, nutrition, hydration, shelter).
## 4. Rope Management and Knots
**4.1. Preparation, care, inspection.** New rope comes coiled or on a spool; cut it to length and whip or melt the ends. Care: do not step on or drag ropes; do not let rope contact sharp edges (nylon cuts easily); keep rope as dry as possible (if wet, hang in large loops above the ground); never leave rope knotted or tightly stretched longer than necessary; never let one rope rub continuously against another; inspect before each use for frayed or cut spots, mildew, rot, or defects; whip or melt ends to prevent unraveling; **do not splice ropes for mountaineering**; do not mark ropes with paints or let them contact oils or chemicals; never use a mountaineering rope for any other purpose; each rope has a corresponding rope log (**DA Form 5752-R, Rope History**); never subject rope to high heat or flame; wash periodically to remove dirt and grit; ultraviolet radiation deteriorates nylon over long periods; coil loosely and hang on wooden pegs rather than nails.

**4.2. Terminology.** Bight (bend, rope does not cross itself); loop (bend, rope crosses itself); half hitch (loop binding on itself around an object); turn (360-degree contact around an object); round turn (one and one-half wraps); running end (loose, working end); standing part (static, nonworking end); lay (direction of twist); pigtail (the portion of the running end between the knot and the end of the rope); dress (proper arrangement of all knot parts, removing twists and crossed parts).

**4.3. Coiling and throwing.** Mountain coil (grasp the rope about 1 meter from the end and coil in large loops over the shoulder or pack — the carry coil for long ropes).

**4.4. The knots — 26 taught with tying steps and checkpoints.** Square knot; fisherman's knot; double fisherman's knot; figure-eight bend; water knot; bowline; round turn and two half hitches; figure-eight retrace (rerouted figure-eight); clove hitch; wireman's knot; directional figure-eight; bowline-on-a-bight (two-loop bowline); two-loop figure-eight; figure-eight loop (figure-eight-on-a-bight); Prusik knot; Bachman knot; bowline-on-a-coil; three-loop bowline; figure-eight slip knot; transport knot (overhand slip knot / mule knot — for tying off a tensioned system so it can be released); Kleimhiest knot; frost knot; girth hitch; Munter hitch; rappel seat; guarde knot.
## 5. Anchors
**5.1. Natural anchors.** Trees (rocky-terrain trees have shallow roots — check; pad soft, sap-producing trees to keep sap off ropes and slings).

**5.2. Slinging techniques.** Drape (sling over the anchor — simplest, but the sling can slip off); wrap (sling wrapped around the anchor and the ends connected — more secure); girth (sling girth-hitched around the anchor — the hitch tightens under load, but reduces the sling's strength).

**5.3. Deadman.** An object with a large surface area and length (a log, a pack filled with snow, a buried ski) buried in snow or earth; in extremely hard, rocky terrain where digging is impractical, build the deadman above ground and weight it.

**5.4. Artificial anchors.** Pitons: advantages (strong in the right crack, fast to place once the crack is read) and disadvantages (noisy, slow to place well, damage rock, hard to remove); placement is critical — the piton must match the crack's shape and be driven until it rings solid; test by pulling up about 1 meter of slack in the climbing rope or by bouncing on it; attach a carabiner and sling before removal so a freed piton cannot fall; soft iron pitons that have been used, removed, and straightened may be reused once, then discarded.

**5.5. Equalizing anchors.** Self-equalizing anchor (maintains an equal load on each piece as the direction of pull changes — built with a sliding sling); pre-equalized anchor (distributes the load equally to each piece for a known direction of pull — built with fixed-length slings tied to a master point).
## 6. Climbing
**6.1. Route selection and preparation.** Select the route from the ground before anyone climbs — read the rock, the hazards, and the line of the rope.

**6.2. Climbing technique.** Five body parts: right hand, left hand, right foot, left foot, and the body itself as a counterweight.

**6.3. Using the feet and hands.** The beginner looks up for handholds; the good climber looks down for footholds.

**6.4. Down climbing.** Down climbing can inadvertently lead into terrain harder than the ascent; it is accomplished at a difficulty level above the climber's up-climbing ability in reverse — face the rock, test every hold, and never rush.

**6.5. Tying in and harnesses.** Tie into the climbing rope with a figure-eight retrace through the harness tie-in point. Improvised harnesses: swami belt (webbing wrapped around the waist); bowline-on-a-coil (the traditional method — the rope wrapped 4–6 times around the waist and tied with a bowline); improvised seat harness (webbing); improvised chest harness (rope or webbing); improvised full-body harness (chest and seat combined).

**6.6. Belaying.** The belayer must perform three functions while maintaining total control of the rope: pay out rope as the climber advances, take in rope as the climber descends or rests, and brake a fall.

**6.7. Commands.** Verbal commands (belayer and climber, with the meaning and action for each): "On belay" / "Belay on" (the belayer is ready); "Climbing" / "Climb" (the climber is starting); "Slack" (give rope); "Tension" / "Up rope" (take in rope); "Falling" (the climber is falling — brake now); "Rock" (falling object — protect yourself); "Off belay" (the climber is safe — the belayer may come off belay).
## 7. Rope Installations
**7.1. Fixed rope with intermediate anchors.** Two experienced climbers prepare for a roped climb; the leader climbs and places intermediate anchors as he goes; the rope is fixed at the top and at each intermediate anchor so the rest of the unit can move on it with Prusiks or ascenders.

**7.2. Rappel point.** Selection: anchor quality, a route free of obstacles, and loading/unloading platforms.

**7.3. Types of rappels.** Hasty rappel — moderate pitches only, fast, no belay from below.

**7.4. One-rope bridge.** Site selection: anchors on both sides, a crossing the unit can use.

**7.5. A-frame and vertical hauling line.** Even in wooded mountainous terrain, constructing an A-frame may be necessary to gain height. Vertical hauling line installation: maintain a 12-inch bight in the hauling line; tie clove hitches above the A-frame lashing; anchor the A-frame with a transport tightening system using the doubled rope; tie the ends of another installation rope together with a joining knot to form the hauling loop; attach equipment to the hauling line 12 inches above the joining knot; add fixed loops in the hauling line for more control; when personnel are moved, make a knotted hand line; use as many men as needed to pull the load to the top.

**7.6. Z-pulley and U-pulley systems.** Anchors must be sturdy and able to support the load.
## 8. Mountain Walking Techniques
**8.1. Basic principles.** The soldier's weight is centered directly over the feet at all times; he places his foot flat and deliberately. The **rest step** is used for steep slopes, snowfields, and higher elevations: lock the downhill leg straight, rest the weight on the skeleton for a beat, then step.

**8.2. Techniques by ground.** Hard ground (firmly compacted, rocky soil): flat foot, deliberate placement.

**8.3. Safety.** Whenever a rock is kicked loose, shout **"Rock!"** immediately.

**8.4. Navigation.** Compasses: the magnetic compass is the simplest and most widely used navigational aid in the mountains.

**8.5. Route planning and selection.** Map reconnaissance is the primary source of route information. **Time-distance formulas** (Table 8-1, for individuals carrying a rifle and loaded rucksack, flat or gently rolling terrain): on foot with no snow cover, 2–3 kph cross-country (unbroken trail) and 3–4 kph on a trail (broken trail); on foot with less than 1 foot of snow, 1.6–3.2 kph unbroken and 2–3.2 kph broken; on foot with more than 1 foot of snow, 0.4–1.2 kph unbroken and 2–3.2 kph broken; snowshoeing, 1.6–3.2 kph unbroken and 3.2–4 kph broken; skiing, 1–5.6 kph unbroken and 4.8–5.6 kph broken; skijoring, 3–24 kph broken trail. March distances in mountains are often measured in time rather than distance: measure the map distance, add **one-third** for a good estimate of actual ground distance, then add **one hour for each 1,000 feet of ascent or 2,000 feet of descent**. Combined soldier loads exceeding **50 pounds per man** slow movement significantly in mountains — and with ammunition, mountaineering gear, and cold-weather clothing, soldiers will be carrying well in excess of that.
## 9. Mountain Stream Crossing
**9.1. Reconnaissance.** Site selection is critical once you determine you must make a wet crossing.

**9.2. Preparation of troops and equipment.** Waterproof water-sensitive items — radios, binoculars, SOI, papers, maps.

**9.3. Individual crossings.** Face upstream and slightly sideways, leaning into the current; shuffle the feet along the bottom rather than lifting them (a lifted foot is a foot the current can take); cross at a slight downstream angle so as not to fight the current directly; constantly feel for obstacles, holes, and drop-offs with the feet; use a long ice ax, sturdy tree limb, or other staff for balance — a third point of contact.

**9.4. Team crossing.** A team crosses in mutual support — the strongest members upstream, the weaker sheltered behind them, arms linked or holding a rope. **Rope installations:** establishing the far anchor (the far anchor goes in first, by a strong swimmer or a thrown line); installation of a handline (if a rope can be placed high enough above the water, soldiers cross holding the rope without being swept off their feet). Safety: identify all weak and nonswimmers before the crossing so stronger swimmers can assist them; not every river or stream can be crossed safely — if the installation becomes too difficult, move to another site; the safest methods are always with a handline or one-rope bridge; a lookout is posted **50 to 100 meters upstream** to watch for obstacles and debris (and to warn of anything floating down toward the crossing); when the unit knows a rope installation will be required, at least two soldiers should be trained to build it.

**9.5. Swimming.** Immediately jettison any equipment or clothing that restricts movement; do not fight the current — maneuver toward shore feet-first, head above water, watching for obstacles; avoid backwater eddies and converging currents (they contain debris and can trap a swimmer); if the shore is too difficult to reach, seek the closest and safest spot, such as a sandbar or eddy.
## 10. Movement Over Snow and Ice
**10.1. Movement over snow and ice.** The progression from walking on flat terrain to moving on steep terrain is gradual — learn each step before the next.

**10.2. Ice ax and crampons.** The ice ax is a third point of contact when walking on snow or ice — the adze for cutting steps, the pick for arresting a fall, the shaft for probing. When a climb on ice reaches a **60- to 70-degree angle**, two ice tools are needed (one in each hand). **Step cutting** is an extremely valuable technique and a required skill: the adze cuts a step the boot can stand on, one step at a time.

**10.3. Self-arrest.** The large number of climbers injured or killed while sliding on snow makes self-arrest a survival skill, not a drill.

**10.4. Glissading.** A controlled slide down a snow slope.

**10.5. Snow and ice anchors.** Ice pitons (driven into ice — not as secure as an ice screw, and they loosen as the ice melts around them).

**10.6. Roped climbing on ice and snow.** Tie-in is normally to the harness with a figure-eight retrace.

**10.7. Movement on glaciers.** Characteristics and definitions: glaciers flow, crevasses form where the ice flows over a slope or around an obstacle, snow bridges span crevasses, seracs are blocks of ice that can fall without warning. **Crevasse rescue techniques:** build a solid snow anchor, transfer the load from the fallen climber to the anchor, and raise the climber — with a **3-to-1** system the load rises 1 foot for every 3 feet of rope pulled.
## 11. Mountain Rescue and Evacuation
**11.1. Considerations and planning.** When evacuating a victim from mountainous areas, the purpose is to get the casualty to medical care without creating more casualties. **The rescuer must not become a casualty** — that is the first rule of rescue planning.

**11.2. Mass casualties.** Separate stages: triage and treatment at the scene, then evacuation by priority — the most seriously injured who can survive evacuation go first.

**11.3. Manual carries and litters.** One-man carries as taught in the Soldier's Manual of Common Tasks (fireman's carry, supporting carry, and others).

**11.4. Rescue systems.** Belay assist (bringing a climber over a section he cannot climb — the belayer hauls the rope to help him past the crux).

**11.5. Low- and high-angle evacuation.** Low-angle: raising systems, belays, and backup safeties are rigged before the litter moves; the litter is raised or lowered on a slope that can be walked; when descending a moderately steep slope, the litter is lowered with tenders controlling it. Secure the litter to the lowering rope with a minimum of **four tie-in points** (one at each corner); two litter tenders descend with the litter to control the descent and monitor the casualty.
## Appendix A — Levels of Military Mountaineering
- **Level 1: Basic Mountaineer.** On technically difficult terrain, the basic mountaineer is capable of moving as a member of a rope team, tying the knots, building the anchors, and performing the individual skills — under the supervision of a Level 2 or 3 leader. Particularly adept soldiers may be selected for special-purpose teams. In a unit training program, Level 1 soldiers are identified and tracked. - **Level 2: Assault Climber.** The assault climber leads climbs, builds rope installations, and trains Level 1 soldiers. - **Level 3: Mountain Leader.** The mountain leader plans and supervises mountain operations, trains Levels 1 and 2, and is responsible for the unit's mountain training program and for risk management on mountain missions.
## Appendix C — Avalanche Search and Rescue
**Immediate action:** mark the victim's last-seen point, and start the search immediately — survival time under snow is measured in minutes. **General procedures:** organize initial searchers and probers; if using avalanche beacons, immediately switch them to receive and search the signal; make a coarse probe of all likely areas of burial and repeat as long as a live victim is possible. **Establishing the victim's most probable location:** use the last-seen point, the avalanche's flow line, and terrain traps to focus the search. **Probing:** probe poles are rigid steel tubing approximately **3/4-inch in diameter**; probing lines must be organized so the ground is covered systematically; two methods — coarse probe (widely spaced, fast, to find a victim quickly) and fine probe (closely spaced, to pinpoint the victim before digging).

## Operating Rules

1. **The mountain is the enemy's ally.** Terrain, weather, and altitude limit combat power before the enemy does.
2. **Acclimatize or evacuate.** Altitude illness is a leadership problem with a timetable, not bad luck.
3. **Rope up on glaciers, always.** The first rule of glacier movement has no exceptions.
4. **Measure the march in time, not distance.** Map distance plus one-third, plus one hour per 1,000 feet of ascent.
5. **The rescuer must not become a casualty.** Plan the rescue before touching the victim.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** TC 3-97.61 (2025) supersedes FM 3-97.61 (2002) where they conflict; the 2002 manual is the deep field reference, not the authority.
