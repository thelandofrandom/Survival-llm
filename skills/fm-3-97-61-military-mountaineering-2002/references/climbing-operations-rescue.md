# Climbing, Installations, Movement, and Rescue — FM 3-97.61 (2002)

## Climbing Technique

**Route selection and preparation:** select the route from the ground before anyone climbs — read the rock, the hazards, and the line of the rope. Terrain selection for training: start on easy ground and build. Preparation: check every man's equipment, tie-in, and understanding of the commands before the first pitch. **Spotting:** the spotter stands below and behind the climber, ready to guide a fall, not catch it.

**Technique:** five body parts — right hand, left hand, right foot, left foot, and the body itself as a counterweight. Stance or body position is probably the single most important element — weight over the feet, hips in, arms straight, eyes on the next holds. Climbing sequence: test the hold, shift weight, move the foot, then the hand — never move two points at once on difficult ground.

**Safety precautions:** on a seldom- or never-traveled route, expect loose rock; if a climber falls, maintain control and do not panic; use a spotter when climbing close to the ground without a rope; never climb directly above or below other climbers (except the belayer's position); avoid gloves (lost "feel" for the rock); be extremely careful on wet or moss-covered rock; never grasp small vegetation for handholds (shallow roots). **Margin of safety:** select routes within the team's ability, and widen the margin when the rock is wet or the weather is adverse.

**Using the feet:** the beginner looks up for handholds; the good climber looks down for footholds. The foot stays on the rock by friction — maximum friction comes from the maximum contact area of the boot sole on the rock, with weight driving straight down through the foot. **Using the hands:** hands reposition continually to keep the body in balance; the beginner puts too much weight on the arms — arms are for balance, legs do the work. **Combination techniques:** chimney, lie-back, stemming, mantling, and others combine hand and foot techniques when no single technique fits the rock.

**Down climbing:** can inadvertently lead into terrain harder than the ascent; it is accomplished at a difficulty level above the climber's up-climbing ability in reverse — face the rock, test every hold, never rush. If down climbing is not possible, a rappel is the safe alternative.

## Tying In and Harnesses

Tie into the climbing rope with a figure-eight retrace through the harness tie-in point. **Presewn harnesses:** seat harness (the standard); chest harness (an additional connecting point — used with a seat harness on steep ground and for glacier travel); full-body harness (chest and seat combined — for anyone who might invert, including the injured). **Improvised harnesses:** swami belt (webbing wrapped around the waist); bowline-on-a-coil (the traditional method — rope wrapped 4–6 times around the waist and tied with a bowline); improvised seat harness (webbing); improvised chest harness (rope or webbing); improvised full-body harness (chest and seat combined). Attach the rope to the improvised harness at the front, at the waist, so a fall does not invert the climber.

## Belaying

The belayer must perform three functions while maintaining total control of the rope: pay out rope as the climber advances, take in rope as the climber descends or rests, and brake a fall.

**Choosing a belay technique:** body belay (the most widely used on moderate ground — friction around the body); mechanical belay (must be used whenever there is potential for a serious fall — a belay device multiplies the braking force).

**Establishing a belay:** direct belay (removes forces from the belayer — the load goes straight to the anchor); indirect belay (the most commonly used — the belay device is attached to the belayer, who is anchored).

**Setting up a belay, in order:** select position and stance; aim the belay (it must point at the climber's line — a mis-aimed belay pulls the belayer sideways in a fall); anchor the belay (for a climbing belay to be considered bombproof, the belayer must be anchored into position); stack the rope (so it feeds without tangles); attach the belay (connect the live rope through the belay device).

**Top-rope belay:** the belayer is positioned at the base of the climb with the rope running up through an anchor at the top and back down to the climber, or at the top with the rope running down.

**Verbal commands** (belayer and climber, with the meaning and action for each): "On belay" / "Belay on" — the belayer is ready. "Climbing" / "Climb" — the climber is starting. "Slack" — give rope. "Tension" / "Up rope" — take in rope. "Falling" — the climber is falling, brake now. "Rock" — falling object, protect yourself. "Off belay" — the climber is safe, the belayer may come off belay. **Rope tug commands:** when voice commands cannot be heard, three distinct tugs on the rope after "Belay on" mean the leader is climbing; the tug signals are agreed before the climb.

**Top-roped climbing** is valuable for training — it lets climbers attempt harder moves with a top rope as protection. **Lead climbing** with two climbers is the preferred combination for movement on technical ground; leading a difficult pitch is the most hazardous task in roped climbing — the leader places protection as he climbs, and a fall can be long. **Following (seconding)** means climbing the pitch the leader has protected, removing protection as you go and racking it properly. **Aid climbing:** the belay is the same as in normal lead climbing; the rope is used to ascend by stepping in etriers; seconding an aid pitch means ascending the rope (usually by Jumaring) and cleaning the protection; seconding through a traverse requires the second to be protected against a pendulum fall. **Clean aid climbing** uses only removable protection — no pitons, no bolts. **Three-man climbing team:** at a disadvantage on a steep belayed climb (the middle man complicates rope management), but sometimes unavoidable — the procedures for tying in three men and managing the rope are in the manual.

## Rope Installations

**Fixed rope with intermediate anchors:** two experienced climbers prepare for a roped climb; the leader climbs and places intermediate anchors as he goes; the rope is fixed at the top and at each intermediate anchor so the rest of the unit can move on it with Prusiks or ascenders. Utilization: personnel attach to the fixed rope during movement for safety. Retrieval: the next-to-last man on the system removes the intermediate anchors as he descends, and the last man retrieves the rope.

**Rappel point — selection:** anchor quality, a route free of obstacles, loading/unloading platforms. **Installation:** if the rappel lane is less than half the rope length, the climber may use one rope doubled (or a single rope with a retrievable rig); if greater than half the rope length, two ropes or a single rope with a mid-rope anchor; if the rappel is so long the rope cannot reach, a multi-stage rappel with intermediate anchors. **Operation:** communication (voice commands or, in a tactical situation, rope tugs); duties and responsibilities (rappel point NCOIC, belayer, rappeller); tying off during the rappel (lock the brake to stop mid-descent). **Recovery:** establish a retrievable rappel (rigged so the rope can be pulled down from below); the next-to-last rappeller descends the lane and prepares the rope for retrieval by the last man.

**Types of rappels:** hasty rappel — moderate pitches only, fast, no belay from below. Body rappel — the rappeller faces the anchor and straddles the rope, friction across the body; hasty and body rappels are not belayed from below. Seat-hip rappel — friction across the hip and seat, the standard military rappel. Figure-eight descender — puts fewer kinks in the rope than a body rappel and gives a smoother descent. Other devices work the same way. Extending the rappel device (moving it away from the body on a sling) gives room to work and keeps the device clear of clothing and equipment. Self-belay techniques: a friction knot (Prusik) above the descender as a backup that locks if the rappeller loses control.

**One-rope bridge — transport tightening system:** the rope is first anchored on the far side of the obstacle. If crossing a stream, the far side is reached by a swimmer or a thrown line. One man on the near side ties a fixed-loop knot (for example, a wireman's knot); the far-side man anchors the rope; a three-man pull team on the near side pulls the slack out; the rope is secured with a transport knot, a tensionless anchor, or a fixed loop.

**One-rope bridge — Z-pulley tightening system:** the rope is brought across the same way; once across, the far-side man anchors it; one soldier ties a friction knot (autoblock, web wrap, or Kleimheist) with a sling on the bridge rope; the rope is routed around the near-side anchor and through carabiners to form the Z; a second sling rope is tied to the bridge rope and anchored to the near side; the three-man pull team pulls, creating the pulley's mechanical advantage; when tight, the rope is tied off with a tensionless anchor knot, transport knot, or fixed loop.

**Utilization:** if dry crossing is impossible, soldiers cross on the bridge by the rappel-seat method or with a pulley. Rigging special equipment: crew-served weapons, litters, and rucksacks are attached to the bridge rope and hauled across on a hauling line — an additional rope brought across and anchored, with carabiners attaching rucksack frames or litter bridles to the bridge rope. **Retrieval:** if the bridge-tightening system was anchored with a retrievable rig (dry method), the rope can be pulled down from the far side.

**A-frame:** even in wooded mountainous terrain, constructing an A-frame may be necessary to gain height. Two sturdy poles (exact size depends on the load). Construction: place the two poles with butt ends flush, mark the apex, lash them with a sling rope (horizontal wraps, then vertical wraps, tied off with the pigtail), and install a spreader. Installation: one man rappels down the pitch and secures two anchor points; the A-frame is raised and anchored; the hauling line is rigged. Retrieval: the A-frame is lowered and disassembled once the load is across.

**Vertical hauling line:** maintain a 12-inch bight in the hauling line; tie clove hitches above the A-frame lashing; anchor the A-frame with a transport tightening system using the doubled rope; tie the ends of another installation rope together with a joining knot to form the hauling loop; attach equipment to the hauling line 12 inches above the joining knot; add fixed loops in the hauling line for more control; when personnel are moved, make a knotted hand line; use as many men as needed to pull the load to the top. If only equipment is hauled, the knotted hand line is not necessary.

**Z-pulley and U-pulley systems:** anchors must be sturdy and able to support the load. Theory: carabiners can substitute for pulleys (with more friction); the mechanical advantage comes from the number of rope segments supporting the load. Construction of the Z-pulley: anchor, friction knot on the load rope, rope routed through carabiners to form the Z, pull team. If the two pulleys touch, the Z is lost along with the mechanical advantage — keep them separated. The U-pulley is the simpler two-segment system — less advantage, faster to build.

## Mountain Walking Techniques

**Basic principles:** the soldier's weight is centered directly over the feet at all times; he places his foot flat and deliberately. Pace adapts to conditions — set a pace sustainable all day. Terrain, weather, and light affect the rate of climb. Breaks are kept to a minimum — a moderate pace removes the need for rest stops. The **rest step** is used for steep slopes, snowfields, and higher elevations: lock the downhill leg straight, rest the weight on the skeleton for a beat, then step — it turns climbing into a series of rests. Downhill walking uses less energy than uphill but is much harder on the body (knees, ankles, balance); shorten the stride and keep weight over the feet. Side-hill travel on any surface should be avoided whenever possible — it loads one leg and one ankle and invites a slip.

**Techniques by ground:** hard ground (firmly compacted, rocky soil) — flat foot, deliberate placement. Snow slopes — kick steps, ice ax as a third point of contact, cut steps on steep snow. Grassy slopes — step on the tussocks, not between them, and expect them to give. Thick brush — both help (handholds, concealment) and hindrance (slow, noisy, hides holes). Scree slopes — descend by running or sliding with the scree, ascend by kicking steps and using the scree's give. Talus slopes — step on the tops of stable rocks, test before weighting, never step on a rock that can roll.

**Safety:** whenever a rock is kicked loose, shout "Rock!" immediately. If a soldier slips or stumbles on sloping terrain, he should try to self-arrest with his ice ax or body — and the team should be trained in self-arrest before it is needed. When traveling through steep terrain, soldiers should be trained in the use of the ice ax as a brake.

**Navigation:** compasses — the magnetic compass is the simplest and most widely used navigational aid in the mountains. Altimeters — a vital piece of equipment that can save a unit; altitude is often the best confirmation of position on a featureless slope. GPS — a space-based, global, all-weather system, but a tool, not a replacement for map and compass; batteries die and signals are blocked by cliffs and canopy. Navigation techniques: terrain association (matching the ground to the map) and dead reckoning (compass bearing plus pace count) — the choice of technique often determines whether the unit arrives. Approach observations: watch the mountain during the approach march — the route looks different from below than from the map. Natural indicators of direction in the Northern Hemisphere: southern slopes are warmer and drier (more vegetation, less snow); northern slopes hold snow longer. Winter route selection: consider snow depth, avalanche hazard, frozen water crossings, and the shorter day.

**Route planning and selection:** map reconnaissance is the primary source of route information. Enemy situation: route selection only after reviewing the enemy picture. Analysis of the operational area: not all mountainous terrain is equal — slope, exposure, and vegetation change the problem. Unit assessment: the key indices are the unit's training level, physical condition, and equipment.

**Time-distance formulas** (Table 8-1, for individuals carrying a rifle and loaded rucksack, flat or gently rolling terrain):

| Mode | Unbroken trail | Broken trail |
|---|---|---|
| On foot, no snow cover | 2–3 kph (cross-country) | 3–4 kph (trail walking) |
| On foot, less than 1 foot of snow | 1.6–3.2 kph | 2–3.2 kph |
| On foot, more than 1 foot of snow | 0.4–1.2 kph | 2–3.2 kph |
| Snowshoeing | 1.6–3.2 kph | 3.2–4 kph |
| Skiing | 1–5.6 kph | 4.8–5.6 kph |
| Skijoring | N/A | 3–24 kph |

March distances in mountains are often measured in time rather than distance: measure the map distance, add **one-third** for a good estimate of actual ground distance, then add **one hour for each 1,000 feet of ascent or 2,000 feet of descent**. Snow cover significantly affects rates of march, and snow can be expected in the mountains most months of the year. Combined soldier loads exceeding **50 pounds per man** slow movement significantly — and with ammunition, mountaineering gear, and cold-weather clothing, soldiers will be carrying well in excess of that. Helicopter support cannot be relied on in the mountains. Route selection guidelines: select a current map (check the date — an old map lies); gather intelligence (the most important consideration in every operation); select a route from the starting point to the movement objective, with alternates.

## Mountain Stream Crossing

**Reconnaissance:** site selection is critical once you determine you must make a wet crossing. A dry crossing on fallen timber or log jams is preferable to any wet crossing. Evaluate depth, current speed, bottom conditions, and the far bank before committing anyone.

**Preparation:** waterproof water-sensitive items — radios, binoculars, SOI, papers, maps. Trousers unbloused and shirts pulled out of trousers; all pockets emptied or secured. Load-carrying equipment harness and load-bearing vest unbuckled (so the load can be shed instantly if the soldier is swept away). Helmets normally removed and placed in the rucksack in slow-moving water. The rucksack worn well up on the shoulders and snug enough that it does not shift. Individual weapons attached to the pack or slung over the shoulder.

**Individual crossings:** face upstream and slightly sideways, leaning into the current; shuffle the feet along the bottom rather than lifting them (a lifted foot is a foot the current can take); cross at a slight downstream angle so as not to fight the current directly; constantly feel for obstacles, holes, and drop-offs with the feet; use a long ice ax, sturdy tree limb, or other staff for balance.

**Team crossing:** a team crosses in mutual support — the strongest members upstream, the weaker sheltered behind them, arms linked or holding a rope. **Rope installations:** establishing the far anchor (whether a handline or rope bridge is to be built, the far anchor goes in first, by a strong swimmer or a thrown line); installation of a handline (if a rope can be placed high enough above the water, the handline lets soldiers cross holding the rope without being swept off their feet). Safety: identify all weak and nonswimmers before the crossing; not every river or stream can be crossed safely — if the installation becomes too difficult, move to another site; the safest methods are always with a handline or one-rope bridge; a lookout is posted **50 to 100 meters upstream** to watch for obstacles and debris; when the unit knows a rope installation will be required, at least two soldiers should be trained to build it.

**Swimming:** immediately jettison any equipment or clothing that restricts movement; do not fight the current — maneuver toward shore feet-first, head above water, watching for obstacles; avoid backwater eddies and converging currents (they contain debris and can trap a swimmer); if the shore is too difficult to reach, seek the closest and safest spot, such as a sandbar or eddy.

## Movement Over Snow and Ice

**Movement over snow:** the progression from walking on flat terrain to moving on steep terrain is gradual — learn each step before the next. When descending on snow, one can usually come straight downhill, even on steep slopes, by plunging the heels and using the ice ax as a brake. **Movement over ice:** the same techniques apply with crampons — flat-foot the crampons, all points in contact, and never trust a single point.

**Ice ax and crampons:** when walking on snow or ice, the ice ax can be used as a third point of contact — the adze for cutting steps, the pick for arresting a fall, the shaft for probing. Crampons: walking in them is not complicated, but it presents its own hazards — the points catch on clothing and on the other boot, and a trip on steep ground is a fall. **Vertical ice:** when a climb on ice reaches the 60- to 70-degree angle, two ice tools are needed (one in each hand). **Descending with crampons and ice ax:** whenever possible, descend straight down, facing outward on gentle slopes and inward on steep ones. **Normal progression:** the use of the ice ax and crampons follows a simple, repeatable cycle — place the tool, place the feet, shift weight, repeat. **Climbing sequence:** using most of these positions, a single ax can be "climbed" — the ax is placed above, the feet move up, and the ax is reset. **Step cutting:** an extremely valuable technique and a required skill — the adze cuts a step the boot can stand on, one step at a time, on ice too hard for crampons alone.

**Self-arrest:** the large number of climbers injured or killed while sliding on snow makes self-arrest a survival skill, not a drill. The technique: roll onto the stomach, drive the pick of the ice ax into the snow, hold the shaft across the body with the pick at the shoulder and the adze at the hip, and press the body down onto the ax — the pick bites and the slide stops. Practice it until it is reflex; a slide gives no time to think.

**Glissading:** a controlled slide down a snow slope. Squatting glissade: the body is placed in a squat with the ice ax as a rudder and brake. Sitting glissade: the glissader sits on the snow with the legs extended, the ice ax as a brake. Safety: a glissade should never be attempted on a slope where the bottom cannot be seen — a hidden crevasse, rock, or cliff at the bottom turns a fast descent into a fatal one.

**Snow and ice anchors:** ice pitons (driven into ice — not as secure as an ice screw, and they loosen as the ice melts around them); ice screws (the most common ice protection — screwed into solid ice, strong in the direction of pull); horseshoe or bollard anchor (an artificial anchor shaped like a horseshoe or a bollard cut into the snow or ice — the rope runs around it; strong when the snow is consolidated); pickets and ice axes (driven into snow as anchors — holding power comes from buried surface area); equalized anchors (snow and ice anchors must be constantly checked — sun and temperature change melt them out).

**Roped climbing on ice and snow:** tie-in is normally to the harness with a figure-eight retrace. On gentle or moderate slopes with little fall danger, the rope is kept taut between climbers so a slip is caught immediately. Belaying on snow and ice follows the same principles as on rock, with snow anchors in place of rock anchors. Fixed ropes on ice are recommended for moving units over steep ice — the rope is fixed at the top and intermediate anchors, and soldiers ascend with Prusiks or ascenders.

## Movement on Glaciers

**Characteristics and definitions:** glaciers flow; crevasses form where the ice flows over a slope or around an obstacle; snow bridges span crevasses; seracs are blocks of ice that can fall without warning; meltwater streams cut the ice.

**Dangers and obstacles:** crevasses (hidden under snow bridges), snow bridges (which can collapse), seracs, and meltwater streams. **Roped movement: the first rule for movement on glaciers is to rope up.** **Use of Prusik knots:** Prusik knots are attached to the climbing rope for all members of the rope team — each climber carries Prusiks to ascend the rope if he falls in. **Securing the backpack/rucksack:** if an individual falls into a crevasse, a loose pack can drag him down or be lost — secure it so it cannot do either. **Routes:** follow the glacier's flow lines, avoid the center of icefalls, and cross crevasses where they are narrowest. **Crossing crevasses:** open crevasses are obvious; hidden ones are the danger — probe ahead, cross one at a time, with the rope team spread out. **Snow bridges:** formed by windblown snow building a bridge over a crevasse; usually strongest at the edge of the crevasse and weakest in the middle — cross at the edge. **Arresting and securing a fallen climber:** the simplest and most common method is for the rope team to drop to the snow, drive ice axes in as anchors, and hold the fall; then secure the fallen climber so he cannot fall farther. **Crevasse rescue techniques:** build a solid snow anchor, transfer the load from the fallen climber to the anchor, and raise the climber — with a **3-to-1** system, the load rises 1 foot for every 3 feet of rope pulled. Snow bridges are usually strongest at the edge of the crevasse; the rescue is built from that edge.

**Glacier bivouac procedures:** probe the ground before pitching a tent — if the probe suddenly has no resistance while pushing down, a crevasse is present. Occasionally while probing, increased pressure is noticed without reaching a solid layer — that is a snow bridge over a void. There should be no unroped movement outside the probed and marked areas. Multiple tent sites can be connected, which keeps tents closer together. If there is a chance for severe storms with high winds, snow walls may be built around the tents.

## Mountain Rescue and Evacuation

**Considerations:** when evacuating a victim from mountainous areas, the purpose is to get the casualty to medical care without creating more casualties. Rescues are unplanned (improvised) or planned rescue operations. Immediate actions at the rescue scene: secure the scene, assess the victim, give first aid, protect the victim from the elements, and call for help. **The rescuer must not become a casualty** — the first rule of rescue planning. The rescue team leader must evaluate the situation and analyze the factors (victim condition, terrain, weather, time, resources, enemy); identify the task; and build a plan that proceeds step by step — secure, stabilize, package, move. Circumstances of the rescue (hostile action, environmental threat, time pressure) change the plan; the plan is written and briefed, not improvised on the slope.

**Mass casualties:** separate stages — triage and treatment at the scene, then evacuation by priority; the most seriously injured who can survive evacuation go first. Evacuation of wounded personnel is based on the victim's condition and the means available.

**Special training:** rescue techniques are perishable skills — train them before they are needed, in the terrain where they will be used.

**Preparation for evacuation:** package the casualty (splint, bandage, insulate, secure in the litter) before moving; check the route and the team; brief everyone on their task.

**Manual carries:** one-man carries as taught in the Soldier's Manual of Common Tasks (fireman's carry, supporting carry, and others). Buddy rappel: the carrier conducts a seat-hip rappel with a victim attached — the rescuer's rope carries both.

**Litters:** manufactured litters (SKED, Stokes, and others readily available) and field-expedient litters improvised from poles, rope, ponchos, and clothing.

**Rescue systems:** belay assist — used to bring a climber over a section he cannot climb; the belayer hauls the rope to help him past the crux. Belay escape — used when a climber has taken a serious fall and the belayer must leave the belay to help; the belay is locked off, tied off with half hitches, and the belayer escapes the system.

**Low-angle evacuation:** raising systems, belays, and backup safeties are rigged before the litter moves. Raising the litter: the litter is prepared as described (packaged, tied in, with tenders). Descending slopes: when descending a moderately steep slope that can be walked, the litter is lowered with tenders controlling it.

**High-angle evacuation:** use multiple anchors for the litter and litter tenders. Secure the litter to the lowering rope with a minimum of **four tie-in points** (one at each corner). Two litter tenders descend with the litter to control the descent and to monitor the casualty. Once the steep slope has been negotiated, continue the rescue with a low-angle evacuation.

## Appendix A — Levels of Military Mountaineering

- **Level 1: Basic Mountaineer.** On technically difficult terrain, the basic mountaineer should be capable of moving as a member of a rope team, tying the knots, building the anchors, and performing the individual skills — under the supervision of a Level 2 or 3 leader. Particularly adept soldiers may be selected as members of special-purpose teams. In a unit training program, Level 1 qualified soldiers should be identified and tracked.
- **Level 2: Assault Climber.** The assault climber leads climbs, builds rope installations, and trains Level 1 soldiers. He is the unit's technical expert on rope systems and the one who gets the unit over vertical ground.
- **Level 3: Mountain Leader.** The mountain leader plans and supervises mountain operations, trains Levels 1 and 2, and is responsible for the unit's mountain training program and for risk management on mountain missions. (The manual advises the Mountain Leader to review this publication before using it for training; Appendix B contains a measurement conversion chart.)

## Appendix C — Avalanche Search and Rescue Techniques

**Immediate action:** mark the victim's last-seen point and start the search immediately — survival time under snow is measured in minutes, and the first 15 minutes are the most survivable.

**General procedures:** organize initial searchers and probers. If using avalanche beacons, immediately switch them to receive and search the signal. Make a coarse probe of all likely areas of burial, and repeat it as long as a live victim is possible.

**Establishing the victim's most probable location:** use the last-seen point, the avalanche's flow line, and terrain traps (depressions, the toe of the deposit, the outside of a bend in the flow) to focus the search — most victims are found along the flow line or in terrain traps.

**Probing for avalanche victims:** probe poles are rigid steel tubing approximately **3/4-inch in diameter** and long enough to reach a buried victim (the manual's poles are sectional). Probing lines must be organized so the ground is covered systematically — probers stand shoulder to shoulder and advance in a line. **Probing techniques:** two distinct methods are recognized. Coarse probe (widely spaced, fast — to find a victim quickly): the sequence is "down probe, up probe, step forward", with left, center, and right probes at each step. Fine probe (closely spaced, slow — to pinpoint the victim before digging): the same sequence at closer spacing, used once the coarse probe has found something or the search area is small. Dig fast once the victim is located — and keep an airway open the moment the face is uncovered.
