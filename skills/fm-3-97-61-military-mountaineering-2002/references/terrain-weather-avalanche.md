# Terrain, Weather, and Avalanche — FM 3-97.61 (2002)

## Rock and Slope Types

- **Granite:** fewer rockfalls, but jagged edges make pulling rope difficult and cut rope.
- **Chalk and limestone:** slippery when wet; limestone is prone to loose blocks.
- **Slate and gneiss:** can be firm and brittle in the same area — test every hold.
- **Sandstone:** usually soft, causing handholds and footholds to break.
- **Grassy slopes:** penetrating roots and increased frost cracking cause a continuous supply of loose rock.
- **Firm spring snow (firn snow):** stopping a slide on small, leftover snow patches is possible — but the snow can fail.
- **Talus:** rocks larger than a dinner plate but smaller than boulders.
- **Scree:** small rocks from pebble size to dinner-plate size; running down scree is fast but hard on the body.

Rock classification by origin: igneous (cooled from molten rock), sedimentary (laid down in layers), metamorphic (igneous or sedimentary rock transformed by heat and pressure). Mountains are built by plate tectonics — folding, faulting, volcanic activity, and erosion.

## Route Classification (YDS)

North American standard, the Yosemite Decimal System:
- **Class 1** — hiking trail. **Class 2** — off-trail scramble. **Class 3** — climbing, ropes for beginners (moderate scrambling). **Class 4** — belayed climbing (moderate to difficult scrambling, some exposure). **Class 5** — free climbing (roped up, belay, intermediate protection).
- Class 5 subdivisions: 5.0–5.4 little difficulty (advanced rock scrambling); 5.5 moderate (three points of contact necessary); 5.6 medium (vertical or overhangs, moderate energy); 5.7 great difficulty (considerable experience, longer stretches, several points of protection); 5.8 very great difficulty (increasing protection, high conditioning and technique); 5.9 extremely great difficulty (well above average ability, exposed positions, often combined with aid A0–A4); 5.10 extraordinary difficulty (improved equipment, intense training, acrobatic technique, refined security technique); 5.11–5.14 greater increases (only talented and dedicated climbers).
- Subdivisions: a/b/c/d from 5.10 to 5.14 (e.g., 5.10d); +/− from 5.9 and below (e.g., 5.8+). **R** or **X** indicates run-out — protection cannot be placed on portions of the route; a fall may mean a ground fall (e.g., 5.8R). Stars (one to five) mark popularity, not difficulty. Always check the local guidebook.
- **Aid grades:** A0 French-free (use a piece of gear to pass one or two moves); A1 easy aid (straightforward, reliable placements); A2 moderate aid (awkward placements, no serious fall danger); A3 hard aid (test protection; falls of 40–60 feet possible, usually clean); A4 serious aid (placements hold body weight only; falls up to two-thirds of a rope length); A5 extreme aid (no placement can be trusted to stop a fall); A6 extremely severe (continuous A5 with A5 belays — if the leader falls, the whole team probably falls). Aid grades also take +/− subdivisions.
- **Grade ratings (commitment grades)** state the approximate time a route takes. Ice climbing ratings have commitment ratings and technical ratings of their own.

## Cross-Country Movement and Tactics

A pre-operations intelligence effort should include topographic and photographic reconnaissance. Movement is often restricted by terrain and weather; erratic weather can stop a unit that is otherwise ready. When the tactical situation requires continued movement during a storm, plan for it — do not improvise in it. Cover and concealment are thin above the treeline; observation and fields of fire are superb from heights and mutual. Avenues of approach are few and obvious (passes, saddles, ridgelines, valley floors). Weather often determines the success or failure of a mission; severe weather decreases morale and increases basic survival problems. Planning considerations: weather, terrain, enemy, the safety or danger of high mountain regions (especially in winter), dry and barren desert mountains, and the unit's conditioning.

## Mountain Air and Weather

- **Pressure:** low in mountainous areas; the barometer usually falls with altitude. Air is thinner, drier, and filters less ultraviolet radiation.
- **Wind:** the force exerted by wind quadruples each time the wind speed doubles. Winds form from uneven heating of the air by the sun and the earth's rotation. Two types are peculiar to mountains: valley winds and foehn winds. The jet stream is a long, meandering current of high-speed winds often exceeding 100 knots.
- **Humidity:** if air is cooled beyond its saturation point, it releases moisture. The adiabatic lapse rate is the rate at which air cools as it rises or warms as it descends.
- **Cloud formation:** convective lifting (sun heating the ground), frontal lifting (two air masses meeting), cyclonic lifting (low pressure pulling air in), orographic lifting (air pushed up and over a mountain).
- **Cloud types:** low-level (0–6,500 feet) — cumulus or stratus; mid-level (6,500–20,000 feet) — prefix "alto" (altocumulus, altostratus); high-level (more than 20,000 feet) — cirrus, cirrostratus, cirrocumulus; vertical-development clouds (cumulonimbus) can grow to 40,000 feet or more. Cloud interpretation is prone to serious error — a cloud's appearance from below does not show its full extent or the weather behind it.
- **Fronts:** warm front (warm air moving over slower cold air); cold front (cold air overtaking slower warm air — faster, sharper weather); occluded front (cold front catching a warm front); stationary front (no significant air movement).
- **Temperature:** for each 100-meter rise in altitude, temperature drops approximately 1°C. On cold, clear, calm mornings, temperature inversions trap cold air in valleys. At high altitudes, solar heating causes the greatest temperature swings. Local weather patterns force air up and over mountaintops, cooling it.
- **Weather forecasting:** weather at various elevations may be quite different; a forecast must reach the small-unit leaders who will act on it, and it must be for the elevation they will occupy. Treat every forecast as a guess with a short horizon; re-evaluate at every halt.
- **Recording data:** wind direction (magnetic, the direction the wind comes from); wind speed in knots; visibility in meters (farthest visible major terrain or man-made feature); present weather (precipitation or obscuring weather); total cloud cover in eighths; ceiling height (where the cloud base intersects elevated terrain); temperature (with or without a thermometer); pressure trend (barometer or altimeter); observed weather and trends. Update continuously.

## Hazards

**Subjective (human) hazards:** falling (carelessness, over-fatigue, heavy equipment); bivouac site selection (protect from rockfall, wind, lightning, avalanche); equipment — ropes are not total security; they can be cut on a sharp edge or break.

**Objective (terrain) hazards:** altitude (especially over 6,500 feet — endurance and judgment fall off); visibility (fog, rain, darkness, blowing snow cause disorientation); gullies (rock, snow, and debris channel down them — if ice is in the gully, a fall is a slide); rockfall (blocks and scree at the base of a climb indicate recurring rockfall); avalanches; hanging glaciers and seracs (avoid if at all possible); crevasses (formed when a glacier flows over a slope and cracks).

**Weather hazards:** winds stronger and more variable in the mountains (force quadruples as speed doubles); more precipitation on the windward side than the leeward; above approximately 8,000 feet, snow can be expected any time of year; air is drier at altitude, so equipment rusts less but skin and lungs dry faster; lightning is frequent, violent, and attracted to high points; after rain, expect slippery rock and terrain; high winds bring frostbite from increased wind chill; for each 100-meter rise, temperature drops about 1°C; if it is snowing, gullies may contain avalanches or snow sloughs that can bury a man; higher altitudes provide less UV filtering.

## Avalanche Hazards

**Slope stability** is the key factor in determining avalanche hazard. **Triggers:** new snow, wind loading, temperature change, and the climber himself. **Snow pits** can be used to determine slope stability — dig into the snowpack and examine the layers for weak layers (faceted snow, surface hoar, depth hoar) under a cohesive slab.

**Types of snow avalanches:** loose-snow avalanches (surface snow sliding from a point, fanning out as it descends) and slab avalanches (a cohesive layer breaking away over a weak layer — the more dangerous type, capable of burying a whole party).

**Protective measures:** avoiding known or suspected avalanche areas is the first and best measure. **Route selection:** select the correct route to avoid avalanche-prone areas — travel on ridges and in dense timber rather than in gullies and on open slopes; cross suspect slopes one at a time, with the rest of the party in a safe place; avoid slopes of 30–45 degrees after new snow or warming. **Stability analysis:** look for nature's billboards on slopes similar to the one you intend to cross — recent avalanches, cracking, collapsing (whumphing), and heavy new snow all signal instability.

**Survival if caught:** (1) Discard equipment — it can injure or burden you, and discarded equipment marks your position. (2) Swim or roll to stay on top of the snow; fight for your life; work toward the edge; if your feet touch the ground, push hard and try to "pop out" onto the surface. (3) If your head goes under, shut your mouth, hold your breath, and position hands and arms to form an air pocket in front of your face — many victims suffocate with mouth and nose plugged with snow. (4) When you sense the avalanche slowing, try your hardest to reach the surface — several victims have been found quickly because a hand or foot was sticking above the surface. (5) When the snow stops it sets up like cement; even if only partially buried, it may be impossible to dig yourself out. Don't shout unless you hear rescuers immediately above — in snow, no one can hear you scream. Don't struggle — you will only waste energy and oxygen. (6) Try to relax; if you feel yourself about to pass out, do not fight it — the respiration of an unconscious person is more shallow, pulse rate declines, and body temperature is lowered, all of which reduce oxygen needed. See Appendix C for search and rescue techniques.
