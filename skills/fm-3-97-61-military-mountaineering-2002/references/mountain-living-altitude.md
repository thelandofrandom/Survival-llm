# Mountain Living and Altitude Illness — FM 3-97.61 (2002)

## Water Supply

Mountain water should never be assumed safe for consumption. Fluids lost through respiration, perspiration, and urination must be replaced if the soldier is to operate efficiently.

- Maintaining fluid balance is a major problem in mountain operations. Thirst may be dulled by high elevations despite the greater threat of dehydration. Hyperventilation and the cool, dry atmosphere bring about a three- to four-fold increase in water loss by evaporation through the lungs. Hard work and overheating increase perspiration. **Drink one quart of water, or the equivalent, every four hours; more during rigorous physical activity.**
- **Three to six quarts of water each day should be consumed.** About 75 percent of the human body is liquid. A loss of two quarts of body fluid (2.5 percent of body weight) decreases physical efficiency by 25 percent; a loss of 12 quarts (15 percent of body weight) is usually fatal. Salt lost by sweating should be replaced in meals — consuming the usual military rations (three meals a day) provides sufficient sodium. Salt tablets are not necessary and may contribute to dehydration.
- Even when water is plentiful, thirst should be satisfied in increments. Quickly drinking a large volume may slow the soldier; if he is hot and the water is cold, severe cramping may result. Drink small amounts often. Keep pure water in reserve for first aid.
- **Three rules of water discipline:** drink only treated water; conserve water for drinking (potable water in the mountains may be in short supply); do not contaminate or pollute water sources.
- Snow, mountain streams, springs, rain, and lakes provide good sources — all must be treated before drinking. Water is scarce above the timberline; after setting up a perimeter (patrol base), send water parties down to the source.

## Nutrition

Three major food components are required to maintain a well-functioning body: carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Increased fatigue may cause soldiers to become disinterested in eating properly — leaders must enforce eating. A balanced diet provides the energy needed for daily activities and for the extra fuel the body burns to stay warm. Nutritional acclimatization at altitude includes increased caloric intake and attention to the balance of the three components.

## Personal Hygiene and Sanitation

Personal hygiene is especially important in the high mountains — infection spreads fast in a cold, tired unit, and a sick soldier is a casualty. Wash hands, keep feet dry, treat small wounds before they become infections. In rocky or frozen ground, digging latrines is usually difficult; use designated areas away from water sources and bivouac sites, cover waste, and enforce the latrine discipline.

## Acclimatization

A person is acclimatized to high elevations when he can effectively perform physically and mentally. The process begins immediately upon arrival. Disappearance of AMS symptoms in four to seven days does **not** indicate complete acclimatization — adjustment continues for weeks or months. The altitude at which complete acclimatization is possible is not a set point, but for most soldiers with proper ascent, nutrition, and physical activity it is about **14,000 feet**.

Immediately upon arrival at high elevations, only minimal physical work can be performed. Incidence and severity of AMS symptoms vary with initial altitude, rate of ascent, level of exertion, and individual susceptibility:
- Rapid ascent (less than 24 hours) to 6,000 feet: 10–20 percent experience mild symptoms.
- Rapid ascent to 10,000 feet: mild symptoms in 75 percent of personnel.
- Rapid ascent to 12,000–14,000 feet: moderate symptoms in over 50 percent; 12–18 percent severe.
- Rapid ascent to 17,500 feet: severe, incapacitating symptoms in almost all individuals.

Vigorous activity during ascent or within the first 24 hours after ascent increases both incidence and severity. Behavioral effects in unacclimatized personnel: increased errors in simple mental tasks; decreased ability for sustained concentration; deterioration of memory; decreased vigilance or lethargy; increased irritability; impairment of night vision and up to 30 percent constriction in peripheral vision at 6,000 feet; loss of appetite; sleep disturbances; irregular breathing; slurred speech; headache. **Judgment and self-evaluation are impaired the same as in a person who is intoxicated.** During the first few days at high altitude, leaders have extreme difficulty maintaining a coordinated unit. A soldier who cannot walk a straight line and has a loss of balance, or suffers an incapacitating headache, should be evacuated to a lower altitude — a descent of at least **1,000 feet for at least 24 hours**.

## Physical and Psychological Conditioning

U.S. forces do not routinely train in mountainous terrain, so extensive preparation is needed. Units must be physically and psychologically conditioned and adjusted before rigorous mountain operations, and conditioned as a team. Conditioning must consider: climatic and terrain conditions of the area of operations; time available; operations with other U.S. or Allied forces and language barriers; attached personnel; current fitness and mountaineering expertise; expected operations; the advance party; predeployment areas that resemble the area of operations; qualified mountain-warfare instructors; equipment requirements and availability; whether equipment goes with the advance party. Since acclimatization cannot be shortened, and the absence of acclimatization cannot be overcome by motivation, the advance party should arrive early to prepare the area and begin acclimatization. Training on high-altitude effects can prevent psychological preconceptions. Regardless of previous training, a physical fitness program that gradually increases in difficulty should be followed. Conditioning should begin with basic climbing and instill confidence — a soldier who has climbed before will climb again.

## Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)

Personnel arriving at moderate elevations (5,000–8,000 feet) usually feel well for the first hours, then symptoms appear. A headache is the most noticeable symptom and may be severe. Even when a soldier feels well, performance is degraded. Treatment: rest, fluids, no further ascent, and descent if symptoms worsen. AMS is nonfatal, although if left untreated or if further ascent is attempted, it can progress to HAPE or HACE.

## High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)

HAPE is swelling and filling of the lungs with fluid, caused by rapid ascent. It occurs under conditions of low oxygen pressure, is encountered at high elevations (over 8,000 feet), and can occur in healthy soldiers. It can cause death; incidence and severity increase with altitude. Few cases have been reported after 10 days at high altitude. Contributing factors: a history of HAPE, rapid or abrupt transition to high altitude, strenuous physical exertion, exposure to cold, anxiety. Soldiers who have had previous attacks are prone to second attacks.

**Symptoms:** progressive dry coughing with frothy white or pink sputum (usually a later sign), then coughing up blood; cyanosis — blue color to the face, hands, and feet; increased ill feeling, labored breathing, dizziness, fainting, repeated clearing of the throat, development of a cough; respiratory difficulty, which may be sudden, accompanied by choking and rapid deterioration; progressive shortness of breath, rapid heartbeat (pulse 120–160), and coughing out of contrast to others who arrived at the same time; crackling, cellophane-like noises (rales) in the lungs; unconsciousness if left untreated, bubbles in the nose and mouth, and death.

**Prevention:** good nutrition, hydration, and gradual ascent — no more than 1,000–2,000 feet per day to the sleeping altitude, with a rest day (no gain in altitude, no heavy physical exertion) for every 3,000 feet gained.

**Treatment:** immediate descent (2,000–3,000 feet minimum) if possible; if not, treatment in a monoplace hyperbaric chamber. A descent of 300 meters may help; manual descent is not delayed to await air evacuation. Rest (litter evacuation), supplemental oxygen if available. Medications listed in the manual (for medical personnel): morphine for systemic vasodilatation and reduction of preload (carefully considered — respiratory depression); furosemide (Lasix), a diuretic, given orally; nifedipine (Procardia); diphenhydramine (Benadryl) for the histamine response. **Mannitol must not be used** — it crystallizes at low temperatures, and since almost all high-altitude environments are cold, using it could be fatal. If untreated, HAPE may become irreversible and cause death; cases recognized early and treated promptly may recover with no aftereffects.

## High-Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)

HACE is the accumulation of fluid in the brain, causing swelling and a depression of brain function. Contributing factors include rapid ascent to heights over 8,000 feet and aggravation by continued ascent, exertion, and cold. **Symptoms:** mild personality changes, paralysis, stupor, loss of coordination, severe headache, vomiting. **Prevention:** good eating habits, maintaining hydration, and gradual ascent as for HAPE. **Treatment:** immediate descent and evacuation to lower altitude — HACE is a medical emergency and can kill quickly.

## Hydration in HAPE and HACE

Maintain hydration but do not force fluids; the casualty is a litter patient in either case. Do not give alcohol. Keep the casualty warm, resting, and supine; evacuate by litter to the lowest altitude available.

## Cold-Weather Injuries

Soldiers must be prepared to survive, move, and fight in winter conditions. Cold injuries divide into two types: **freezing** (frostbite — tissue actually frozen) and **nonfreezing** (trench foot, immersion foot — tissue damaged by prolonged cold and wet without freezing). The wind-chill factor chart in the manual gives the cooling power of wind expressed as an equivalent chill temperature: wind multiplies cold injury risk far beyond the thermometer reading. Many factors in combination determine whether cold injuries occur: temperature, wind, wetness, exposure time, activity level, clothing, and the soldier's condition. Commanders ensure measures are taken: proper clothing and equipment, buddy checks, warming areas, and work-rest cycles. Medical procedures are needed when sickness and injuries occur — leaders must know the signs and the evacuation criteria. Body heat is lost through radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation — stop the losses (shelter, insulation, dry clothing) before treating the injury. Common cold-weather injuries are described in the manual in detail: frostbite (superficial and deep), trench foot, immersion foot, hypothermia, and snow blindness. Never rub frostbitten tissue; never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze; rewarm gradually with body heat or warm water; evacuate all cold injuries for medical evaluation.

## Heat Injuries

- **Heat cramps:** caused by an accumulation of lactic acid in the muscles during exertion in heat, with heavy sweating and salt loss. Treatment: rest in shade, drink water, stretch the cramped muscle.
- **Heat exhaustion:** may occur when a soldier exerts himself in heat — heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, pale and clammy skin. Treatment: rest in shade, loosen clothing, drink water, cool the body.
- **Heat stroke:** a life-threatening situation caused by overexposure to heat — the body's temperature regulation fails; skin is hot and dry, the casualty is confused or unconscious, and the pulse is rapid. Treatment is immediate cooling (shade, water, airflow, ice if available) and urgent evacuation. Heat stroke kills if cooling is delayed.

## Solar Injuries

- **Sunburn:** burning of exposed skin by ultraviolet radiation — worse at altitude, where the air filters less UV. Prevention: cover skin, use sunscreen, wear a hat and sunglasses.
- **Snowblindness:** sunburn of the cornea of the eye, caused by ultraviolet radiation reflected from snow — painful, with tearing, redness, and temporary blindness. Prevention: wear eye protection (goggles or sunglasses) at all times on snow. Treatment: rest in darkness, cover the eyes, and evacuate if vision does not return.
