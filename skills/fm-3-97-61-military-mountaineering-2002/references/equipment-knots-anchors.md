# Equipment, Knots, and Anchors — FM 3-97.61 (2002)

## Footwear and Clothing

- **Footwear:** the hot-weather boot provides an excellent all-round platform for movement; mountain operations in extreme cold require mission-specific footwear — Level 2 and Level 3 mountaineers need boots beyond the standard issue.
- **Clothing (ECWCS layering):** socks are one of the most under-appreciated parts of the clothing system — keep feet dry, change socks regularly, never wear cotton against the skin. Underwear should be made of materials that move moisture away from the body. Insulating layers are worn over the base layer to trap air. Outer layers (ECWCS jacket and pants) are durable, windproof, and water-resistant. Gaiters protect the lower leg from snow and ice. Hand wear must balance warmth against the dexterity needed to work rope, weapons, and equipment. Headwear: a large majority of heat loss (25 percent) occurs through the head — cover it. Helmets: the Kevlar ballistic helmet can be used for most basic mountaineering. Eyewear: the military supply system does not currently provide adequate eye protection for mountain operations — units must source goggles or sunglasses that protect against glare and snowblindness. Maintain clothing per the manufacturer's instructions.

## Climbing Software (Rope, Webbing, Harnesses)

- **Ropes and cord:** the most important pieces of mountaineering equipment. Ropes are kernmantle construction — a braided sheath (mantle) over a load-bearing core. Dynamic rope stretches to absorb a fall; static rope stretches little and is used for fixed ropes, hauling, and rescue.
- **Webbing and slings:** loops of tubular webbing or cord, called slings or runners, connect the climber, the protection, and the anchor. Slings are tied with the water knot (for webbing) or the double fisherman's knot (for cord).
- **Harnesses:** years ago climbers secured themselves to the rope by wrapping it around the waist; presewn harnesses are now standard. Seat harness (the standard for climbing), chest harness (an additional connecting point, used with a seat harness on steep ground and for glacier travel), full-body harness (chest and seat combined — for anyone who might invert, including the injured).

## Climbing Hardware

- **Carabiners:** one of the most versatile pieces of equipment available — connect rope to protection, protection to anchor, and form field-expedient pulleys. Locking carabiners for life-support connections; non-locking for racking and non-critical connections. Gates opposite and opposed when two carabiners are used together.
- **Pitons:** a metal pin hammered into a crack in the rock. Advantages: strong in the right crack. Disadvantages: slow and noisy to place, damage the rock, hard to remove.
- **Piton hammers:** a flat metal head with a handle of wood or metal; used to drive and remove pitons and to drill bolts.
- **Chocks:** a generic term for artificial protection placed in constrictions — stoppers, hexcentrics, and similar devices.
- **Three-point camming device:** a unique design that cams against three points in the crack.
- **Spring-loaded camming devices (SLCDs):** mechanical devices that expand in a crack; the cams must be in the middle of their range when placed.
- **Chock picks:** used to extract chocks from rock when they are stuck.
- **Bolts:** screw-like shafts drilled into rock to provide a fixed anchor point; a hanger and nut are placed on the bolt.
- **Belay devices:** range from the least equipment-intensive (the body belay) to mechanical devices (stitch plate, figure-eight, and others).
- **Descenders:** the figure-eight descender has been used for generations — it puts fewer kinks in the rope than a body rappel.
- **Ascenders:** may be used for climbing a rope, for rescue hauling, and as a personal safety on fixed ropes.
- **Pulleys:** change direction in rope systems and create mechanical advantage; carabiners can substitute with more friction.

## Snow and Ice Hardware

- **Ice ax:** one of the most important tools for the mountaineer — walking aid, self-arrest brake, step-cutting tool, and anchor.
- **Ice hammer:** as short or shorter than the technical ax; used for steep ice.
- **Crampons:** used when footing becomes treacherous; all points must contact the snow or ice; walking in them presents its own hazards (points catching clothing and the other boot).
- **Ice screws:** provide artificial protection and anchors in ice; the most common ice protection.
- **Ice pitons:** used to establish anchor points in ice; less secure than ice screws and they loosen as ice melts around them.
- **Wired snow anchors (flukes):** provide security in snow — buried and pulled tight, they hold by the surface area of the fluke.
- **Snow picket:** used in constructing anchors in snow and ice — driven vertically or at an angle, the holding power comes from the buried surface area.

## Sustainability Equipment

- **Snow saw:** cuts into ice and snow — used to cut steps, blocks for snow shelters, and to test snow layers.
- **Snow shovel:** cuts and removes ice and snow — for shelters, avalanche rescue, and camp work.
- **Wands:** mark routes, crevasses, snow bridges, caches, and safe paths.
- **Avalanche rescue equipment:** beacon (transceiver), probe, and shovel.
- **Packs:** many types and brands are used; the two main considerations are fit and capacity for the mission.
- **Stoves:** define the purpose before choosing — melting snow for water is the primary mountain stove task; the stove must work at altitude and in cold.
- **Tents:** define the mission before choosing — a tent that is too light fails in a storm; one that is too heavy never leaves the rucksack.
- **Skis:** mountaineering skis are wide and short, with a binding that pivots at the toe for climbing.
- **Snowshoes:** the traditional aid to snow travel, attaching to most boots.
- **Ski poles:** assist balance during skiing and walking on snow.
- **Sleds:** vary greatly in size, from the squad-size Ahkio (a component of the standard cold-weather equipment set) to smaller pulks.
- **Headlamps:** a small item not appreciated until it is needed — carry spare batteries.

## Choice of Equipment and Packing

- **Personal gear:** emergency survival kit, carried on the body at all times.
- **Standard gear:** what can be individually worn or carried.
- **Mountaineering equipment and specialized gear:** ropes, protection, anchors, ice tools — mission-specific.
- **Day pack:** for a day away from the bivouac site.
- **Squad or team safety pack:** for a squad-sized element leaving the bivouac — spare rope, first aid, shelter, and rescue gear.
- **The Ten Essentials:** regardless of what equipment is carried, the individual always carries them — navigation (map, compass), sun protection, insulation (extra clothing), illumination (headlamp), first-aid supplies, fire (matches, lighter, tinder), repair kit and tools (knife, cord, tape), nutrition (extra food), hydration (water and purification), emergency shelter.
- **Packing tips:** speed and endurance are enhanced if the load is carried high and close to the body; in rougher terrain, modify the pack plan — heavy articles lower; equipment that may be needed during movement should be arranged for quick access; the pack and its contents should be soundly waterproofed; clothing and sleeping gear in waterproof bags; the ice ax, if not carried in hand, stowed on the outside of the pack; crampons secured to the outside rear of the pack with points covered.

## Rope Preparation, Care, and Inspection

- **Preparation:** new rope comes from the manufacturer in different packaging — on a spool or coiled; cut it to length and secure the ends (whip or melt) to prevent unraveling.
- **Care and maintenance:** do not step on or drag ropes on the ground unnecessarily (dirt and grit cut fibers); do not allow the rope to come into contact with sharp edges (nylon cuts easily); keep the rope as dry as possible (if wet, hang in large loops above the ground); never leave a rope knotted or tightly stretched longer than necessary; never allow one rope to continuously rub over or against another; inspect the rope before each use for frayed or cut spots, mildew, rot, or defects; whip or melt the ends; **do not splice ropes for use in mountaineering**; do not mark ropes with paints or allow them to come into contact with oils or chemicals; never use a mountaineering rope for any purpose except mountaineering; each rope should have a corresponding rope log (**DA Form 5752-R, Rope History**); never subject the rope to high heat or flame; wash all ropes periodically to remove dirt and grit and rinse thoroughly; ultraviolet radiation (sunlight) deteriorates nylon over long periods; when not in use, coil loosely and hang on wooden pegs rather than nails.
- **Inspection:** although the core of a kernmantle rope cannot be seen, core damage is possible — it usually consists of filaments or yarn bunching under the sheath. Check suspected areas by putting them under tension (body weight) and feeling for soft spots, lumps, or flat sections. Many dynamic kernmantle ropes are quite soft and may retain an indentation after loading — distinguish a temporary indentation from permanent core damage by feel and by the rope's history.

## Terminology

- **Bight:** a simple bend of rope in which the rope does not cross itself. **Loop:** a bend in which the rope does cross itself. **Half hitch:** a loop that runs around an object in such a manner as to bind on itself. **Turn:** wraps around an object, providing 360-degree contact. **Round turn:** wraps around an object one and one-half times. **Running end:** the loose or working end of the rope. **Standing part:** the static, stationary, or nonworking end. **Lay:** the direction of twist used in rope construction. **Pigtail:** the portion of the running end between the knot and the end of the rope. **Dress:** the proper arrangement of all the knot parts, removing twists and crossed parts.

## Coiling, Carrying, and Throwing

- **Mountain coil:** grasp the rope approximately 1 meter from the end and coil in large loops over the shoulder or pack — the carry coil for long ropes. **Butterfly coil:** the quickest and easiest technique for coiling a rope for carrying — bights over both shoulders, tied off with the remaining rope. **Coiling smaller-diameter rope:** smaller loops, same principles. **Uncoiling, back-feeding, and stacking:** when the rope is needed, stack it so it feeds without tangles — back-feed from the anchored end.
- **Throwing the rope:** back-feed and neatly stack the rope into coils beginning with the anchored end; throw overhand or sidearm; in strong wind, adjust for drift.

## The Knots (26)

Each knot in the manual is taught with tying steps and checkpoints — the checkpoints are the proof the knot is correct. The 26 knots: **square knot** (join two ropes of equal diameter); **fisherman's knot**; **double fisherman's knot** (self-locking, joins two ropes); **figure-eight bend** (joins two ropes with a figure-eight); **water knot** (joins two ends of webbing); **bowline** (fixed loop in the end of a rope); **round turn and two half hitches** (anchor knot around an object); **figure-eight retrace / rerouted figure-eight** (tie-in knot); **clove hitch** (adjustable anchor hitch); **wireman's knot** (fixed loop in the middle of a rope); **directional figure-eight** (loop points in the direction of pull); **bowline-on-a-bight / two-loop bowline** (two loops, self-equalizing or for the middle man); **two-loop figure-eight**; **figure-eight loop / figure-eight-on-a-bight** (strong loop, readily untied after loading); **Prusik knot** (friction knot — smaller-diameter cord on a larger rope, set and released); **Bachman knot** (friction knot with a carabiner — easier to slide than a Prusik); **bowline-on-a-coil** (tie-in by wrapping the rope around the waist); **three-loop bowline** (three adjustable bights); **figure-eight slip knot** (slip loop for retrievable anchors); **transport knot / overhand slip knot / mule knot** (ties off a tensioned system so it can be released under control); **Kleimhiest knot** (friction knot, one-directional); **frost knot** (fixed loop in webbing or cord); **girth hitch** (sling around an object or through itself); **Munter hitch** (friction hitch in a carabiner — a belay or rappel with no device); **rappel seat** (expedient harness from a sling rope); **guarde knot** (a load-releasable hitch used in hauling systems).

## Anchors

**Natural anchors:** trees (shallow root systems in rocky terrain — check before trusting; pad soft, sap-producing trees to keep sap off ropes and slings); boulders; chockstones (often directional — secure when pulled in one direction; a climber can wedge his own chockstone into a crack; never wedge slings between the chockstone and the rock wall); rock projections; tunnels and arches; bushes and shrubs.

**Slinging techniques:** drape (sling over the anchor — simplest, can slip off); wrap (sling wrapped around the anchor, ends connected — more secure); girth (sling girth-hitched around the anchor — tightens under load, reduces sling strength). **Rope anchor:** the climbing rope itself used as the anchor sling. **Tensionless anchor:** the rope wrapped several times around a large anchor such as a tree — the wraps share the load so the finishing knot carries almost nothing; the strongest anchor for a rope that will be tensioned.

**Deadman:** an object with a large surface area and some length (a log, a pack filled with snow, a buried ski) buried in snow or earth; in extremely hard, rocky terrain where digging a trench is impractical, build the deadman above ground and weight it. Holding power comes from buried surface area, not the object's weight alone.

**Artificial anchors — pitons:** advantages (strong in the right crack, fast once the crack is read); disadvantages (noisy, slow to place well, damage rock, hard to remove). Placement is critical — the piton must match the crack's shape and be driven until it rings solid. Test by pulling up about 1 meter of slack in the climbing rope or by bouncing on it. Attach a carabiner and sling before removal so a freed piton cannot fall. Soft iron pitons that have been used, removed, and straightened may be reused once, then discarded.

**Chocks:** advantages (fast, quiet, no rock damage, removable); disadvantages (need the right crack, can walk out under load changes). Placement: find a crack with a constriction and seat the chock so a pull wedges it tighter. Test by tugging in the direction of the expected pull — a chock that moves is not seated.

**Spring-loaded camming devices (SLCDs):** hold the device like a syringe, pull the retractor, place in the crack, release. Study the crack before selecting the size; the cams must sit in the middle of their range.

**Bolts:** one of the most secure means of establishing protection — a hanger and nut on a bolt drilled into rock with a piton hammer and hand drill with a masonry bit.

**Equalizing anchors:** self-equalizing anchor (maintains an equal load on each piece as the direction of pull changes — sliding sling); pre-equalized anchor (distributes the load equally to each piece for a known direction of pull — fixed-length slings to a master point). Pre-equalized is stronger for a known pull; self-equalizing is safer when the direction may change.
