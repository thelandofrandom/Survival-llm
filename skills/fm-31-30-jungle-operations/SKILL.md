# FM 31-30 Jungle Training and Operations

## Purpose

FM 31-30, *Jungle Training and Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 23 September 1965, 232 pages) — the Vietnam-era Army manual written as "an aid to the individual soldier and the junior leader in learning how to prepare for and conduct jungle combat." Its emphasis is on techniques that let the soldier fight, live, and move in the varied terrain, vegetation, and climatic conditions of the jungle, and it also serves commanders and staff officers preparing units for jungle combat. It supersedes FM 31-30 of 5 October 1960.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 31-30 was itself superseded by **FM 31-35, *Jungle Operations* (26 September 1969)**, and then by **FM 90-5, *Jungle Operations* (16 August 1982)** — both of which are also skills in this vault. For current doctrine, the governing reference is **MCTP 12-10C, *Jungle Operations* (2020)**, already a skill in this vault; where the older manuals disagree with MCTP 12-10C, follow the newer publication. What remains durable in FM 31-30 is its treatment of the jungle as a psychological problem (fear and misconception), its heat-acclimation and heat-disorder doctrine, its movement and march discipline, and its tracking, raiding, and tactical-base concepts — all written from direct experience in the wet tropics. What is dated is its organization and equipment (1965-era weapons, radios, and aviation), its CBR material, and its salt-tablet regimen, which should be checked against current medical guidance before use.

**Sibling note — the four jungle manuals in this vault.** FM 31-30 (1965) is the earliest and the most training-heavy of the four: it is the only one of the four that is explicitly a training manual as well as an operations manual, with a full recommended training program and SOP/manload/equipment appendices. FM 31-35 (1969) is its direct successor — shorter, more operations-focused, and the first to add riverine and stability operations. FM 90-5 (1982) is the post-Vietnam rewrite, "How to Fight," built around a 25th Infantry Division after-action voice and much heavier on small-unit tactics, survival, and the individual soldier's load. FM 31-36 (TEST) (1968), despite its vault label, is not a jungle manual at all — it is *Night Operations*, and its skill is filed under that title. Read FM 31-30 for the foundation; read FM 31-35 for the tightened 1969 doctrine; read FM 90-5 for the fighting manual.

## 1. The Jungle — What It Is and What It Is Not

**1.1. The definition.** For this manual, "jungle" means an area in the wet tropics dominated by large trees and varied associated vegetation, with an abundance of animal, insect, and bird life. The tropical zone lies between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, but the manual is concerned only with the humid tropics — the part where temperature and relative humidity stay high throughout the year.

**1.2. The misconception to unlearn first.** Chapter 2 opens by attacking the popular image of the jungle as "a veritable green hell of large trees and dense underbrush growing over vast expanses of flat, swampy ground and inhabited by thousands of animals, snakes, and insects which are hostile to man." That image, the manual says, produces conditioned fear before a soldier ever sets foot in the jungle, and the fear is intensified by the oppressive humidity and heat, the unfamiliar noises, and "the abject feeling of loneliness that one feels when entering the jungle." The cure is systematic and thorough training and acclimation: "Once this knowledge is acquired, the soldier will respect the jungle, not fear it." That sentence is the manual's thesis, and it recurs in every successor manual.

**1.3. The five elements of jungle terrain.** Terrain is analyzed through topography (the shape of the land — jungle coasts are often alluvial plains and swamps cut by many-branched ridge-and-drainage systems), hydrography (many streams draining the ridges, and large rivers that are deep, swift, difficult to cross, and become raging torrents in the rainy season), vegetation (rapid growth, dense concentration, natural resistance to enemies), surface materials (fine-grained clay-like upland soils, silty lowlands — trafficability "generally very poor, especially during rainy periods"), and cultural features (so scarce that any work of man, however primitive, "is liable to assume critical significance in combat"). Climate is the dominant factor: heavy rainfall, oppressive humidity, high temperatures.

**1.4. Primary jungle vs. secondary growth.** The distinction drives everything tactical. In mature rain forest, tall trees interlace into a canopy that blocks sunlight, so the forest floor is relatively open — fair observation and good fields of fire at ground level are possible. Where old forest has burned or been destroyed, secondary growth forms a tangle that seriously limits both observation and fields of fire. Open areas can be just as limiting: jungle grass sometimes grows several feet over a man's head. Dense foliage gives excellent concealment from ground and air observation; cover comes from surface irregularities — ravines and gullies. Extensive ground-level roots can make digging so difficult that positions must be built above ground level. → `references/jungle-environment-and-movement.md`

## 2. Heat, Acclimation, and Living in the Jungle

**2.1. Acclimation is the first training objective.** "The first concern of commanders who are faced with the task of preparing individuals for jungle combat is the physical and mental acclimation of troops to the oppressive jungle environment." Acclimation is the adaptation of the individual to work in heat with maximum efficiency and least discomfort. Its characteristics, as the manual states them:

- Acclimation begins the first day of arrival in the wet tropics and is well developed by the fourth day.
- As it continues, sweating increases and begins more readily, but salt loss becomes less.
- Physical exercise speeds the process by inducing profuse sweating.
- The body remains acclimated for one to two weeks after departure from the wet tropical environment.
- Troops arriving from southern mid-latitudes acclimate more easily than troops from farther north; troops arriving by surface transport suffer less on arrival than troops arriving by air.
- Some soldiers may never become acclimated, "but, fortunately, the percentage is very small."

**2.2. How the body cools.** Normal body temperature is 98–99°F. The body sheds excess heat three ways: conduction (when air is cooler than the body), radiation (when surrounding surfaces are cooler than the skin), and evaporation of sweat — which becomes the most important means of cooling when radiation cannot keep up. Dilation of blood vessels and a faster heartbeat bring heat to the skin. An unacclimated man becomes irritable, sluggish, unable to sleep, and substandard in performance.

**2.3. Water — the numbers.** During normal operations soldiers should drink **6 to 15 canteens of water a day**, depending on how much they sweat; at very high temperatures this rises to **as much as 25 canteens per day**. "Water must not be rationed in training." In actual operations there are ample water sources in the jungle — but troops must not drink from them without purifying the water.

**2.4. The heat disorders.** Dehydration (about two-thirds of the body is water; the victim becomes sluggish and listless) is the base condition. Heat exhaustion — caused by excessive loss of water — shows as headache, confusion, dizziness, drowsiness, weakness, incoordination, loss of appetite, vomiting, visual disturbances, rapid weak pulse, and cool, wet skin. Treatment: move the victim to a cool, shaded place, elevate his feet, and give salt solution — **two salt tablets, well crushed, dissolved in a canteen of cool water**. Heat cramps come from excessive salt loss: severe, painful muscle cramps, treated the same way; prevention is salt tablets with water at **two salt tablets per canteen, not to exceed six tablets per day**. Heat stroke (sunstroke) is the killer: the body's heat-regulating mechanism breaks down, sweating stops, the skin is hot, red, and dry, and temperature rises to **106–110°F** — "the brain will literally bake in the skull unless the temperature is lowered immediately to at least 102°F." Cool the victim at once — put him in a creek or stream, pour canteens of water on him, fan him, use ice if available — and evacuate as soon as possible.

**2.5. The basic rules for preventing heat disorders.** Drink plenty of water; become acclimated; use extra salt with food and water; eat the heavy meal during the cool of the day (food is the most important source of salt); dress properly for heat — one loose layer, fatigue jacket worn outside the trousers, no undershirt. Loose clothing matters because tightly fitted uniforms become saturated with sweat and hinder evaporation and air circulation.

**2.6. Living in the jungle.** The soldier must be conditioned to the jungle before commitment to combat, or he will "become occupied solely with their surroundings and give little attention to the assigned mission." Jungle operations require independent action by small units, so each soldier must be able to care for his own needs. He usually has to manpack his essentials, since normal logistical support will not be available — so he must learn what his requirements are, how to keep them to a minimum, and how best to use them. Disease is a constant: malaria ("another common" tropical disease — nets and anti-malaria medicines are prescribed), dysentery, and filariasis. Personal hygiene and sanitation are treated as combat duties, not comforts. → `references/heat-acclimation-and-living.md`

## 3. Movement and Marches — Time, Not Distance

**3.1. Calculate in time.** "Movement in the jungle is calculated in terms of time rather than distance." Road nets may be limited or nonexistent in the early stages of a campaign, and cross-country movement is slow and difficult. Planning figures in the manual run to roughly **one-half mile to one kilometer per hour** in jungle movement, and as little as **about 500 meters per hour** in the worst going.

**3.2. The operational effects of the jungle.** Chapter 4 summarizes them as seven limitations: forces must move on widely separated axes; the amount of heavy equipment and supporting weapons that can be used is severely limited; control is difficult and must be decentralized; frontages must be shortened and intervals lessened in movement; numerous limited objectives must be assigned in an attack; maintaining direction of movement is difficult; silence and security measures increase in importance. The jungle unit "must be so balanced that it is capable of developing at any time an effective degree of mobility and firepower without support from higher echelons," and it has to operate within its own resources more often than in any other type of terrain. Junior leaders may find themselves operating on their own initiative for long periods, and the possibility of sudden enemy air or ground attack at any time must not be overlooked.

**3.3. March discipline.** Control is the central problem: dense vegetation restricts mobility and observation, so distances and intervals are reduced, definite march objectives are assigned, and every unit leader must know the complete march plan. Rules of conduct: prescribed distances and intervals rigidly maintained to prevent loss of contact and bunching; at halts, men relax physically but never mentally, and security posts are maintained at all halts; the company commander marches well forward where he can control movement and any tactical employment. March rate depends on the type of jungle, availability of trails, formation, security elements, obstacles, and the physical condition of the troops; commanders must keep rate and rest periods in line with the men's endurance, since extreme heat makes frequent halts almost mandatory. Pack animals accompanying a column slow the march and should not be kept loaded for more than 8 hours a day.

**3.4. Route selection.** Select a route presenting the least obstacles and plan it in a series of legs from one march objective to the next. High ground should not be overemphasized as key terrain — it matters mainly where it controls likely avenues of approach or affords observation, and at the junctions of primary jungle with secondary growth, grasslands, or cultivations. → `references/jungle-environment-and-movement.md`

## 4. Jungle Operations — Offense, Defense, Retrograde

**4.1. The shape of jungle combat.** Standard combat principles apply, modified by wet tropical conditions. Operations are characterized by restricted maneuver, slow tempo, close combat under extremely limited visibility, and difficulty in providing logistical support. Patrolling, operations from forward positions, ambush, and other forms of close combat are common.

**4.2. Command and control.** Because of the difficulties of command, control, and communications, jungle operations require centralized planning and decentralized execution. Commanders must use mission-type orders that give maximum latitude to subordinates, and the mission must be understood by all personnel. A detailed standing operating procedure should be prepared by all units down to and including fire teams, covering organization of combat teams, supply procedures, bivouac and shelter preparation, march rate, and formations.

**4.3. Combat intelligence.** Extensive patrolling is necessary because other collection sources are limited by poor observation from ground and air. Commanders must not conclude that the enemy cannot attack through supposedly impenetrable areas or reinforce in time — movement to an objective may take longer than estimated, and the enemy may reinforce with a unit initially considered too far away.

**4.4. The defense and the retrograde.** In the defense, frontages are shortened and positions are sited for all-round security; in retrograde, the same movement and control problems apply in reverse, with security at every halt. The manual's treatment is brief — the detailed fighting techniques are in Chapter 5.

## 5. Special Combat Techniques — Raids, Ambush, Tracking, Bases

**5.1. Raids.** Jungle terrain lends itself to raids: limited visibility and excellent concealment let skilled raiding patrols penetrate deep into enemy-controlled territory. A platoon or smaller unit is best suited to raid operations. Supporting fires may be used if available, but fire control, timing, and communications make fire support very difficult. A patrol base near the objective lets reconnaissance patrols scout the enemy position while the rest of the raiding force rests and prepares; Army aircraft should be used to transport raiding patrol members into or near the objective whenever possible.

**5.2. Ambush and counterambush.** The manual treats ambush as a core jungle technique and points to FM 21-50, FM 21-75, FM 31-16, FM 31-21, and FM 31-22 for detailed discussion. The development of initiative, boldness, and determination in the small-unit commander, and self-reliance and reliability in the individual soldier, are the stated prerequisites.

**5.3. Tactical base operations.** Because of the difficulty of supporting forces in the jungle and the limited time units can operate independently, tactical bases are established to enable deep penetration of hostile territory. The **combat base** is semipermanent — the focal point for all operations in an area, containing command, control, and combat service support elements, and providing rest, rehabilitation, baths, hot meals, and recreation; its forces provide the reserves. The **patrol base** is temporary, established by units operating away from the combat base; it is not protected by elaborate defenses and must retain a high degree of mobility.

**5.4. Tracking.** Forces may have to hunt down small groups of guerrillas native to an area who know the terrain intimately. Signs that disclose the presence or passage of the enemy: footprints or portions of footprints (know what footwear the enemy is likely to be wearing, to avoid confusion with friendly tracks); the direction of grass, leaves, or sticks kicked up by feet; unnatural formations of vines, dead grass, or broken twigs pushed aside; sap, latex, or fluid exuding from cuts. Tracking is treated again, in more detail, in FM 31-35.

**5.5. Village clearing and counterinsurgency.** Chapter 5 also covers village clearing and counterinsurgency/counterguerrilla operations — the manual's recognition that jungle war in 1965 was as much a population fight as a terrain fight. → `references/operations-logistics-and-support.md`

## 6. Combat Support and Logistics

**6.1. Supporting arms in the jungle.** Chapter 6 covers infantry, artillery, infantry/tank cooperation (small tank units attached to infantry for reducing definitely located targets and enemy strong points where terrain permits), air defense artillery and naval gunfire, engineers, CBR operations, close air support, and Army aviation. The difficulty of movement and the weight of equipment decrease the speed and effectiveness of the supporting arms, forcing foot troops to reduce their speed accordingly.

**6.2. Transportation.** The basic means of jungle transport is hand-carry by indigenous help or troops, though pack animals are frequently employed — jungle vegetation is not satisfactory forage for domesticated animals unless supplemented, so a large part of their load must include feed. Waterborne transportation is "the most economical and often the surest means of supply": streams, lagoons, and waterways should be used to the maximum, with boats, canoes, and rafts. Air transportation matters when all other means fail: supplies can be airlifted, or delivered by parachute or free drop when aircraft cannot land; float aircraft can be used where suitable water areas exist. Wheeled vehicles are generally impracticable except on roads, in the dry season on wide trails, and where jungle growth is light; track-laying vehicles are generally reliable but increase maintenance problems.

**6.3. Supply and maintenance.** Supply requirements must be anticipated well in advance; replenishments requisitioned well in advance; supply routes and water points reconnoitered continuously; alternate routes and distributing points developed. Rapid deterioration is a primary consideration: issue clothing, particularly combat boots and socks, "last a very short time," so requirements must be estimated well in advance and small units should stock limited emergency supplies of boots, socks, and similar short-wear items. Convoys should move in daylight, when the jungle's concealment from air observation makes ambush protection easier.

**6.4. Medical service.** Chapter 7's medical section covers personal hygiene and sanitation, insect- and animal-borne diseases, waterborne diseases, intestinal infections, and special tropical diseases. The medical platoon and division medical battalion organization is described, and evacuation — by air and by water — is treated as a planning factor from the start, since a river may be less than ankle deep in the dry season yet flow several feet deep in the wet season, and sudden flood crests are a danger to cargo-laden craft. → `references/operations-logistics-and-support.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a jungle question from FM 31-30, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (water quantities in canteens per day, salt-tablet rates, acclimation days, march rates in time rather than distance), then the cautions. State the priority order explicitly: acclimation and heat discipline first, then movement and control, then the fighting technique. Name the environment the advice belongs to — wet tropical jungle — and say which manual it comes from when a sibling manual differs. Where current doctrine (MCTP 12-10C, 2020) differs from FM 31-30, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Respect, not fear.** The jungle is neutral; training and acclimation turn fear into respect.
2. **Acclimate first.** Acclimation begins day one and is well developed by day four — do not commit unacclimated troops to hard jungle work.
3. **Water is not rationed in training.** 6–15 canteens a day in normal operations, up to 25 in extreme heat — and purify every jungle source before drinking.
4. **Move in time, not distance.** Plan jungle movement by hours, at roughly a half mile to a kilometer per hour, less in the worst going.
5. **Decentralize control.** Centralized planning, decentralized execution, mission-type orders, SOPs down to fire teams.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** MCTP 12-10C (2020) governs jungle operations today; FM 31-30 is the foundation and the training manual, not the authority.
