# The Jungle Environment and Movement — FM 31-30

## The Jungle Defined

For FM 31-30, "jungle" means an area in the wet tropics dominated by large trees and varied associated vegetation, with abundant animal, insect, and bird life. The manual is concerned only with the humid tropics — the part of the tropical zone (between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn) where temperature and relative humidity stay high throughout the year.

## Unlearning the "Green Hell"

The popular image of the jungle as a green hell of dense underbrush over flat, swampy ground, inhabited by thousands of hostile animals, snakes, and insects, produces conditioned fear before a soldier ever enters it. Heat, humidity, unfamiliar noises, and loneliness intensify it. The cure is systematic, thorough training and acclimation: "Once this knowledge is acquired, the soldier will respect the jungle, not fear it."

## Five Terrain Elements

- **Topography:** jungle coasts are often alluvial plains and swamps cut by many-branched ridge-and-drainage systems — rugged, complex terrain.
- **Hydrography:** many streams draining the ridges; large rivers deep, swift, difficult to cross, becoming raging torrents in the rainy season.
- **Vegetation:** rapid growth, dense concentration, natural resistance to enemies.
- **Surface materials:** fine-grained clay-like upland soils, silty lowlands; trafficability "generally very poor, especially during rainy periods."
- **Cultural features:** so scarce that any work of man, however primitive, "is liable to assume critical significance in combat."

Climate is the dominant factor: heavy rainfall, oppressive humidity, high temperatures.

## Primary Jungle vs. Secondary Growth

- **Mature rain forest:** tall trees interlace into a canopy blocking sunlight, so the forest floor is relatively open — fair observation and good fields of fire at ground level are possible.
- **Secondary growth** (where old forest burned or was destroyed): a tangle that seriously limits observation and fields of fire.
- **Open areas:** jungle grass can grow several feet over a man's head.
- Dense foliage gives excellent concealment from ground and air observation; cover comes from ravines and gullies. Extensive ground-level roots can make digging so difficult that positions must be built above ground level.

## Movement — Time, Not Distance

- Movement is calculated in terms of time rather than distance.
- Planning figures: roughly **one-half mile to one kilometer per hour** in jungle movement, and as little as **about 500 meters per hour** in the worst going.
- Road nets may be limited or nonexistent early in a campaign; cross-country movement is slow and difficult.

## The Seven Operational Limitations (Chapter 4)

1. Forces must move on widely separated axes.
2. Heavy equipment and supporting weapons are severely limited.
3. Control is difficult and must be decentralized.
4. Frontages must be shortened and intervals lessened in movement.
5. Numerous limited objectives must be assigned in an attack.
6. Maintaining direction of movement is difficult.
7. Silence and security measures increase in importance.

The jungle unit must be able to develop mobility and firepower without support from higher echelons, and it operates within its own resources more often than in any other terrain.

## March Discipline

- Distances and intervals reduced; definite march objectives assigned; every unit leader knows the complete march plan.
- Prescribed distances and intervals rigidly maintained to prevent loss of contact and bunching.
- At halts, men relax physically but never mentally; security posts maintained at all halts.
- Company commander marches well forward where he can control movement and tactical employment.
- March rate depends on jungle type, trails, formation, security elements, obstacles, and troop condition; frequent halts are almost mandatory in extreme heat.
- Pack animals slow the march and should not be kept loaded for more than 8 hours a day.

## Route Selection

Select a route presenting the least obstacles and plan it in a series of legs from one march objective to the next. Do not overemphasize high ground as key terrain — it matters mainly where it controls likely avenues of approach or affords observation, and at the junctions of primary jungle with secondary growth, grasslands, or cultivations.
