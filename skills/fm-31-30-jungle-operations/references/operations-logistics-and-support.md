# Operations, Logistics, and Support — FM 31-30

## Command and Control

Jungle operations require centralized planning and decentralized execution. Commanders must use mission-type orders giving maximum latitude to subordinates, and the mission must be understood by all personnel. A detailed standing operating procedure should be prepared by all units down to and including fire teams, covering organization of combat teams, supply procedures, bivouac and shelter preparation, march rate, and formations.

## Combat Intelligence

Extensive patrolling is necessary because other collection sources are limited by poor observation from ground and air. Never conclude that the enemy cannot attack through supposedly impenetrable areas or reinforce in time — movement to an objective may take longer than estimated, and the enemy may reinforce with a unit initially considered too far away.

## Raids

- Jungle terrain lends itself to raids: limited visibility and excellent concealment let skilled patrols penetrate deep into enemy-controlled territory.
- A platoon or smaller unit is best suited to raid operations.
- Supporting fires are difficult: fire control, timing, and communications make fire support very hard to employ.
- Establish a patrol base near the objective; reconnaissance patrols scout the enemy position while the rest rests and prepares.
- Use Army aircraft to transport raiding patrol members into or near the objective whenever possible.

## Ambush and Counterambush

A core jungle technique. Detailed discussion is referred to FM 21-50, FM 21-75, FM 31-16, FM 31-21, and FM 31-22. Prerequisites: initiative, boldness, and determination in the small-unit commander; self-reliance and reliability in the individual soldier.

## Tactical Bases

- **Combat base:** semipermanent, the focal point for all operations in an area, containing command, control, and combat service support elements; provides rest, rehabilitation, baths, hot meals, recreation; its forces provide the reserves.
- **Patrol base:** temporary, established by units operating away from the combat base; not protected by elaborate defenses; must retain a high degree of mobility.

## Tracking

Signs disclosing the presence or passage of the enemy:

1. Footprints or portions of footprints — know what footwear the enemy is likely to wear, to avoid confusion with friendly tracks.
2. Direction of grass, leaves, or sticks kicked up by feet.
3. Unnatural formations of vines, dead grass, or broken twigs pushed aside.
4. Sap, latex, or fluid exuding from cuts.

## Combat Support (Chapter 6)

Infantry, artillery, infantry/tank cooperation (small tank units attached to infantry for definitely located targets and strong points where terrain permits), air defense artillery and naval gunfire, engineers, CBR operations, close air support, and Army aviation. The difficulty of movement and the weight of equipment decrease the speed and effectiveness of the supporting arms, forcing foot troops to reduce speed accordingly.

## Transportation (Chapter 7)

- **Hand-carry** by indigenous help or troops is the basic means; pack animals frequently employed — jungle vegetation is not satisfactory forage unless supplemented, so much of their load must be feed.
- **Waterborne** is "the most economical and often the surest means of supply": streams, lagoons, waterways used to the maximum; boats, canoes, rafts.
- **Air:** supplies airlifted, or delivered by parachute or free drop when aircraft cannot land; float aircraft where suitable water areas exist.
- **Wheeled vehicles** generally impracticable except on roads, in the dry season on wide trails, and where jungle growth is light. Track-laying vehicles generally reliable but increase maintenance problems.

## Supply and Medical

- Anticipate supply requirements well in advance; requisition replenishments well in advance; reconnoiter supply routes and water points continuously; develop alternate routes and distributing points.
- Rapid deterioration is a primary consideration: combat boots and socks "last a very short time" — estimate requirements well in advance and stock emergency supplies at small-unit and battalion level.
- Move convoys in daylight, when jungle concealment from air observation makes ambush protection easier.
- Medical: personal hygiene and sanitation, insect- and animal-borne diseases, waterborne diseases, intestinal infections, special tropical diseases. Evacuation by air and water is a planning factor from the start — a river may be less than ankle deep in the dry season yet flow several feet deep in the wet season, and sudden flood crests endanger cargo-laden craft.
