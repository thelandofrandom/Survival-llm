# MCTP 12-10E Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations

## Purpose

MCTP 12-10E, *Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations* (24 February 2025, including Change 1), is the U.S. Marine Corps' doctrine for fighting in the Arctic — published jointly with the Army as ATP 3-90.96. It supersedes MCRP 12-10A.4 (January 2011) and is approved for public release, distribution unlimited. The manual runs ten chapters — the Arctic environment, operational considerations, planning and executing Arctic operations, command and control, movement and maneuver, intelligence, fires, sustainment, protection, and training — with appendixes on units of measure, mobility, ice, cold-weather injury identification and prevention, and unit measures.

For Marines the Arctic problem is expeditionary at its core. The Marine Corps does not own Arctic bases the way the Army owns Alaska installations; Marines arrive by ship and aircraft, fight from austere positions, and sustain themselves from what they brought and what they can cache. That makes the manual's principles — **campaign mentality, cold-weather capability, isolated endurance, and Arctic task organizing** — a description of how Marines already think, applied to the hardest environment on earth. A Marine unit that cannot endure isolation, move over snow and ice, and keep its people from becoming cold-weather casualties is not an Arctic force; it is a liability the enemy and the weather will both exploit.

The historical warning is in Appendix D and it is a Marine-relevant one: in the winter of 1944–45, cold-weather injuries on the European western front totaled more than **23,000 troops** — about an infantry division and a half — and General Omar Bradley assessed that cold-weather injuries had seriously crippled U.S. fighting strength in Europe. Marines have their own cold-weather history, from the Chosin Reservoir to Norway's cold-weather exercises: the lesson is always the same. ## 1. The Arctic as a Marine Operating Environment

**1.1. What makes it Arctic.** The Arctic is defined by the combination of extreme cold, prolonged darkness and prolonged daylight, permafrost, sea ice, snow and ice terrain, vast distances, and thin infrastructure. It is strategically significant — sea routes, resources, and great-power competition — which is why the Marine Corps must be able to operate there rather than merely survive there. Arctic operations differ from ordinary cold-weather operations by the combination of all these factors at once.

**1.2. Terrain from the sea inward.** Marines typically arrive from the sea, so the terrain problem starts at the shoreline: sea ice limits seaport access, frozen beaches and ice-covered littorals change landing conditions, and the ground inland is tundra, taiga (boreal forest), mountains, glaciers, muskeg, rivers, and lakes. Permafrost makes digging difficult and drainage poor; muskeg swallows vehicles in thaw; rivers and lakes become highways when frozen and obstacles when thawing. **Snow depth and density are the most critical parameters for mobility**, followed by snowpack strength and layering. The same ground is a different battlefield in January, April, and July — terrain analysis must account for season.

**1.3. Weather as a weapon and a threat.** Extreme cold, high winds, blowing and drifting snow, ground blizzards, whiteout, and icing. A **ground blizzard** creates blizzard-like conditions without any precipitation: strong winds drive unpacked snow through the air, visibility collapses, and conditions can last for days. An attacker or defender with the wind at their back gains an advantage; the force facing the wind fights blind. Weather changes rapidly and forecasting is limited by sparse observation networks — commanders plan for weather they cannot predict precisely.

**1.4. Seasons and the ice problem.** Winter (dark, cold, frozen), spring thaw, summer (long daylight, thawed ground, insects, wet), autumn freeze-up. The transition seasons — freeze-up and spring thaw — are the most dangerous for movement: ice is unreliable, ground is impassable, conditions change daily. **Ice thickness is a commander's critical information requirement**: it determines safe travel over frozen water, and leaders must pay particular attention during thaw and freeze. Failure to determine safe ice thickness for travel can result in vehicles and personnel breaking through. Appendix C gives the methods for calculating safe ice thickness for travel and for aircraft, including ice runways.

**1.5. Wildlife.** Bears, moose, wolves, and other Arctic wildlife are a planning factor: a threat to personnel, a hazard to camps and food storage, and a consideration for bivouac site selection.

**1.6. Windchill — the numbers that govern exposure.** Windchill dramatically amplifies cold. Vehicle movement, free-fall parachuting, and helicopter operations all create wind, and any wind can exponentially increase windchill. Table 1-4 is the windchill chart; Table 1-5 gives time to cheek frostbite:

| Frostbite danger | Time to frostbite (dry, exposed cheek skin) |
|---|---|
| Slight danger | Greater than 120 minutes |
| Increased danger | Less than 45 minutes |
| Great danger | Less than 5 minutes |

Frostbite does not occur when ambient air is above freezing — but trench foot and immersion foot may occur at any point on the chart. Table 1-5 data are based on dry cheek skin of the most susceptible five percent of personnel; wet skin or exposed extremities such as fingers can significantly decrease the time. Worked examples: at −20°F with a 25 mph wind, cheek frostbite in about **7 minutes**; at −50°F with a 40 mph wind, about **2 minutes**. For Marines working around helicopters and vehicles — both wind generators — exposure times are shorter than the chart alone suggests, and exposed-skin checks are a leadership duty.

## 2. The Principles — Campaign Mentality, Capability, Endurance, Task Organizing

**2.1. Campaign mentality.** Arctic operations cannot be a single battle or a short deployment. Distances, weather windows, and sustainment timelines force a campaign approach: sequenced operations, pre-positioned supplies, patience. Think in weeks and months, not hours and days.

**2.2. Cold-weather capability.** A unit is either cold-weather capable or it is not, and the difference is training, equipment, and leadership — not willpower. Units with superior cold-weather clothing sustain fewer injuries. Non-cold-weather-capable units need significant time and training before they can operate in the Arctic at all. Fundamental training includes clothing layer management, cold-weather bivouacking, and equipment care.

**2.3. Isolated endurance.** Arctic forces must operate cut off from resupply, communications, and support for extended periods — the normal Marine condition, made harder by cold. Endurance is built by carrying more, caching supplies, and training to function without the logistics tail temperate operations assume.

**2.4. Arctic task organizing.** Forces are task-organized specifically for Arctic operations: the right mix of infantry, armor, aviation, engineers, sustainment, and specialized cold-weather elements, with over-snow vehicles, skis, snowshoes, and cold-weather shelters for the mission and season. For a Marine task force this means the ground combat element, aviation combat element, and logistics combat element are all configured for cold from the start — not adapted after arrival.

## 3. Planning and Executing Arctic Operations (Chapter 3)

**3.1. Planning.** Environmental considerations come first: weather, terrain, ice, darkness, and season shape every course of action. Course-of-action development must use Arctic movement rates, sustainment consumption, and casualty rates — all worse than temperate planning factors. The manual works through PMESII-PT considerations (political, military, economic, social, information, infrastructure, physical environment, time) and the competition continuum: competition, crisis, and armed conflict in the Arctic each pose different demands.

**3.2. Maneuver ashore.** Offensive operations exploit mobility corridors — frozen rivers, ice, snow-packed routes — and the enemy's cold-weather limitations; defensive operations exploit prepared positions, warming shelters, and knowledge of the ground. Enabling operations, stability operations, and defense support of civil authorities are all addressed.

**3.3. The maritime domain.** Amphibious operations, supporting arms, supply by water, and riverine operations are the Marine-relevant core of this chapter: getting ashore, staying supplied from the sea, and operating where sea ice and frozen littorals change both landing and sustainment. Seaport access can be limited by sea ice, and supply by water must account for ice thickness and conditions.

**3.4. The air domain.** Rotary-wing, fixed-wing, and unmanned aircraft systems. Rotary-wing operations face icing, visibility limits, and maintenance burdens; fixed-wing operations need prepared surfaces, with ice-runway thickness and condition requirements and ski landing area control elements; UAS operations are degraded by cold batteries and icing.

## 4. Command and Control (Chapter 4)

**4.1. Small-unit leadership is the control system.** Arctic leadership is hands-on: leaders continually check the fitness of Marines and the adequacy of clothing and equipment, rotate Marines into warming tents, and enforce the buddy system. In the Arctic, a squad leader who does not check feet, gloves, and faces is not leading — the environment will do the checking for them, at casualty cost.

**4.2. Mission command.** Mission command matters more in the Arctic because communications fail more often — cold degrades radios, batteries, and electronics. Subordinates must be able to act on intent when the radio does not work. Headquarters layout must account for heating, power, and shelter, and information systems must be protected from the cold.

**4.3. Risk management.** Cold-weather risk management is continuous: windchill, ice thickness, carbon monoxide, cold-weather injury rates, and vehicle reliability are tracked as risks, not assumed away. Unit organization and headquarters layout are adapted to the environment, not the other way around.

## 5. Movement and Maneuver (Chapter 5)

**5.1. Infantry.** Terrain and snow depth govern infantry movement; cold, ice, and snow affect weapons — lubrication, icing, brittle parts, reduced effectiveness if not managed. Tactical considerations include shorter bounds, warming halts, and the burden of cold-weather clothing and equipment. Bulky clothing reduces mobility and increases fatigue, and many items are less efficient or even harmful if used improperly.

**5.2. Armor.** Deep snow, ice crossing, spring thaw, freeze-up, and summer movement each impose different limits on armor. Winter preparation, observation of fire, ammunition handling (ammunition tends to freeze in the wooden fuse protective ring), and maintenance in extreme cold are specified. Constant supervision is necessary to prevent frostbite among crews, and the wind generated by vehicle movement adds windchill.

**5.3. Rotary-wing aviation.** Attack, reconnaissance, security, air assault, air movement, aeromedical evacuation, C2 support, and aviation sustainment — all with cold-weather limits. Door gunners and crew chiefs face severe frostbite risk; Marines check each other for frostbite after the aircraft departs.

**5.4. Special operations.** Civil affairs, direct action, special reconnaissance, and unconventional warfare in the Arctic, with the same cold-weather constraints applied to small, often isolated teams — the Marine special operations problem in miniature.

## 6. Intelligence and Fires (Chapters 6–7)

**6.1. Intelligence.** The intelligence process — plan and direct, collect and process, produce, disseminate and integrate — operates in the Arctic with degraded collection (weather, darkness, sparse infrastructure), slower dissemination, and a premium on human and local knowledge. Arctic intelligence takes longer and is less certain; plans must carry that uncertainty explicitly.

**6.2. Fires.** Artillery movement, position areas, observation, survey, delivery of fire, and smoke are all affected by cold, snow, and ice: frozen ground changes survey and position preparation, snow changes observation, and cold affects ammunition and fuzes. Air defense artillery faces the same constraints plus the difficulty of tracking low, slow, or icing aircraft in poor visibility. For Marines, supporting arms from the sea face the same Arctic degradation as fires ashore.

## 7. Sustainment (Chapter 8) — The Arctic Eats Logistics

**7.1. Sustainment planning and sites.** Longer lines of communication, fewer routes, weather closures, higher consumption. Sustainment site selection and layout must consider shelter, heating, snow load, drainage, and defense. For an expeditionary force, the sustainment problem is sharper: there is no theater logistics base to fall back on, so what comes ashore and what is cached is what the force has.

**7.2. Maintenance and transportation.** Cold-weather maintenance takes longer and needs heated facilities or shelters; planners should expect substantial delays common with cold-weather maintenance. Transportation over snow and ice needs over-snow vehicles, and movement rates are far below temperate norms. Appendix B covers mobility considerations in detail.

**7.3. Supply — food and water.** The caloric requirement in extreme cold is **4,600 calories per day on average for men and 3,150 for women**, depending on workload. The altitude/cold-weather pack supplements rations with about **1,100 calories** of eat-on-the-move items (caffeinated gum, dried fruits, powdered carbohydrate beverages, energy bars); the Unitized Group Ration series has an arctic supplement adding about **900 calories**. The body consumes food to counteract heat loss and requires increased calories during cold exposure; fats and carbohydrates are quick energy-producing foods. Water requirement is **3.5 to 5 quarts per day**. Bulk water is transported in 5-gallon cans filled only three-quarters full (to allow for freezing expansion); individuals can furnish cooking and drinking water by melting snow or ice. Priority in water distribution: a hot drink first, then personal canteens, then the 5-gallon cans.

**7.4. Field services, distribution, contract support, engineering.** Field services (laundry, bath, mortuary, food service) are harder and slower in the cold; distribution is constrained by weather and routes; operational contract support and general engineering support must be planned for Arctic conditions.

**7.5. Health service support.** Medical treatment, hospitalization, medical evacuation, and medical logistics in the Arctic. Helicopter evacuation is the preferred method of casualty transport, but ground evacuation must always be planned. Preventing casualty hypothermia starts at the point of injury: insulation to treat and prevent hypothermia during tactical casualty combat care and advanced trauma care. Medical personnel can treat mild hypothermia and superficial frostbite effectively — but all hypothermia cases are evacuated to a medical facility, and cold can induce hypothermia that exacerbates treatment timelines.

## 8. Protection and Force Health (Chapter 9)

**8.1. Survivability and camouflage.** Survivability positions in snow and frozen ground (digging is hard; snow positions and ice construction substitute); camouflage and concealment in a white environment, where tracks in snow betray movement and dark objects stand out for miles. For Marines operating from austere positions, the snow position is often the only position.

**8.2. CBRN, electromagnetic protection, EOD.** Chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear hazards behave differently in cold (persistency, detection, decontamination); electromagnetic protection and explosive ordnance disposal support are addressed.

**8.3. Personnel recovery and detainee operations.** Personnel recovery in the Arctic — the same PR problem as elsewhere, made harder by cold, distance, and darkness — and detainee operations under Arctic constraints. For an expeditionary force far from friendly bases, the PR problem is acute: there may be no nearby rescue force, which raises the premium on isolated endurance and on every Marine's SERE fundamentals.

**8.4. Force health protection.** Combat and operational stress control, operational public health services, and cold-weather injury prevention as a command responsibility (see §9).

## 9. Cold-Weather Injuries — Identification, Prevention, Treatment (Appendix D)

**9.1. The injuries (Table D-1).**

| Injury | Signs and symptoms |
|---|---|
| Hypothermia (core below 95°F) | Mild: shivering, lack of sound judgment, confusion, apathy, mild mental impairment, pale cool skin, increased heart/respiratory rates, the "umbles" (fumbles, stumbles, tumbles, grumbles, mumbles). Moderate: uncontrollable shivering, worsening "umbles," increased confusion, cold pale skin. Severe: cessation of shivering, muscle rigidity, stupor progressing to unconsciousness, slow or nonpalpable pulse and respiration, cold bluish skin |
| Frostbite | Freezing of skin; numbness, pale/waxy skin, loss of feeling; time to onset per Tables 1-4/1-5 |
| Trench foot / immersion foot | Can occur at or below 50°F; prolonged wet, cold feet; numbness, swelling, pain |
| Chilblains | Nonfreezing cold injury; red, itchy, swollen skin |
| Snow blindness (solar keratitis) | Scratchy, sandy feeling under the eyelids; red, watering eyes; headache |

**9.2. Treatment principles.** Hypothermia: change the environment from cold and wet to warm and dry; replace damp clothing with dry clothing; add a windproof or waterproof layer and/or place the casualty in shelter; add extra insulation under and around the casualty; provide food and warm liquids; exercise mildly hypothermic patients; package a moderately hypothermic casualty in a hypothermia wrap. **All cases of hypothermia are evacuated to a medical facility.** Snow blindness: blindfold with a dark cloth, reassure the patient, evacuate to a medical treatment facility. Never rub frostbitten tissue; never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze.

**9.3. Prevention — the leader's job.** Cold-weather injuries are always a possibility in cold environments, and if allowed to develop they can be debilitating, diminishing a unit's combat power. Prevention is 24 hours a day: adequate clothing, regular meals, hot drinks, rest, dry clothing. Attentive leadership is essential — leaders ensure Marines have and are using available cold-weather clothing and equipment, frequently rotate Marines into warming tents or areas, and continually check fitness and clothing. The **buddy system** reinforces leadership: buddies monitor each other for eating properly, drinking adequate water, dressing warmly, and equipment condition. Rapid evacuation of cold-weather casualties is essential to minimize long-term effects; helicopter evacuation is preferred, ground evacuation always planned, and risks to patient, unit, and mission assessed.

**9.4. Layer management.** Figure D-1 (Appendix D) gives layer management of cold-weather clothing: insulation layers trap air, the windproof/waterproof outer layer blocks wind and wet, and layers are added or removed to manage sweat — because wet clothing loses insulation and accelerates heat loss. Sweat management is the core individual discipline: work hard enough to stay warm, not so hard you soak your layers.

## 10. Training for Arctic Operations (Chapter 10)

**10.1. Developing Arctic training plans.** Training objectives, preliminary training, winter training, and summer training are sequenced so units build competence before they face the Arctic for real. Preliminary training covers the fundamentals — clothing layer management, cold-weather bivouacking, equipment care — before field training. For Marines, this connects directly to the Corps' cold-weather exercise tradition in Norway: the training is the capability.

**10.2. Other training emphasis.** Physical readiness training for cold-weather demands; specialized training (over-snow movement, skiing, ice operations, cold-weather medical); officer and NCO training, because Arctic operations punish units whose leaders do not understand cold-weather risk management. Units can reduce risks by conducting routine training on cold-weather fundamentals before a crisis occurs.

## 11. Appendixes — The Working Tools

- **Appendix A — Unit of Measure.** Conversion tables for Arctic operations so joint and multinational forces use common figures.
- **Appendix B — Mobility Considerations.** How snow depth, density, strength, and layering affect movement; vehicle, ski, and snowshoe mobility; route selection.
- **Appendix C — Ice Considerations.** Calculating safe ice thickness for travel and for aircraft (including ice runways), ice quality and temperature factors, and bearing-capacity limits. Ice thickness is only one factor in bearing capacity — temperature, quality, and loading matter too.
- **Appendix D — Cold Weather Injury Identification and Prevention.** The injury tables, prevention program, and the World War II vignette.
- **Appendix E and following — Unit Measures.** Checklists and measures units use to assess and sustain cold-weather readiness (including the calorie and water figures in §7.3).

## Output Contract

When asked about Marine Corps Arctic or extreme cold weather operations, answer from this publication's structure: (1) frame the problem as expeditionary — arrival from the sea, austere positions, sustainment from what was brought and cached; (2) describe the environment — terrain from the shoreline inward, weather, seasons, windchill, and ice — with the numbers (frostbite times, 95°F hypothermia threshold, 50°F trench-foot threshold); (3) apply the principles — campaign mentality, cold-weather capability, isolated endurance, Arctic task organizing across the ground, aviation, and logistics combat elements; (4) walk the warfighting functions, emphasizing the maritime domain, supporting arms, and aviation; (5) give the sustainment numbers (4,600/3,150 calories, 3.5–5 quarts of water); (6) give cold-weather injury recognition, prevention, and treatment from Appendix D, with the buddy system and small-unit leadership as the control measures; (7) close with training. Note that this publication is the Marine Corps twin of ATP 3-90.96 — same joint text, different Service framing — and cross-check the two skills against each other.

## Operating Rules

1. **The environment is the first enemy.** Plan for cold, wind, darkness, and ice before planning for the human enemy.
2. **Know the windchill and the frostbite clock.** At −20°F with a 25 mph wind, exposed cheek skin can frostbite in ~7 minutes; at −50°F with 40 mph wind, ~2 minutes. Helicopter and vehicle wind shortens exposure further.
3. **Ice thickness is a commander's critical information requirement.** Never cross frozen water on assumption; verify thickness, temperature, and quality — especially in freeze-up and thaw.
4. **Manage sweat like ammunition.** Layer management is the core individual discipline; wet clothing kills.
5. **Feed and water the force like a weapon system.** 4,600 calories (men) / 3,150 (women) per day; 3.5–5 quarts of water; hot drinks first.
6. **Small-unit leadership is the control measure.** Squad leaders check feet, gloves, and faces; buddies check eating, drinking, dressing, and equipment.
7. **Evacuate every hypothermia case.** Mild hypothermia gets first aid and still goes to a medical facility; never thaw a frozen limb that may refreeze.
8. **Train before you deploy.** Units without cold-weather fundamentals take casualties from the environment before contact — and an expeditionary force has no base to fall back on.
