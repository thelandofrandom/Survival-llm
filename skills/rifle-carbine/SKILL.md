# Rifle and Carbine

## Purpose

TC 3-22.9 *Rifle and Carbine* (U.S. Army, 13 May 2016, with Change 3 of 20 November 2019, 252 pages) is the Army's training circular for the M4-series and M16-series rifle and carbine — the standard service weapons. It replaces the older FM 3-22.9 and reframes marksmanship as a combat skill, not a range skill: the manual's structure follows the shot process (stability, aim, control, movement) and the employment of the weapon in combat conditions, not just on a known-distance range.

Core idea: **every shot is a process, and the process is the same whether the target is at 25 meters or 300, stationary or moving, in daylight or darkness.** The manual's four functional elements — stability, aim, control, movement — are interdependent: a failure in any one of them is a miss, and the shooter's job is to manage all four under time pressure, fatigue, and enemy fire. The second core idea is overmatch: the small unit that shoots faster, more accurately, and at longer range than the enemy wins the firefight, and the manual is built to produce that advantage.

This skill distills chapters 1–9 and appendices A–F: overview and safety, principles of operation, aiming devices, mountable equipment, employment, stability, aim, control, movement, ammunition, ballistics, complex engagements, drills, zeroing, and boresight offsets.

## 1. Overview — Safety First, Safety Always

### Rules of firearms safety

The manual standardizes four rules for any weapon a soldier may employ, in training and in combat, regardless of ammunition type:

1. **Treat every weapon as if it is loaded.** Whether or not a weapon is loaded must not affect how it is handled.
2. **Never point the weapon at anything you do not intend to destroy.** The soldier must know what is in the path of the projectile if the weapon fires, and must minimize the time the muzzle is oriented toward people or objects they do not intend to shoot.
3. **Keep your finger straight and off the trigger until ready to fire.** The soldier is the most important safety feature on any weapon. Mechanical safeties may fail; the trigger finger is the primary safety. Whenever possible, move the weapon to mechanical safe when no target is present.
4. **Ensure positive identification of the target and its surroundings.** The soldier is responsible for every bullet fired, including the projectile's final destination. This rule minimizes fratricide, collateral damage, and damage to infrastructure.

The only exception is when a unit commander directs otherwise for a specific training purpose (for example, blank rounds or training munitions) — and then the commander must take appropriate risk-mitigation actions.

### Weapon safety status (WSS)

Readiness is coded by color, and every firer and leader must be fluent in each status:

| Status | Meaning | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Green — Fully Safe** | Weapon clear | No ammunition present, chamber empty, selector on SAFE |
| **Amber — Substantially Safe** | Administrative/preparatory | Bolt forward, chamber empty, ammunition introduced to the weapon; used primarily for mounted operations and when directed, to allow rapid escalation to red or black |
| **Red — Marginally Safe** | Ready to fire on command | Selector on SAFE, magazine locked in, round in the chamber, bolt locked forward |
| **Black — Not Safe** | Actively engaging | Target positively identified, selector on FIRE, finger on the trigger, in the process of engaging |

Black is not a carried status — it describes the firer in a red status entering an engagement sequence. The manual gives the standard color-code actions for the M4/M16 series in figures 1-2 through 1-5: the soldier performs a defined series of actions to move from one status to the next, and the transitions are drilled until they are automatic.

### Weapons control status and overmatch

Weapons control status (the commander's direction on when weapons may be fired) is separate from weapon safety status and is set by the commander for the situation. **Overmatch** is the manual's framing concept: the small unit achieves overmatch when its effective range, accuracy, and rate of accurate fire exceed the enemy's — the range at which the unit can hit the enemy while the enemy cannot effectively return fire. Everything in the manual serves overmatch.

## 2. Rifle and Carbine Principles of Operation

### The weapon

The Army standard service rifle is the M4-series carbine (with the M16-series rifle as its longer-barreled counterpart). The manual covers the upper receiver (barrel, bolt carrier group, charging handle, handguards/rail) and the lower receiver (trigger group, magazine well, stock, pistol grip) — the parts a soldier must know by name, function, and failure mode.

### Cycle of function

The mechanical process the weapon follows during firing, in eight steps:

1. **Feeding** — a round is stripped from the magazine and moved toward the chamber.
2. **Chambering** — the round is seated in the chamber.
3. **Locking** — the bolt locks into the barrel extension.
4. **Firing** — the firing pin strikes the primer; the round fires.
5. **Unlocking** — gas pressure cycles the bolt carrier rearward; the bolt unlocks.
6. **Extracting** — the extractor pulls the spent case from the chamber.
7. **Ejecting** — the ejector throws the case out of the ejection port.
8. **Cocking** — the hammer is reset for the next shot.

A soldier who knows the cycle of function can diagnose a malfunction by its symptom: the phase where the cycle stopped identifies the general problem. This is the foundation of the malfunction procedures in §8.

### Cooling

Sustained fire heats the barrel, and a hot barrel changes point of impact, degrades accuracy, and can cook off a chambered round. The manual's cooling guidance is practical: manage the rate of fire, let the barrel cool between sustained engagements, and never leave a round chambered in a hot weapon longer than necessary.

## 3. Aiming Devices — MOA, Mils, Optics, Thermals, Lasers

### Units of angular measurement

Two units dominate rifle marksmanship, and the manual teaches both:

- **Minute of angle (MOA):** 1/60th of a degree. **One MOA equals 1.047 inches at 100 yards** — for most applications, a soldier treats it as 1 inch per 100 yards (so 1 MOA ≈ 2 inches at 200 yards, 3 inches at 300 yards). MOA is the language of sight adjustment: a sight that moves 1 MOA per click moves the point of impact about 1 inch at 100 yards.
- **Mil (milliradian):** 1/1000th of a radian; 1 mil subtends 1 meter at 1,000 meters (about 3.6 inches at 100 yards). Mils are the language of range estimation and holdover with mil-dot reticles.

Knowing both matters because optics and reticles mix them: a scope may adjust in MOA while its reticle is graduated in mils, and the shooter must convert without hesitation.

### Optics

The manual covers the close combat optic (CCO), the rifle combat optic (RCO), and magnified optics — their reticles, their zeroing, and their employment. Key points: an optic does not make a bad shooter good; it makes a good shooter faster. The shooter must know the optic's reticle (what each mark means at which range), its adjustment values, and its failure modes (dead battery, fogged lens, loose mount — and the iron sights underneath).

### Thermal sights

Thermal weapon sights detect heat, not light — they see through darkness, smoke, and light foliage, but they are defeated by glass, water, and heavy rain, and they present a digital image the shooter must learn to read. The manual's stadia reticle (vertical and horizontal) gives a rapid range estimate based on standard target dimensions: a standing dismounted threat measured against the vertical stadia, an exposed threat against the horizontal stadia.

### Pointers, illuminators, and lasers

Visible and infrared laser aiming devices, illuminators, and their employment — including the safety rules for lasers (never point a laser at friendly forces or aircraft; infrared lasers are invisible to the naked eye and therefore more dangerous, not less).

→ `references/weapon-operation-and-optics.md` for the MOA/mil tables, the optic and thermal summaries, and the stadia range estimation method.

## 4. Mountable Equipment

The adaptive rail system (the handguard rail) is the mounting platform. The manual covers mountable weapons (grenade launchers, shotguns), mountable aiming devices (optics, lasers, illuminators), and mountable accessories (vertical grips, bipods, lights). The rule: every mounted item adds weight and changes the weapon's balance and point of impact — mount only what the mission requires, confirm zero after mounting, and know how to remove a failed accessory under pressure.

## 5. Employment — The Shot Process

The shot process is the manual's central framework, and it is taught as a sequence with four functional elements running through it:

### The shot process

Pre-shot (target acquisition, weapon presentation, stability) → shot (aim, trigger control, follow-through) → post-shot (call the shot, evaluate, re-engage or move). The process is the same for every engagement; what changes is the speed, driven by target size, target distance, and shooter capability.

### The four functional elements

- **Stability** — stabilize the weapon to provide a consistent base, maintained through the shot until the recoil pulse has ceased. This includes how the soldier holds the weapon, uses structures or objects for support, and positions the body. Stability is the foundation: without it, aim and control cannot compensate.
- **Aim** — the continuous process of orienting the weapon correctly, aligning the sights, aligning on the target, and applying the appropriate lead and elevation (hold) during the engagement.
- **Control** — all the conscious actions before, during, and after the shot that the soldier controls: whether, when, and how to engage. Trigger control is the first of these. Control also incorporates the soldier as a function of safety and the ultimate responsibility for firing the weapon.
- **Movement** — the soldier's movement during the engagement: forward, retrograde, lateral, and turning, while maintaining stabilization, aim, and control.

**Target acquisition** is the front end of the process: detect, identify, and locate the target, then present the weapon and acquire the sight picture. The manual stresses that target acquisition is a skill — scanning, sectoring, and using optics and the naked eye in combination — not a gift.

### Time, target, distance, and skill

The effort required of each functional element depends on time available, target size, target distance, and the soldier's skill. A large, close, stationary target needs little stability and little precision; a small, distant, moving target under limited visibility needs everything. The most complex shooting is under combat conditions — the soldier moving, the enemy moving, limited visibility — and training must progress from the simplest shot to the most complex, not the reverse.

## 6. Stability — Support, Relaxation, Natural Point of Aim

- **Support:** use the ground, a wall, a sandbag, a bipod, or a barricade. Support is the single greatest accuracy multiplier available, and the manual's stabilized firing and firing positions sections teach how to build it from whatever is at hand — including barricade shooting, where a bipod or the weapon's handguard is braced against cover.
- **Muscle relaxation:** tension destroys accuracy. The shooter relaxes every muscle not needed to hold the position, and lets bone support (elbows on the ground, skeleton carrying the weight) do the work.
- **Natural point of aim (NPA):** the point the weapon naturally points when the shooter is relaxed in position. The shooter finds NPA by closing the eyes, relaxing, opening the eyes, and seeing where the sights are — then adjusts the *body*, not the muscles, to bring NPA onto the target. A shooter who muscles the weapon onto the target will drift off it the moment they relax or fire.
- **Recoil management:** the shooter's body position and grip absorb recoil and return the sights to the target. Recoil is managed by position, not by strength.
- **Shooter–gun angle, field of view, carry positions:** the geometry of the shooter behind the weapon, the field of view through the optic, and the carry positions (low ready, high ready, patrol carry) that balance readiness against safety and fatigue.

## 7. Aim — Common, Complex, and Environmental Engagements

- **Common engagements:** the standard shots — stationary targets at known and unknown distances, in daylight, from standard positions. The manual's common aiming errors (cant, improper sight alignment, incorrect sight picture, flinch, and improper hold) are catalogued so a coach can diagnose them.
- **Complex engagements:** moving targets (lead), multiple targets (engagement order and transitions), targets at angles (uphill/downhill — gravity affects the bullet's drop based on horizontal distance, not slope distance), limited visibility (night, smoke, thermal), and engagements from moving vehicles or while moving.
- **Target conditions:** target size, exposure, and movement change the hold and the lead.
- **Environmental conditions:** wind (the dominant environmental factor — read it by mirage, vegetation, and feel, and hold into it), temperature and altitude (air density changes trajectory), rain, and light (glare, shadow, and backlight change what the shooter can see through the optic).

## 8. Control — Trigger, Breathing, Workspace, Malfunctions

- **Trigger control:** the skillful manipulation of the trigger that causes the rifle to fire without disturbing aim. The manual's standard: a smooth, straight-to-the-rear press, with the finger placed on the trigger so the press does not pull the muzzle off target, and follow-through that holds the trigger to the rear until the recoil pulse ends.
- **Breathing control:** fire at the natural respiratory pause — the brief moment at the end of an exhalation when the body is still. Do not hold the breath for more than a few seconds; oxygen deprivation degrades vision and judgment faster than it steadies the sights.
- **Workspace management:** the area in front of the shooter's body where loading, reloading, and malfunction clearance happen — kept clear, efficient, and consistent so the shooter can work without looking.
- **Calling the shot:** the shooter predicts where the shot went based on the sight picture at the moment of firing — before looking at the target. A shooter who can call the shot can diagnose their own errors; a shooter who cannot is guessing.
- **Rate of fire:** the sustainable rate that maintains accuracy — faster is not better if it is not accurate. The manual distinguishes deliberate, rapid, and sustained rates and their purposes.
- **Follow-through:** hold the position, hold the trigger, reacquire the sight picture, and be ready for the next shot. Follow-through is what makes the second shot as accurate as the first.

### Malfunctions — immediate and remedial action

Malfunctions are recognized by their symptoms, and the corrective action is determined by the symptom, not by analysis. The manual's rule: **do not over-analyze; execute corrective action immediately, without hesitation or investigation, during combat conditions.**

- **Immediate action** — simple, rapid actions taken when the trigger is squeezed, the hammer falls with an audible **"click,"** and the weapon does not fire. Immediate action corrects rudimentary failures: failure to fire (round chambered, hammer falls, no bang), failure to feed (bolt does not return fully forward — a visible gap between the bolt carrier and the ejection port; may cause a stovepipe or double feed), failure to chamber (round feeds but the bolt does not fully seat and lock), failure to extract (extractor loses its grip; the case stays partially or fully seated), and failure to eject (case extracted but not thrown clear of the ejection port).
- **Remedial action** — a skilled technique applied to a specific problem that immediate action will not fix. Taken when the trigger has little resistance (**"mush"**) or cannot be squeezed. Remedial action corrects: immediate action failing after a minimum of **two cycles** (first without a magazine change, second with a magazine change); a **stovepipe** (a case lodged sideways, stopping the bolt); a **double feed** (two rounds competing for the chamber); and a bolt override or other stoppage that requires the weapon to be cleared by hand.
- **Transition to secondary weapon:** when the primary weapon cannot be brought back into action quickly, or the threat cannot wait, transition to the secondary weapon — if one exists. If no secondary weapon is available, move to cover, announce status to the team, and execute corrective action. The end state of any corrective action is a properly functioning weapon.

## 9. Movement — Shooting on the Move

Movement techniques: forward, retrograde, lateral, and turning — each with its own footwork, weapon presentation, and stability compromises. The manual's rule is that movement degrades stability, and the shooter compensates by shortening the engagement distance, accepting a larger acceptable target area, or stopping to shoot when precision matters. Moving and shooting is a skill trained deliberately: the shooter who has never practiced it will either stop to shoot (and be a stationary target) or shoot while moving (and miss).

## Output Contract

When asked about rifle marksmanship, answer with the manual's structure: (1) state the safety status and the rules that apply; (2) name the shot-process element involved (stability, aim, control, movement); (3) give the procedure as a numbered sequence with the numbers that matter (MOA values, distances, rates); (4) name the common error and its correction. If the user reports a malfunction, ask for the symptom (click, mush, trigger won't move, visible stovepipe) and give the corrective action for that symptom — never a generic "clear the weapon." If the user asks about zeroing, give the 25-meter procedure and the confirmation requirement.

## Operating Rules

1. **The four rules are absolute.** No training objective, no time pressure, no commander's convenience overrides them.
2. **The shooter owns every shot.** Positive identification, awareness of what is beyond the target, and responsibility for the projectile's final destination.
3. **The shot process is the same every time.** Stability, aim, control, movement — in that order, every shot.
4. **Diagnose by symptom, act by drill.** A malfunction is a symptom with a drilled response, not a puzzle to solve under fire.
5. **Zero is verified, not assumed.** Borelight is a starting point; live-fire zero at 25 meters is the confirmation.
6. **Overmatch is the goal.** Faster, more accurate, longer-range fire than the enemy — everything else is support to that end.
