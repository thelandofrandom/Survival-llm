# MARCH — Hemorrhage, Airway, Breathing

Source: ATP 4-02.11, Chapters 4 (Massive Bleeding Control), 5 (Airway Control), 6 (Respiration and Breathing Control), 7 (Circulation Control), 8 (Hypothermia Control).

## Massive hemorrhage (Ch. 4)

**Order of control:**
1. Tourniquet for life-threatening extremity bleeding — in CUF, hasty and "high and tight"; in TFC, 2–3 inches above the wound (or high and tight if the wound cannot be seen or there are multiple wounds). If bleeding continues, apply a second tourniquet above the first.
2. Junctional bleeding (groin, armpit, neck): junctional tourniquet, or pack with hemostatic dressing and hold firm direct pressure for at least 3 minutes.
3. Other compressible bleeding: pack with hemostatic dressing (CoTCCC-recommended, e.g., Combat Gauze) or plain gauze, then apply a pressure bandage.
4. Expose and clearly mark all tourniquet sites with the time of application. Reassess all bleeding control continuously.

**Expedient technique:** improvised tourniquet — band at least 1.5 inches wide with a windlass (stick, jack handle, cleaning rod, pipe). Commercial windlass tourniquets are vastly more reliable; carry one.

**Pulse sites for checking circulation:** carotid (neck), femoral (groin), radial (wrist), posterior tibial (ankle). Loss of the radial pulse with a palpable carotid pulse indicates shock.

## Airway (Ch. 5)

- Open: head-tilt/chin-lift, or jaw-thrust if a neck injury is suspected. Clear blood, vomit, debris.
- Unconscious casualty who cannot protect their own airway: recovery position.
- Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) when the gag reflex is intact; supraglottic airway when indicated; surgical airway (cricothyroidotomy) is a medic-level intervention for a casualty who cannot be oxygenated any other way.
- Choking: abdominal thrusts for a conscious casualty; chest thrusts for a pregnant or very obese casualty; abdominal thrusts on an unresponsive casualty lying down.

## Respiration and breathing (Ch. 6)

- Open chest wound: apply a **vented chest seal** (CoTCCC-recommended, self-adhering) immediately. If breathing worsens after sealing, "burp" the seal — lift a corner to let trapped air out.
- Suspect **tension pneumothorax** with a chest wound plus increasing breathing difficulty, absent breath sounds on one side, and deteriorating vitals. Treat with needle decompression.

### Needle decompression (NDC) — exact procedure

- **Sites:** second intercostal space in the midclavicular line (outside the nipple line), OR anterior axillary line in the fifth intercostal space.
- **Technique:** insert perpendicular to the chest wall; hold the needle in place for **5–10 seconds**, then remove the needle and leave the catheter.
- **Warnings:** **do NOT put an NDC through a chest seal** — use an alternate site. Watch placement to avoid the heart and arteries.
- **Aftercare:** unconscious → recovery position; conscious → sit if breathing is easier. Monitor continuously — tension pneumothorax can recur.
- **If it fails:** burp the chest seal; second NDC at the alternate site; if it worked and recurs, repeat at the same site beside the original; if still no improvement after the second NDC, move on to circulation — do not keep needling. Bilateral NDC may be indicated with bilateral chest trauma.

## Circulation (Ch. 7)

- Treat the cause of shock (bleeding) first. Resuscitate with **blood or blood products** when available; avoid large-volume crystalloid, which dilutes clotting factors.
- Assess pelvic fractures (pelvic binder if indicated); reassess every tourniquet and dressing; expose fully to find hidden bleeding, then cover to prevent heat loss.

## Hypothermia (Ch. 8)

Hypothermia begins the moment a casualty is exposed and bleeding, and it prevents blood from clotting — one leg of the lethal triad (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy). Prevention is treatment: minimize exposure, remove wet clothing, cover with a hypothermia prevention kit or blankets, and keep the casualty warm through evacuation. The "H" in MARCH stands for hypothermia prevention and head injury.
