## PAWS

| Letter | Content |
|---|---|
| P — Pain | Combat Wound Medication Pack: **two 500 mg acetaminophen caplets (1,000 mg total, extended-release) + one 15 mg meloxicam tablet**. Neither alters platelet function; meloxicam is the preferred NSAID. Do not give NSAIDs that impair clotting to a bleeding casualty. |
| A — Antibiotics | Give for open wounds per unit medical guidance; document dose and time on the TCCC Card. Prevention of wound infection, never a substitute for bleeding control. |
| W — Wounds | Dress all wounds; check the back and exit wounds; eye injuries get a rigid eye shield (never press a penetrated eye, never remove an impaled object). |
| S — Splinting | Splint as found; immobilize joint above and below; pad; check circulation below the splint; recheck after movement. |

## Eye trauma (Ch. 10)

Rigid eye shield or cup secured over the injury without pressure. Do not apply pressure, do not remove foreign objects, do not let the casualty rub the eye. Bilateral injuries: cover both, guide the casualty.

## Head injury (Ch. 11)

Assess consciousness (AVPU/GCS); protect the airway; avoid hyperventilation unless directed by a medic; evacuate urgently if consciousness falls, pupils are unequal, or the casualty vomits repeatedly. Keep the head elevated slightly if no spine injury is suspected. Do not give sedatives that mask the neurological exam without medical direction.

## Burns (Ch. 12)

Cool, cover with a dry sterile dressing, treat for shock. Rule of Nines: head 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, front torso 18%, back torso 18%, perineum 1%. Burns over 20% body surface, and burns of face, hands, feet, or genitals, are urgent evacuations with fluid resuscitation. Analgesia may be given for burn pain; antibiotics are not given for burns alone.

## Fractures and splinting (Ch. 13)

Splint as found; immobilize above and below; pad; check circulation. Open fractures: cover with a dressing first, never push bone back in. Pelvic fractures: binder. Femur fractures: traction splint when trained and equipped. Spinal injuries: do not move unless the scene is unsafe; log-roll as a unit.

## Other secondary injuries (Ch. 14)

- Abdominal wounds: do not push organs back in; cover with a clean moist dressing, then dry over it; nothing by mouth; evacuate fast.
- Impalement: stabilize the object in place; never remove it in the field.
- Bandaging: snug enough to hold, loose enough to preserve circulation; check below the bandage every 15 minutes.

## Monitoring and evacuation prep (Ch. 15)

Reassess continuously: breathing, pulse, mental state, bleeding control, temperature. Repackage before evacuation: secure dressings, splints, and lines; confirm the TCCC Card is attached; communicate MIST. Conduct an after-action review after evacuation.

## Bites, stings, climatic injuries (Ch. 16–17)

Snakebite: keep still, limb at heart level, remove rings/watches, splint, no cutting, no suction, no tourniquet, no ice — evacuate for antivenin. Heatstroke: cool aggressively, evacuate. Frostbite: rewarm in warm water only if refreezing is impossible; never rub. Hypothermia: rewarm gradually, no alcohol. Mountain injuries (altitude sickness, cold) are covered in Ch. 17.

## Sickle cell trait (Ch. 18)

Exertional collapse in a person with sickle cell trait can be fatal and is often mistaken for heat injury. A casualty who collapses during exertion with muscle pain and weakness — especially a known carrier — is a medical emergency: cool them, give fluids if conscious, evacuate immediately.

## CBRN first aid (Ch. 19)

Protect yourself first (mask, gloves); remove the casualty from the contaminated area; decontaminate skin before treating wounds. Nerve-agent signs (pinpoint pupils, drooling, difficulty breathing, convulsions): atropine and 2-PAM autoinjectors per the Mark I kit. Biological: symptoms appear hours to days later; supportive care and evacuation. Radiological/nuclear: treat burns and wounds; decontaminate; evacuate.

## Combat and operational stress control (Ch. 20)

Stress reactions (shaking, confusion, withdrawal, panic) are normal responses to abnormal events. Psychological first aid: keep the casualty safe, warm, and fed; give simple purposeful tasks; reassure; stay with them. Most recover with rest, food, and reassurance; those who do not improve need evacuation. Leaders watch for stress reactions in their people early, as a team problem.
