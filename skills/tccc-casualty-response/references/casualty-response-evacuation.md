# Casualty Response, Extraction, and Evacuation

Source: ATP 4-02.11, Part One, Chapters 1 (Casualty Response) and 2 (Casualty Extraction and Movement).

## Chapter outline

- Ch. 1: Casualty response overview; terminology; casualty collection point operations; casualty response for U.S. Army units; casualty response for U.S. Marine Corps units
- Ch. 2: Casualty extraction; casualty movement

## Casualty collection points (CCPs)

A CCP is a covered, concealed location near the point of injury where casualties are gathered, triaged, treated, and staged for evacuation. Requirements: cover from fire and observation; access for litter teams and vehicles; space to sort casualties by urgency. The manual gives layouts for CCPs in open areas and inside buildings. Deceased personnel are moved to a separate area on MEDEVAC platforms to maintain dignity and keep the focus on the living.

## MEDEVAC vs. CASEVAC

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| MEDEVAC | Dedicated medical evacuation by ground or air ambulance, with medical personnel aboard. Most seriously injured go here. Primary means during large-scale combat operations is ground MEDEVAC. |
| CASEVAC | Evacuation by non-medical transport (any available vehicle/aircraft). Less seriously injured. |

If both are used, the most seriously injured go on MEDEVAC. When MEDEVAC is unavailable, units use CASEVAC and coordinate through the chain of command. Rehearse CASEVAC and MEDEVAC like any other critical part of an operation.

## The 9-line MEDEVAC request

Transmit over the radio, announcing **"I HAVE A MEDEVAC REQUEST"**, then the lines in sequence (IAW STANAG 2020 and STANAG 2627 for the NATO format, used only when directed):

1. Location of the pickup site (grid coordinates)
2. Radio frequency and call sign
3. Number of patients by precedence (urgent, urgent-surgical, priority, routine, convenience)
4. Special equipment required (hoist, ventilator, litter, etc.) — also where additional medical supplies are requested
5. Number of patients by type (litter / ambulatory)
6. Security at the pickup site (no enemy, possible enemy, enemy in area, enemy troops)
7. Method of marking the pickup site (panels, smoke, signal)
8. Patient nationality and status (U.S. military, civilian, EPW, etc.)
9. CBRN contamination (none, chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear)

There is no difference between a ground or air evacuation request format.

## MIST report and DD Form 1380

Along with the 9-line, the receiving facility may require a MIST report:

- **M** — Mechanism of injury
- **I** — Injury type
- **S** — Signs and symptoms
- **T** — Treatments given

The completed **DD Form 1380 (TCCC Card)** is the MIST report. Record all care on it; attach it to the casualty (belt loop or pocket) so it travels with them to the battalion aid station and Role 2. The BAS and Role 2 read the card — a casualty without documentation arrives as an unknown.

## Extraction and movement

- Drags and carries: chosen by the casualty's injuries, the terrain, and the tactical situation.
- Litters: standard litter is preferred; improvised litters from poles and ponchos work when nothing else is available. Litter teams rotate bearers on a schedule — a tired team drops casualties.
- Loading: secure the casualty, protect the airway during movement, keep the casualty warm, and recheck bleeding control after every move.
