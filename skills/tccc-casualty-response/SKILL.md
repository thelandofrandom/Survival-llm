# TCCC Casualty Response

## Purpose

ATP 4-02.11, *Army Techniques Publication for Casualty Response, Tactical Combat Casualty Care, and First Aid* (U.S. Army, 23 March 2026; supersedes TC 4-02.1, 21 January 2016) — the current Army standard for treating casualties in combat. This skill is for soldiers, combat lifesavers, medics, and anyone who may have to keep a wounded person alive under fire or in an austere tactical environment until evacuation. It is also the standard a civilian should know when a trauma situation is violent, chaotic, or far from an ambulance.

The core idea is a hard-won lesson from twenty years of combat data: **most preventable deaths on the battlefield are from bleeding out, and they happen in minutes.** The manual's answer is a strict sequence — **MARCH-PAWS** — executed differently in three phases of care, with the right equipment carried on the body and the discipline to treat life threats before anything else. The manual states its content is accurate as of 23 March 2026 and points to the Committee on Tactical Combat Casualty Care (CoTCCC) clinical practice guidelines as the living standard behind it.

**Currency note — read this first:** this is the current standard. Where it conflicts with FM 4-25.11 *First Aid* (2002), distilled in the `first-aid` skill, **this manual wins**. The two biggest changes: (1) the tourniquet is no longer a last resort — for life-threatening extremity bleeding it is applied early and aggressively, "high and tight" under fire; (2) hemorrhage control now includes hemostatic dressings, junctional tourniquets, wound packing, and blood-product resuscitation, none of which existed in the 2002 doctrine. If you learned first aid from the old manual, unlearn those two points first.

## 1. Casualty Response — The System Around the Casualty

**1.1. What casualty response is.** Casualty response is the system that moves a wounded person from the point of injury to a medical treatment facility: casualty collection points (CCPs), casualty extraction and movement, evacuation by MEDEVAC (dedicated medical aircraft/vehicles) or CASEVAC (non-medical transport), and documentation throughout. The most seriously injured go on MEDEVAC; the less seriously injured go by CASEVAC. Ground MEDEVAC is the primary means of evacuation during large-scale combat operations.

**1.2. Casualty collection points.** A CCP is a covered, concealed location near the point of injury where casualties are gathered, triaged, treated, and staged for evacuation. It needs cover from fire and observation, access for litter teams and vehicles, and space to sort casualties by urgency. The manual gives layouts for CCPs in open areas and inside buildings, and stresses rehearsal: CASEVAC and MEDEVAC are rehearsed like any other critical part of an operation.

**1.3. Documentation is treatment.** Every intervention is recorded on the **DD Form 1380, the TCCC Card**, attached to the casualty (belt loop or pocket) and traveling with them to the next role of care. The completed card *is* the MIST report (Mechanism of injury, Injury type, Signs and symptoms, Treatments). A casualty without documentation arrives as an unknown — and unknowns get slower, worse care.

**1.4. The 9-line MEDEVAC request.** Evacuation is requested over the radio with the 9-line format (IAW STANAG 2020/2627 for NATO): announce "I HAVE A MEDEVAC REQUEST," then transmit the lines in sequence — location, radio frequency and call sign, number and precedence of patients, special equipment required, number and type of patients by litter/ambulatory, security at the pickup site, method of marking the pickup site, patient nationality and status, and any CBRN contamination. Transmit exactly, in order, using the brevity codes on the MEDEVAC request card. → `references/casualty-response-evacuation.md`

## 2. The Three Phases of TCCC

**2.1. Care Under Fire (CUF).** The first phase, while still taking effective enemy fire. Care is minimal and mission-first: return fire, get the casualty to cover, and stop life-threatening extremity bleeding — nothing else. In CUF, apply a hasty tourniquet **"high and tight"** on the injured arm or leg, then move to cover as fast as possible. Do not expose yourself to treat an airway or check a pulse while under fire. The casualty's own self-aid matters most here: every soldier should be able to apply their own tourniquet one-handed.

**2.2. Tactical Field Care (TFC).** The second phase, once the casualty and the responder are in relative cover and no longer under effective fire. This is where the full MARCH-PAWS assessment happens: bleeding control, airway, breathing, circulation, hypothermia prevention, then pain, antibiotics, wounds, and splinting. TFC is also where blood-product resuscitation, fluid resuscitation, and advanced interventions (needle decompression, surgical airway) occur.

**2.3. Tactical Evacuation Care (TACEVAC).** The third phase, during movement to a medical treatment facility. The principle is continuity: keep monitoring, reassess every intervention, continue the care begun in TFC, and prepare the casualty for handover — reassess, repackage, document, and communicate (MIST + 9-line if evacuation has not yet been requested). → `references/tccc-phases-march.md`

## 3. MARCH — The Assessment That Saves Lives

MARCH is the order of treatment in TFC. Do it in order, every time, and do not move to the next letter until the current one is controlled.

**M — Massive hemorrhage (the top priority).** Expose the wound — cut clothing away. For life-threatening extremity bleeding, apply a tourniquet **2–3 inches above the wound** (or high and tight if the wound cannot be seen or there are multiple wounds). If bleeding continues, apply a second tourniquet above the first. For bleeding at the junction of limbs and torso (groin, armpit, neck) where a limb tourniquet cannot work, use a **junctional tourniquet** or pack the wound with a **hemostatic dressing** (CoTCCC-recommended, e.g., Combat Gauze) and hold firm direct pressure for at least 3 minutes. For other compressible bleeding, pack with hemostatic or plain gauze and apply a pressure bandage. Expose and clearly mark all tourniquet sites with the time of application. Reassess all bleeding control continuously — tourniquets loosen, dressings soak through, and a casualty who was stable can re-bleed. **Expedient technique:** if no commercial tourniquet is available, an improvised tourniquet can be made from a band at least 1.5 inches wide with a windlass (stick, jack handle, cleaning rod) — but a commercial windlass tourniquet is vastly more reliable, and every soldier should carry one.

**A — Airway.** Open the airway: head-tilt/chin-lift, or jaw-thrust if a neck injury is suspected. Clear blood, vomit, and debris. An unconscious casualty who cannot protect their own airway goes into the recovery position. If the airway cannot be maintained, the next steps are a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) for a casualty with an intact gag reflex, or a supraglottic airway; a surgical airway (cricothyroidotomy) is a medic-level intervention for a casualty who cannot be oxygenated any other way. Do not give anything by mouth to a casualty with a compromised airway.

**R — Respiration (breathing).** Look for open chest wounds and seal them immediately with a **vented chest seal** (CoTCCC-recommended, self-adhering). A sucking chest wound left open collapses the lung; a sealed chest with trapped air becomes a tension pneumothorax, which is why the seal must be vented. If breathing worsens after sealing, "burp" the seal — lift a corner to let trapped air out. Suspect a **tension pneumothorax** when there is a chest wound with increasing breathing difficulty, absent breath sounds on one side, and deteriorating vital signs: treat with **needle decompression (NDC)**.

**C — Circulation.** Check for shock: weak or absent radial pulse, altered mental state, pale cool clammy skin. Treat the cause (bleeding) first, then resuscitate with blood products when available — the manual emphasizes blood and blood-product resuscitation over large-volume crystalloid fluids, which dilute clotting factors and worsen bleeding. Assess and treat pelvic fractures (pelvic binder, if indicated) and reassess every tourniquet and dressing. Expose the casualty fully to find hidden bleeding, then cover to prevent heat loss.

**H — Hypothermia and head injury.** Hypothermia begins the moment a casualty is exposed and bleeding, and it kills by making blood unable to clot — it is one leg of the "lethal triad" (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy). Prevent it aggressively: minimize exposure, remove wet clothing, cover with a hypothermia prevention kit or blankets, and keep the casualty warm through evacuation. For head injury: assess consciousness (AVPU or GCS), protect the airway, avoid hyperventilation unless directed by a medic, and evacuate urgently — a deteriorating head injury is a time-critical evacuation. → `references/march-hemorrhage-airway-breathing.md`

## 4. Needle Decompression — The Numbers

Tension pneumothorax is treated by inserting a needle into the chest to let trapped air escape. The manual's exact procedure:

- **Sites (either one):** the **second intercostal space in the midclavicular line** (make sure the site is outside the nipple line), or the **anterior axillary line in the fifth intercostal space**.
- **Technique:** insert perpendicular to the chest wall. Hold the needle in place for **5–10 seconds**, then remove the needle and leave the catheter.
- **Warnings:** **do NOT put an NDC through a chest seal** — use an alternate site. Watch placement to avoid the heart and arteries.
- **Aftercare:** place an unconscious casualty in the recovery position; a conscious casualty may sit if breathing is easier that way. Monitor continuously — a tension pneumothorax can recur.
- **If it does not work:** burp the chest seal first. If the first NDC fails, attempt a second NDC at the alternate site. If it worked and then recurs, repeat the NDC at the same site, right beside the original. If there is still no improvement after the second NDC, move on to the circulation assessment — do not keep needling.

## 5. PAWS — After the Life Threats

**P — Pain.** Treat pain after life threats are controlled. The manual's Combat Wound Medication Pack (CWMP) for mild to moderate pain is **two 500 mg caplets of acetaminophen (1,000 mg total, extended-release) plus one 15 mg tablet of meloxicam**. Acetaminophen and meloxicam do not alter platelet function and are safe in combat casualties; meloxicam is the preferred NSAID for this reason. Do not give NSAIDs or aspirin-like drugs that impair clotting to a bleeding casualty. Severe pain in a casualty who is still bleeding or in shock is a medic-level problem — ketamine and other agents are used under medical direction and are not covered as self-aid in this manual.

**A — Antibiotics.** Give antibiotics for open wounds as directed by the unit's medical guidance and document the dose and time on the TCCC Card. Antibiotics are prevention of wound infection, not treatment of the injury itself — they never take priority over bleeding control.

**W — Wounds.** Dress all wounds: clean around them, cover with sterile dressings, check for additional wounds (including the back and exit wounds), and bandage. Eye injuries get a **rigid eye shield** taped over the eye — never press on a penetrated eye, never remove an impaled object, and never give the casualty anything by mouth that could raise eye pressure. Burns are covered and cooled per the burns chapter, with fluid resuscitation guided by the Rule of Nines (see the `first-aid` skill) and evacuation.

**S — Splinting.** Splint fractures as found — do not straighten. Immobilize the joint above and below the break, pad the splint, check circulation below it, and recheck after movement. Pelvic fractures get a binder; femur fractures are splinted in traction when trained and equipped. → `references/paws-secondary-injuries.md`

## 6. Secondary Injuries — Eye, Head, Burns, Fractures

**Eye trauma.** Cover a wounded eye with a rigid eye shield, secured without pressure. Do not apply pressure, do not remove foreign objects, do not let the casualty rub the eye. Bilateral eye injuries are covered the same way and the casualty is guided, not left to walk alone.

**Head injury.** Assess consciousness, protect the airway, and evacuate urgently if consciousness is falling, pupils are unequal, or the casualty vomits repeatedly. Keep the head elevated slightly if there is no spine injury. Do not give sedatives or pain medication that masks the neurological exam without medical direction.

**Burns.** Cool, cover with a dry sterile dressing, treat for shock, and estimate area with the Rule of Nines (head 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, front torso 18%, back torso 18%, perineum 1%). Burns over 20% of body surface, and burns of the face, hands, feet, or genitals, are urgent evacuations with fluid resuscitation. Analgesia may be given for burn pain; antibiotics are not given for burns alone.

**Fractures and splinting.** As in PAWS: splint as found, immobilize above and below, pad, check circulation. Open fractures are covered with a dressing first and the bone is never pushed back in. Spinal injuries: do not move the casualty unless the scene is unsafe; log-roll as a unit.

**Bites, stings, and climatic injuries (Part Three).** The manual carries forward the FM 4-25.11 first-aid content on snakebite (keep still, keep the limb at heart level, no cutting, no suction, no tourniquet, no ice — evacuate for antivenin), heat injuries (heatstroke is a medical emergency: cool aggressively), and cold injuries (rewarm gradually, never rub frostbite). **Sickle cell trait (Ch. 18)** gets its own chapter: exertional collapse in a person with sickle cell trait can be fatal and is often mistaken for heat injury — a casualty who collapses during exertion with muscle pain and weakness, especially a known carrier, is a medical emergency; cool them, give fluids if conscious, and evacuate immediately.

**CBRN first aid (Ch. 19).** Protect yourself first (mask, gloves); remove the casualty from the contaminated area; decontaminate skin before treating wounds; nerve-agent signs (pinpoint pupils, drooling, difficulty breathing, convulsions) are treated with atropine and 2-PAM autoinjectors per the Mark I kit. → `references/paws-secondary-injuries.md`

## 7. Monitoring, Evacuation Prep, and Stress

**Monitoring.** Reassess the casualty continuously: breathing, pulse, mental state, bleeding control, and temperature. A casualty who was stable can deteriorate — especially from re-bleeding, a recurring tension pneumothorax, or worsening shock. Document every change on the TCCC Card.

**Pre-evacuation.** Reassess, repackage (secure all dressings, splints, and lines), confirm the TCCC Card is attached, communicate the MIST report, and stage the casualty for loading. Conduct an after-action review after the evacuation — what was missed, what ran out, what needs resupply. Line 4 of the 9-line MEDEVAC request is where additional medical supplies are requested.

**Combat and operational stress (Ch. 20).** Stress reactions — shaking, confusion, withdrawal, panic — are normal responses to abnormal events. Psychological first aid: keep the casualty safe, warm, and fed; give simple purposeful tasks; reassure; stay with them. Most recover with rest, food, and reassurance. Those who do not improve need evacuation. Leaders watch for stress reactions in their people the same way they watch for bleeding: early, and as a team problem, not an individual failure.

## Output Contract

When asked a casualty-care question, answer in this order: (1) which TCCC phase applies (Care Under Fire, Tactical Field Care, or Tactical Evacuation Care) — the answer changes with the phase; (2) the MARCH-PAWS letter that governs it and what to do, with the numbers (tourniquet placement, NDC sites, drug doses, times); (3) what to document on the DD Form 1380; (4) what NOT to do — the manual's warnings are instructions (never put an NDC through a chest seal; never give NSAIDs that impair clotting to a bleeding casualty; never move a spine injury). Cite the chapter (e.g., "ATP 4-02.11, Ch. 4 — Massive Bleeding Control"). If the question is a non-tactical first-aid problem (a kitchen burn, a child's broken arm, a hiker's sprain), say so and use the `first-aid` skill — TCCC is the standard for combat and tactical casualties, and its phase discipline does not apply to a civilian scene.

## Operating Rules

1. **MARCH before PAWS, always.** A splinted fracture means nothing if the casualty bled out while you splinted it.
2. **The phase decides the care.** Under fire: return fire, cover, hasty tourniquet — nothing else.
3. **Tourniquets early, high and tight under fire, 2–3 inches above the wound in TFC.** Mark the time. Reassess constantly.
4. **Blood, not water.** Resuscitate with blood products when available; large-volume crystalloid dilutes clotting and worsens bleeding.
5. **Hypothermia kills the clot.** Prevent heat loss from the first minute — it is treatment, not comfort.
6. **Document everything on the DD Form 1380.** The card is the casualty's voice at the next role of care.
7. **This manual supersedes FM 4-25.11 where they differ.** The 2002 first-aid manual is still useful for non-tactical care; it is not the combat standard.
8. **Train with the equipment you will carry.** A tourniquet you cannot apply one-handed, in the dark, under stress, is a tourniquet you do not have.
