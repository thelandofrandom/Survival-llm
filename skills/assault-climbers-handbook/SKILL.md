# Assault Climbers Handbook

## Purpose

The *Assault Climbers Handbook* (United States Marine Corps, Mountain Warfare Training Center, Bridgeport, California, Summer 2002, 296 pages) is the lesson-plan course book for the Marine assault climber: the two-man climbing teams who get a rifle company over vertical ground and build the rope installations the rest of the unit moves on. It is written as 26 lesson plans plus an appendix listing the Marine Assault Climber's Kit (MACK) — from mountain safety and rope care through anchors, mechanical advantage, rope bridges, rappelling, party climbing, aid climbing, rescue, and the cliff assault itself.

**Status — read this first.** This is a 2002 Marine Corps course handbook, not current Army doctrine. The current Army mountaineering reference is **TC 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (November 2025)**, already a skill in this vault as `military-mountaineering`. Where the two disagree on a procedure, a rating, or a piece of equipment, follow TC 3-97.61. This handbook remains valuable because it is the most detailed treatment in the vault of the assault-climber mission specifically: the MACK and its issue, the one-rope bridge and suspension traverse as company-level installations, and the cliff reconnaissance report and assault sequence, which the Army manual does not cover in the same form. Equipment strengths, rope specifications, and knot data below are as taught in 2002 — check current issue items against the MACK SL-3 list and current UIAA ratings before relying on a number.

## 1. Mountain Safety — BE SAFE MARINE

**1.1. Planning is the safety system.** The handbook's opening principle: the key to mountain safety is proper prior planning and adherence to basic principles and predetermined actions. The memory key is **BE SAFE MARINE**:

- **B — Be aware of the group's ability.** Evaluate individual abilities before planning; the plan follows the weakest climber, not the strongest. - **E — Evaluate terrain and weather constantly.** Conditions change; re-evaluate at every halt. - **S — Stay as a group.** Individuals acting alone are at a great disadvantage in mountainous terrain.
- **A — Appreciate time requirements.** Efficient use of available time is vital; mountain movement is always slower than the map suggests.
- **F — Find shelter during storms, if required.** Inclement weather can make continued movement lethal rather than merely miserable.
- **E — Eat plenty and drink lots of liquids.** Cold and altitude suppress appetite and thirst; eat and drink on schedule.
- **M — Maintain proper clothing and equipment.**
- **A — Ask locals about conditions.** An often-overlooked source of information.
- **R — Remember to keep calm and think.** Panic kills faster than exposure.
- **I — Insist on emergency rations and kits.** At least one assault load per squad.
- **N — Never forget accident procedures.** Report: time of accident; nature and location; number injured; best approach route to the accident scene.
- **E — Energy is saved when warm and dry.** Adequate shelter, insulation from the ground (branches, rucksack), extra clothing, extra equipment for insulation, and external heat while conserving fuel.

**1.2. Route planning.** Plan movement leg by leg on a route card: leg, azimuth, distance, grid, ETA/ETD, elevation gain/loss, and a description of the ground. → `references/safety-and-mack.md`

## 2. The Marine Assault Climber's Kit (MACK)
**2.1. What it is.** The MACK is a comprehensive collection of climbing equipment that enables a Marine rifle company (reinforced), approximately 200 men with organic equipment, to negotiate an average **300-foot vertical danger area**. It outfits **four 2-man climbing teams** plus the items needed to supply the remainder of the company. The climbing teams conduct 2-party climbs over vertical obstacles and establish rope installations for the rest of the company.

**2.2. Containers and accountability.** Four containers hold the kit; each protects contents from sunlight and moisture for storage periods of up to **5 years**, and the lid interior carries a permanently affixed list of components and quantities. Container #1 holds climbing-team equipment; #2, #3, and #4 hold company climbing equipment.

**2.3. Serviceability.** Any item that becomes unserviceable or shows excessive wear must be replaced immediately. Except for the rope bag and climbing rack bag, no attempt is made to repair MACK components; damaged items are disposed of through standard supply procedures (MILSTRIP, or local purchase for items unavailable through supply — any brand meeting the function, strength, and description in the SL-3 list is acceptable). Only a Marine NCO, SNCO, or officer with formal military mountaineering training (Summer Mountain Leader's Course, school code M7A) may supervise use of MACK components.

**2.4. Preparing the kit for use.** Per Marine, cut/whip one **15–18 foot sling rope** from dynamic rope, plus one non-locking and one locking carabiner. Cut static rope for the mission (spools of 300 ft, 165 ft / 50 m, or 600 ft); cut 1-inch tubular tape into **15–25 foot** static anchor cords; cut 7 mm nylon cord into **3–6 foot** lengths for tightening systems — never cut 7 mm cord for company personnel to use as anchors (too low tensile strength; use static rope or tape only). Team equipment: dynamic climbing rope in 165 ft (50 m) lengths; web runners 4–48 inches tied with the water/tape knot; hexcentrics wired with 5.5 mm Kevlar cord secured with a triple fisherman's knot. Silence the rack by wrapping vinyl tape (one layer only) around non-locking carabiner bodies and large (size 7–11) hexcentrics. Replace rope/cordage after **two seasons of use** or when unserviceable.
## 3. Ropes, Webbing, Carabiners, Protection
**3.1. Ropes.** All military ropes must meet UIAA standards or U.S. Federal Test Standard 191A. Most ropes have a **5-year shelf life and a maximum 2-year service life**.

| Rope | Color | Construction | Min tensile | Max elongation | Diameter | Sizes | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Static | Black | Kernmantle | 7,500 lbs | 1.5% | 11 mm | 165 ft, 300 ft | Rescue, bridging — low stretch under load |
| Dynamic | Olive drab | Water-resistant kernmantle | 6,500 lbs | 6% | 10.5 / 11 mm | 165 ft | Lead / party climbing |
| Maxim Dry | OD or multi-color | Water-repellent kernmantle | 3,472 lbs | 6% | 9 mm | 150 ft | Glacier travel, ice climbing |
| Gold Line II | — | 8-strand braided nylon plymor | 4,500 lbs | 20% | 11 mm | 300 / 600 ft spools | Sling ropes and litters only |

Sling ropes are made from 15-foot lengths of plymor or dynamic rope only. Nylon rope stretches under tension and ruptures at **30–70% elongation** depending on construction; it **loses as much as 30% of its strength when wet**; temperatures as low as **250°F** damage it; nylon fuses at 400°F and melts at 480°F. Manila rope (heavier, kinks when wet, burns above 300°F, edible by rodents) is the inferior alternative the manual compares against.

**3.2. Webbing and carabiners.** 1-inch tubular nylon webbing: approximately **4,000–4,500 lbs** tensile; pre-sewn Spectra runners: approximately **5,500 lbs**. Steel locking "D" carabiners: minimum **5,500 lbs**; the steel-locking oval Stubai 82 (3,300 lbs) is obsolete and beyond service life.

**3.3. Protection strengths (as used at MWTC).** Wired stoppers: #1–#2, **350 kg (~770 lbs)**; #3–#5, **650 kg (~1,430 lbs)**; #6–#12, **1,100 kg (~2,420 lbs)**. Hexcentrics #1–#3 wired, 1,100 kg. Spring-loaded camming devices: large **2,400 lbs**, small **2,600 lbs** minimum. Type I cord (5.5 mm Spectra/Kevlar, for wiring hexcentrics): 4,400 lbs. Type II cord (7 mm kernmantle, Prusik cordage): 2,200 lbs.
## 4. Rope Management and Knots

**4.1. Terms.** Bight (bend that does not cross itself), loop (bend that crosses itself), half hitch, standing end, running end, lay (twist), pigtail (short length left after tying), stacking/flaking, dressing the knot (orienting all parts), setting the knot (tightening all parts). Always mark climbing ropes at their midpoints.

**4.2. Care of rope.** Never step on or drag rope unnecessarily; never let it contact sharp edges; keep it as dry as possible (if wet, hang in large loops above the ground); never leave it knotted or tightly stretched longer than necessary; never let one rope rub continually against another; inspect before, during, and after every use; never mark with paint or let it contact oils or chemicals; never use a climbing rope for any other purpose; never subject it to high heat or flame; clean with mild soap and rinse; coil and hang on wooden pegs, not nails; inspect frequently in loose-rock areas. Maintain an accurate **rope log** for every rope — serial number encodes type (S static / D dynamic), last two digits of manufacture year, a four-digit individual number, length and diameter, and unit code (example: S-96-0001-150/11mm-F2/8). **Any time a dynamic rope is subjected to a fall factor 2, it must not be used again for mountaineering.** Ropes 300–600 feet long should be mountain coiled.

**4.3. Knots, by class.** Class I (end of the rope): square knot (secured with overhand knots both sides), double fisherman's, water/tape knot (pigtails minimum 2 inches), double sheet bend. Class II (anchor knots): bowline (pigtail inside, secured with an overhand), round turn and two half hitches or a bowline, clove hitch (around-the-object and over-the-object). Class IV (special): Prusik (middle-of-the-rope, end-of-the-rope secured with a bowline, French Prusik — never tie a Prusik with tape, it has too little friction), retraced figure eight (tie-in to harness), directional figure eight, slip figure eight (retrievable anchors), Kleimheist, overhand, Münter hitch, timber hitch, mariner's hitch (releasable under load — its advantage over the Prusik), Krägur knot, rappel seat, bowline on a coil (4–6 wraps around the waist), swami wrap (4–6 wraps), three-loop bowline.

**4.4. Knot strength — relative to an unknotted rope at 100%.** Figure 8: 75–80%. Bowline and double bowline: 70–75%. Double fisherman's: 65–70%. Water tape knot: 60–70%. Clove hitch and overhand knot: 60–65%. Square knot: 45%.

**4.5. Knot test time limits.** Most knots: **60 seconds** (30 seconds blindfolded) for unit operations / SML and ACC. End-of-the-line Prusik and retrace figure 8: 90 seconds (45 blindfolded). Rappel (Swiss) seat: 2 minutes (60 seconds blindfolded).
## 5. Anchors and Mechanical Advantage
**5.1. Anchors.** Two types: natural (preferred) and artificial. Rules for both: the anchor must be suitable for the load; its position must suit the direction of pull; **the angle between anchor points must not exceed 90 degrees** — wider angles multiply the load on each point. Natural anchors: trees (sound, not chopped/burned/rotten, at least **6 inches in diameter**; trees on rocky terrain have shallow roots — treat with suspicion), shrubs and bushes (anchor near the base, at the central root), rocks and boulders (stability first; pad sharp edges), spikes and flakes (thump with the heel of the hand — anything that sounds hollow is suspicious; pad sharp edges), threads and chockstones (check contact and symmetry against the direction of pull). Artificial anchors: single timber deadman, picket holdfast, and the equalized anchor built from a minimum of **three pieces** of protection.

**5.2. Mechanical advantage.** The work principle: effort × effort distance = load × load distance. Systems: **1:1** (direct pull), **2:1** (C-pulley), **3:1** (Z-pulley), **9:1** (Z-Z pulley, two Z systems in series). The 9:1 is the workhorse for tightening rope bridges and hauling — built with a 3-foot Prusik (16-inch loop) tied as a French Prusik on the bridge rope and a second Prusik on the bight, clipped through carabiners back to the anchor.
## 6. Rope Installations
**6.1. One-rope bridge.** Site selection: suitable anchors both sides and a crossing point the unit can actually reach. Distance estimation by the azimuth method or the unit-average method (three Marines' best guesses averaged). Organization: bridging team (bridge NCO + one Marine), safety line team (lead swimmer + belay man), mule team (**3 Marines for a 9:1, 6 for a 3:1**), security team (the rest of the party).

**6.2. A-frames.** An artificial device to gain height in a rope installation. Two sturdy poles of approximately equal length; a sling rope comes down **18 inches** from the top of the shortest pole, wraps **6–8 times horizontally** around both poles, hitches down toward the butt, then wraps **4–6 times vertically** between the poles and around the horizontal wraps, and is tied off with the 18-inch pigtail from the clove hitch.

**6.3. Vertical hauling lines.** Purpose: move equipment and troops over vertical obstacles. Site needs a suitable top anchor, good loading/unloading platforms, and clearance for the load at all points. Build with an A-frame or a tree (branch at least **6 inches in diameter**, sound, with a loose swami wrap and two steel locking carabiners, gates opposite and opposed). When personnel are used to gain height instead of an object, the load must not exceed **100 lbs** and the handlers must be secured to the cliff with a safety line.

**6.4. Suspension traverse.** A high-tension rope installation for moving loads across a gap, top to bottom or bottom to top. Needs upper and lower anchors, good platforms, and clearance. **No more than two men tension the installation at any time when using pulleys.** The belay line is rigged at the top with a figure-eight loop at the end and a second figure-eight loop **12–18 inches** from the end; personnel wear a rappel seat clipped into the belay line.
## 7. Rappelling
**7.1. Safety rules.** Rappelling is inherently dangerous. At MWTC two ropes are used for training; all rappels taught can be completed with a single rope if necessary. The rappel point NCOIC ensures anchors and knots are sound, clears loose rock, allows only one man on the loading platform at a time, checks each man's preparation, attaches each rappeller, enforces commands, dispatches each man, is the **last man down**, inspects ropes after every **50 rappels**, and maintains the rope log.

**7.2. Types.** Hasty rappel — moderate slopes, carrying loads, easiest to prepare. Seat-shoulder rappel — heavily laden troops over vertical to near-vertical cliff faces.

**7.3. First man down.** Safety Prusik on the standing end of the rappel rope, rope ends tied together with an overhand knot before deployment, both attached to the harness hard point with locking carabiners. The first man down selects a smooth route, clears loose rock and debris, unties the overhand knot and straightens the ropes once down, belays the next man down, and takes charge of personnel at the bottom.

**7.4. Rescue on rappel.** Self-rescue, buddy rescue, and rappelling casualty rescue — including the method where the rescuer presets a system on a separate rope, attaches the casualty, and cuts the casualty's rappel rope as a last resort. The manual states this is **the only time a knife is used during a rappel, only during an actual rescue, never in training**.

**7.5. Retrievable rappels.** One-rope and two-rope retrievable systems let the last man down recover the rope, so the unit leaves no rope behind on a tactical descent. → `references/anchors-installations-rappel.md`

## 8. Balance Climbing, Top Roping, Harness, Protection
**8.1. Balance climbing.** Safety: never climb more than **10 feet** above the ground; a spotter is required for all balance climbs. Preparation: helmet with serviceable chinstrap, sleeves down, blouse tucked, all watches/rings/jewelry removed, **no gloves** (they slip and give a false feel for the rock), trousers unbloused if they restrict movement, boot soles clean and dry, route with minimal vegetation — never use vegetation for hand or foot holds.

**8.2. Top roping.** Belay stance from the top (sitting belay on the cliff head) or from the bottom (the "yo-yo" or "sling shot" belay). The climber ties in with a retrace figure eight, never out-climbs the belayer (slack in the rope), and wears the weapon across the shoulder, muzzle down and to the left, with a tight sling.

**8.3. Sit harness.** Nomenclature: waist belt, adjustable leg loops, adjustable buttocks straps, two Fastex buckles, doughnut, equipment loops, D-ring (older models), waist belt tie-in point, crotch strap. Tie in at the end of the rope by passing the rope up through the crotch strap and the doughnut and tying a retraceable figure eight close to the body; mid-rope tie-in uses a figure-eight loop and a steel locking carabiner. Expected life **2–3 years** in normal climbing use; **discard any harness that has taken a serious fall**; never tie into the equipment rack of any sit harness as a life-support point.

**8.4. Placing protection.** General rules: carabiner gates down and out (away from the rock), rope running through without twists or kinks, runners untwisted, clip from rear to front so the rope cannot unclip itself. Natural protection: trees, shrubs and bushes, rocks and boulders, spikes and flakes, threads and chockstones. Artificial protection: chocks/stoppers (find a crack with a constriction; the piece must seat so a pull wedges it tighter — never place in a flaring crack; back up #1 and #2 stoppers; stack chocks only as taught), spring-loaded camming devices (place with cams in the middle of their range — a cam over- or under-expanded will fail), and fixed bolts.
## 9. Belaying and Party Climbing
**9.1. Belaying.** Belaying is the procedure by which the belayer controls the rope so a falling climber is caught: the belayer establishes an anchor system, pays out rope keeping minimum slack, and applies the brake with a belay device if the climber falls. Two anchor types: **indirect** (the belayer's body is between the anchor and the climber — the fall load passes through the belayer) and **direct** (the fall load goes straight to the anchor). A hanging belay stance is built on multi-pitch climbs when the leader runs low on rope (about 25 feet of live rope left): four pieces of protection, clove hitches to a master carabiner (MAC), and the rope clipped through in sequence. The belay chain has three elements: anchor, belayer, climber. Two methods of belaying a fall: **static belay** (the belayer holds fast — high impact force on the anchor and climber) and **dynamic belay** (the belayer gives with the fall to absorb energy).

**9.2. Party climbing.** A two-man team is faster than a three-man team; the strongest climber takes the hardest pitch. Route dangers to avoid: wet or icy rock, moss/lichen/grass-covered rock, grass tufts in loose soil, gullies subject to rockfall — and the most common danger, overestimating your own ability. Rules for placing protection: the first-runner rule (a good piece as high as possible just above the belay), then every **10–15 feet** to prevent a long fall, before and after every hard move, whenever in doubt ("stitch it up"), and always back up #1/#2 stoppers.

**9.3. Fall forces.** Impact force is the force the belayer must withstand in a fall; fall factor is the length of the fall divided by the length of rope out — the ratio, not the distance, determines the force. For a **180 lb climber** the manual's table gives: fall factor 0.0 → 360 lbs; 0.4 → 1,137 lbs; 1.0 → 1,676 lbs; 2.0 → 2,288 lbs. The belayer must always be in a position to withstand an impact force of **2,300 lbs**. **Any fall with a fall factor above 1.0 is serious, and the rope should be retired from lead climbing duties.**

**9.4. Night party climbing.** Get eyes on the cliff and complete a cliff sketch before nightfall; use non-verbal commands; drop belay devices to reduce noise and belay with the **Münter hitch**; tape carabiners (without interfering with gate operation) and the sides and ends of size 6–11 hexcentrics to muffle them; dummy-cord the nut pick with 550 cord; use NVGs at the bottom and top of the cliff.

**9.5. Military aid climbing.** A free climber depends entirely on hands and feet; an aid climber uses gear to make upward progress. Reasons to aid: the route is above your ability, fatigue or injury, weather (rain or snow on the rock), or to save energy for the mission or a rescue. Techniques: "hang dogging" and French free (pulling or standing on protection to pass one or two hard moves), and direct aid through multiple moves — slow, gear-heavy work; a 50-meter pitch may require a large rack, and the leader bounce-tests each placement before weighting it.
## 10. Rescue, Steep Earth, and Tree Climbing
**10.1. Rescue techniques for party climbing.** Assess first: is the climber conscious or unconscious? How severe is the injury? Rescue an injured #2 by assisted hoist (most common) or unassisted hoist — a **3:1** system built from the rope and Prusiks. **Escaping from the system** (getting off belay to help): lock the climber off, pass a bight through the belay device carabiner, tie the device off around the load rope with two half hitches, untie your retrace figure eight, and pull the rope end out. Water rescue: **reach, throw, tow, go** — cut the rope only if a man goes under water and no other option exists.

**10.2. Steep earth climbing.** Equipment per climber plus a five-point hand grapnel with at least **20 feet of knotted 7 mm line** for the #2, and an earth ax (a short ice ax for cutting steps). Recon the route for steepness, soil composition, and hazards; climb on a normal belay; the lead digs a belay platform at the end of his rope and plants protection; the #2 follows, collecting unused protection. On top: establish security, select a site with a knotted hand line pre-rigged, bring assault leaders and automatic weapons teams up first, the assistant leader and automatic weapons team last as the 180-degree security, pull up and coil the ropes, and police all evidence (anchors, indentations, rope marks on the edge).

**10.3. Tree climbing (TRAP).** The Tactical Recovery of Aircraft and Personnel mission may require a lead climber to climb a tree to recover a downed pilot. Equipment: harness or sling rope, static rope 165–300 ft, ten aluminum non-locking carabiners, six steel locking carabiners, fifteen assorted runners (longer the better). A corpsman should be present; DOD Form 1833 carries the casualty identification data.
## 11. Fixed Rope Installations

**11.1. Types.** Three cliff-assault rope installations: **simple fixed ropes** (anchored at the top only — quickest and easiest), **semi-fixed ropes** (anchored top and bottom with some slack, used where troops need a safety line), and **fixed ropes** (anchored at both ends and at intermediate points — most time-consuming to establish). An **assault lane** is an alternative to a fixed rope for moving troops up a cliff face.

**11.2. Ascending.** Simple fixed ropes: hand-over-hand. Semi-fixed ropes: the safety Prusik method.

**11.3. Clearing the route.** Once everyone is up, clear the installation: rappel method (from the top), safety Prusik method (from the bottom), or top-rope method (from the bottom).

**11.4. Cable ladders.** Stainless steel cables with aluminum crossbars. Site selection: suitable anchors, good loading/unloading platforms, clearance, and a route the ladder can actually serve.
## 12. Cliff Reconnaissance and Cliff Assault
**12.1. Reconnaissance assets.** Force Reconnaissance Company (MEF level), Reconnaissance Company (division), Surveillance and Target Acquisition (STA) platoon (BLT level), qualified small-boat company scout swimmers, and SEALs. Recon determines mission feasibility (METT), equipment requirements, and the assault force's current capability; it requests aerial reconnaissance and, at a minimum, conducts a detailed map reconnaissance.

**12.2. Cliff sketch.** A pictorial representation of the cliff built from map reconnaissance and on-site observation. The manual states there are seven basic symbols, but lists ten: thin crack, thick crack, left-facing crack, right-facing crack, overhang, ledge, chimney, slab, ramp, and chute.

**12.3. Cliff report — thirteen lines, Alpha through Mike.** Alpha: units of measure. Bravo: date/time the report was completed. The manual's example describes a cliff in feet, reconnoitered at 0800 on 18 January 1998, 250 feet wide, with 6 C-lanes, 3 D-lanes, and 1 F-lane.

**12.4. Cliff assault — planning framework.** Surprise is paramount and silence must be kept to attain it; speed is essential and all ropes available must be used; the cliff head must be well organized; initially the raiding party is very vulnerable. Tactical considerations: the enemy may assume the cliff is not crossable; the cliff-edge-to-objective distance should be within mortar range; enemy security forces may be uncomfortable looking over the edge.

**12.5. Assault sequence — five phases.** Phase One: the first wave arrives at the cliff base and organizes. Phase Two: the first lead climber begins to climb; assault climbers lead two-party climbs while other personnel top-rope, use fixed ropes, vertical hauling lines, or cable ladders. General points: the second wave should precede the first wave with enough time to establish all installations; **three climbing lanes per platoon** for the quickest ascent; cable ladders used to the maximum on all lanes.

## Operating Rules

1. **Plan first, climb second.** BE SAFE MARINE is the planning checklist, not a slogan.
2. **The anchor is everything.** Natural before artificial, direction of pull correct, angle between points never over 90 degrees.
3. **Know your numbers.** Knot strength, fall factor, rope log, and the 2,300-lb belay figure are not trivia — they are the margin.
4. **Two ropes for training, one rope for the mission.** Never train a system more dangerous than the one you will fight with.
5. **Silence is a weapon.** On a cliff assault, noise discipline, taped gear, and the Münter hitch are tactical, not cosmetic.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** TC 3-97.61 (2025) supersedes this 2002 handbook where they conflict; this handbook is the deep assault-climber reference, not the authority.
