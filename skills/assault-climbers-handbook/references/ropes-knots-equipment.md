# Ropes, Knots, and Equipment — Assault Climbers Handbook

## Rope Types and Specifications

All military ropes must meet UIAA standards or U.S. Federal Test Standard 191A. Most ropes have a 5-year shelf life and a maximum 2-year service life.

- **Static (black, kernmantle):** minimum tensile strength 7,500 lbs; maximum elongation 1.5%; 11 mm; sizes 165 ft + 5 ft and 300 ft + 10 ft. Use: rescue operations and bridging, where low elongation under working load is desirable.
- **Dynamic (olive drab, water-resistant treated kernmantle):** minimum tensile strength 6,500 lbs; maximum elongation 6%; 10.5 mm and 11 mm; size 165 ft + 5 ft. Use: lead climbing / party climbing.
- **Maxim Dry Rope (olive drab or multi-colored, water-repellent kernmantle):** minimum tensile strength 3,472 lbs; maximum elongation 6%; 9 mm; size 150 ft + 5 ft. Use: glacier travel / ice climbing.
- **Gold Line II (8-strand braided nylon plymor):** minimum tensile strength 4,500 lbs; maximum elongation 20%; 11 mm; 300 ft or 600 ft spools. Use: sling ropes and litters only.

Sling ropes are made from 15-foot lengths of plymor or dynamic rope only. 25-foot practice coils should be constructed with static rope (dynamic may be used).

## Nylon Rope Properties

- High strength-to-weight ratio; good energy absorption in dynamic ropes; flexible; rot resistant, not affected by frost.
- Low melting point: nylon fuses at 400°F and melts at 480°F. Temperatures as low as 250°F will damage a nylon rope.
- Nylon stretches under tension and ruptures at between 30% and 70% elongation, depending on construction.
- Nylon rope loses as much as 30% strength when wet. Susceptible to abrasions and cuts; affected by chemicals and light.
- Manila rope by comparison: easily gripped, hard wearing, does not deteriorate in heat — but heavy, kinks (especially when wet), absorbs water and swells, burns above 300°F, and is edible by rodents.

## Webbing, Carabiners, Protection

- **1-inch tubular nylon webbing:** tensile strength approximately 4,000–4,500 lbs depending on manufacturer. All rules that apply to nylon rope apply to tubular nylon webbing.
- **Pre-sewn Spectra runners:** tensile strength approximately 5,500 lbs.
- **Carabiners (snaplinks), locking and non-locking.** Purposes: attach ropes or runners to protection; attach the rappel rope to the rappel seat; attach an individual's safety rope to a safety line; form field-expedient pulley systems. Steel locking "D": minimum tensile strength 5,500 lbs. Steel-locking oval Stubai 82: 3,300 lbs — not in the MACK, obsolete, used at MWTC only to save money even though beyond service life. Nomenclature (non-locking): gate, gate pivot pin, locking pin, body. Locking adds a locking notch and locking nut. Do not drop a carabiner — it may be damaged invisibly. Serviceability check before use; preventive maintenance per the MACK manual.
- **Protection strengths (MWTC):** #1/#2 stopper (wired) 350 kg (~770 lbs); #3–#5 stopper (wired) 650 kg (~1,430 lbs); #6–#12 stopper (wired) 1,100 kg (~2,420 lbs); #1–#3 hexcentrics (wired) 1,100 kg (~2,420 lbs). Spring-loaded camming devices: large 2,400 lbs minimum, small 2,600 lbs minimum (vary by manufacturer and size). Type I cord, 5.5 mm Spectra/Kevlar: 4,400 lbs (for wiring hexcentrics). Type II cord, 7 mm kernmantle: 2,200 lbs (Prusik cordage).
- **Other equipment:** caving ladder (stainless steel cables, aluminum crossbars); ascender (placed/removed one-handed, rope passes one way); rescue pulley (two independent side plates); helmets (falling rock and impacts); belay devices. Rack protection, quick draws, and other equipment organized on the climbing harness so each piece is reachable with either hand.

## Rope Care, Inspection, and the Rope Log

- Do not step on or drag rope unnecessarily — small particles of dirt and grit cut fibers. Never let rope contact sharp edges. Keep rope as dry as possible; if wet, hang in large loops above the ground. Never leave rope knotted or tightly stretched longer than necessary. Never allow one rope to rub continually against another. Inspect before each use for frayed or cut spots, mildew, rot, or defects. Mark all climbing ropes at their midpoints. Never mark with paint or allow contact with oils or chemicals. A climbing rope is NEVER used for any purpose except climbing. Cut ends with the rope cutter (melt/whip to prevent unraveling). Never subject rope to high heat or flame. Clean with mild soap, rinse thoroughly. Coil and hang on wooden pegs rather than nails or hooks. In loose-rock areas, inspect frequently for cuts and abrasions.
- **Rope log:** maintain an accurate record for every rope. Serial number format: type (S static / D dynamic) + last two digits of manufacture year + four-digit individual number + length and diameter + responsible unit code. Example: S-96-0001-150/11mm-F2/8 — a static rope manufactured in 1996, first rope issued by Fox Company 2/8, 150 ft, 11 mm. Log date of manufacture, date in service, and the date and number of each use (climbs, falls, top ropes, rappels), remarks, and who inspected it. **Any time a dynamic rope is subjected to a fall factor 2, that rope must not be used again for mountaineering.** Ropes 300–600 feet long should be mountain coiled.

## Coiling and Throwing

- **Mountain coil:** for carrying rope over a pack or over a climber's shoulder. **Butterfly coil:** for carrying when the individual needs the rope quickly available; made with two-foot bights on both sides and 6–8 feet remaining to tie and carry.
- **Rope throwing:** stack/flake the rope, anchor the standing end, take 6–8 coils in the strong arm, call "STAND-BY FOR ROPE", throw overhand or sidearm hard enough to reach the target. If wind or a tree misdirects the throw, reorganize and re-deploy.

## Knots

**Terms:** bight (bend, rope does not cross itself); loop (bend, rope crosses itself); half hitch (loop binding on itself around an object); standing end (anchored, unusable part); running end (free, working part); lay (twist of the rope); pigtail (short length left after tying); stacking/flaking; dressing the knot (orienting all parts); setting the knot (tightening all parts).

**Class I — end of the rope:** square knot (tie ends of two ropes of equal diameter; secure with overhand knots on both sides); double fisherman's knot (self-locking, ties two equal-diameter ropes; can tighten beyond untying); water/tape knot (secures webbing — overhand knot in one end of the tape, thread the other end through from the opposite direction; dress down; pigtails minimum 2 inches); double sheet bend (two or more ropes of equal or unequal diameter).

**Class II — anchor knots:** bowline (fixed loop in the end of a rope; pigtail always on the inside, secured with an overhand); round turn and two half hitches, or round turn and a bowline (360-degree contact around an object, distributes load over a small-diameter anchor); clove hitch (adjustable hitch; around-the-object and over-the-object forms).

**Class III — middle of the rope:** figure-eight loop (strong, readily untied after loading); double figure-eight loop (double loop splits the load between two loops, reducing wear and strength loss at the carabiner); two-loop bowline (self-equalizing belay, or tying the middle person in on a three-person rope).

**Class IV — special knots:** Prusik (friction knot, set and released; tie with smaller-diameter cord on larger-diameter rope; add wraps if it slips; never tie with tape — too little friction): middle-of-the-rope Prusik (endless loop / Prusik cord), end-of-the-rope Prusik (always secured with a bowline), French Prusik (Prusik cord or single strand with figure-eight loops on each end). Retraced figure eight (tie the end of the climbing rope into a harness or swami wrap; pigtail may or may not be secured with an overhand). Directional figure eight (loop points toward the direction of pull when standing and running ends are tensioned). Slip figure eight (retrievable anchors and fixed rope installations — easy to untie). Kleimheist (friction knot). Overhand knot (secures primary knots). Münter hitch (simple hitch in a carabiner to put friction on the line — the noise-free belay for night work). Timber hitch (fixes a rope to a pole for hoisting/towing; casts off easily). Mariner's hitch (advantage over the Prusik: it can be released under load). Krägur knot (friction knot preventing slippage on a rope of the same diameter). Rappel seat (expedient support harness — sling rope centered on the left hip, wrapped around the waist with half hitches, running ends down through the legs and up over the buttocks, tied with a square knot and two overhand knots on the left hip). Bowline on a coil (first and last men on a climbing rope; 4–6 wraps around the waist; distributes fall force over a larger area; also takes up excess rope; overhand knot behind it). Swami wrap (4–6 wraps of rope or webbing around the waist; middle of the sling rope in the small of the back, wrap until pigtails are 15–20 inches, tie on the left side with a square knot and two overhand knots). Three-loop bowline (three bights, each adjustable against the others).

## Knot Strength and Test Times

Rope, cordage, and webbing are strongest loaded in a straight line; bending to form a knot reduces strength. Relative strength (unknotted rope = 100%): figure 8, 75–80%; bowline, 70–75%; double bowline, 70–75%; double fisherman's, 65–70%; water tape knot, 60–70%; clove hitch, 60–65%; overhand knot, 60–65%; square knot, 45%. Dress all knots properly for maximum effective strength.

Knot test time limits (unit operations / SML and ACC; blindfolded times in parentheses): square knot, double fisherman's, water/tape knot, round turn and a bowline, round turn and two half hitches, clove hitch (around the object), Münter hitch, slip figure 8, figure 8 loop, directional figure 8 — 60 seconds (30 seconds). End-of-the-line Prusik, retrace figure 8 — 90 seconds (45 seconds). Rappel (Swiss) seat — 2 minutes (60 seconds).

Source: Assault Climbers Handbook, Ch. 3 (Nomenclature and Care of Mountaineering Equipment) and Ch. 4 (Rope Management), U.S. Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center, Summer 2002.
