# Climbing, Rescue, and Cliff Assault — Assault Climbers Handbook

## Balance Climbing

**Safety precautions:** never climb more than 10 feet above the ground; a spotter is required for all balance climbs.

**Individual preparations:** helmet with serviceable chinstrap; sleeves rolled down for hand and arm freedom; blouse tucked in; all watches, rings, and jewelry removed; gloves NOT worn (they slip and give a false feel for the rock); trousers unbloused if they restrict movement; boot soles clean and dry; route selected where vegetation is minimal — never use vegetation for hand or foot holds.

**Spotter duties:** positions himself directly behind the climber before the climb starts; maintains position facing the cliff, directly below the climber; stands with feet shoulder-width apart and arms ready to stop the climber's fall; if the climber falls, the spotter does not "catch" him — he prevents the climber from striking the ground head-first; never allows anyone to come between himself and the face. Commands: "ROCK" — all personnel in the vicinity take cover from falling objects.

**Actions if falling:** sound the command "falling"; push away from the rock face; maintain proper body position (feet below, arms out, land on the feet and roll).

**Five basic holds:** push holds (pressing down or out against the rock); pull holds (pulling down on an edge or pocket); foot holds (edges and smears the boot stands on); friction holds (relying on friction between boot or body and rock — smearing); jam holds (wedging any part of the body — hand, fist, foot — into a crack). **Combination holds:** chimney climbing (whole body inserted into a crack, pressing outward against both walls); lie-back (pull holds with the hands, friction holds with the feet, leaning away from the crack); push-pull (a push hold and a pull hold used together); mantling (pressing down on a ledge to raise the body without moving the hands); cross-pressure in cracks (both hands in the same crack, pressing in opposite directions); inverted (pull or push with the body upside down); pinch (gripping a small nubbin between thumb and fingers); stemming (spreading arms or legs to maintain body position, usually in a corner or chimney).

**General use of holds:** most handholds can be used as footholds as you move up; use all holds possible to conserve energy; even small projections may be used; do not use knees or elbows (skin on bone — painful and likely to slip). **Movement on slab** is based on friction holds: use every irregularity for additional friction; traversing requires both hands and feet; descending steep slab may require turning inboard to face the slab and backing down; the biggest mistake is leaning into the rock — maintain maximum friction by keeping the body out and the weight over the feet. **Body position:** climb in balance, weight centered over the feet, hips close to the rock only when it helps, arms straight and relaxed where possible — arms are for balance, legs do the work.

## Top Roping

**Belay stance from the top:** the belayer establishes a sitting belay stance on the cliff head — anchored, rope running over the edge to the climber below, brake hand controlling the rope. **Securing the climber:** the climber ties into the end of the top rope with a retrace figure eight (alternative methods as taught). **Belay stance from the bottom:** the "yo-yo" or "sling shot" belay — the rope runs from the belayer at the bottom, up through an anchor at the top of the climb, and back down to the climber.

**Climber's responsibilities:** ensure the belayer is anchored and on belay (by command); never out-climb the belayer (slack in the rope is dangerous); avoid placing excess pressure/weight on the belay man; weapons worn across the shoulder, muzzle down and to the left, with a tight sling. **Climbing commands and signals:** the standard top-rope calls ("On belay", "Climbing", "Climb", "Slack", "Tension", "Falling", "Off belay") as listed in the manual.

## Sit Harness

**Nomenclature:** waist belt; leg loops (adjustable); buttocks straps (adjustable); two Fastex buckles; doughnut; equipment loops; D-ring (older models); waist belt tie-in point; crotch strap.

**Wearing:** disconnect the Fastex buckle at the rear; step through the leg loops with the doughnut and tie-in point at the front; fasten the waist belt snug but comfortable; adjust leg loops high on the thigh; the harness should be a comfortable but snug fit — if it is loose it will ride up in a fall.

**Tying in, end of rope:** pass the end of the climbing rope up through the crotch strap, then through the doughnut, and tie a retraceable figure eight, adjusting the knot to get it as close to the body as possible. **Tying in, middle of the rope:** take up a bight, tie a figure-eight loop, and clip a steel locking carabiner through the loop and the harness tie-in point.

**Care and maintenance:** avoid chemicals (they damage nylon); inspect regularly for abrasion and wear, especially where rope and webbing rub; keep away from heat (open flames, cigarettes); if soiled by grit or sea water, wash in lukewarm water with pure soap and allow to dry in the shade. Expected life: 2–3 years in normal climbing use. **A harness that has experienced a serious fall should be discarded.** Under no circumstances tie into the equipment rack of any sit harness as a life-support point.

## Placing Protection

**General rules:** the gates of carabiners clipped into protection should be down and out (away from the rock); the rope should run from the climber through the carabiner without twists and kinks; when using a runner, be sure it is not twisted before clipping in.

**Natural features as protection:** trees; shrubs and bushes; rocks and boulders; spikes and flakes; threads and chockstones — the same evaluation as for anchors (sound, stable, direction of pull correct, sharp edges padded).

**Artificial protection:** **chocks** — find a crack with a constriction; the piece must seat so a pull wedges it tighter; a correct stopper placement fills the constriction, an incorrect one sits in a flaring crack where a pull will extract it; back up #1 and #2 stoppers with a second piece; stack chocks only as taught; chocks work in horizontal cracks as well as vertical ones. **Spring-loaded camming devices (SLCD)** — hold like a syringe, pull the retractor, place in the crack, release; the cams must be in the middle of their range — over-expanded cams can walk out, under-expanded cams have no holding power. **Fixed bolts** — a bolt and hanger drilled into the rock; the most secure fixed protection where it exists, but placement requires a hammer drill and time.

**Attaching the rope to the protection:** the gate faces away from the rock; the gate faces down and away; clip the carabiner from the rear to the front (so the rope runs from the back of the carabiner forward and cannot unclip itself on the rock); the rope must run smoothly without twists; done correctly, the rope from the retrace figure eight runs straight through the carabiner with no kinks.

**Racking equipment:** a climber's selection of gear is called a rack. Rack several pieces of the same size on one carabiner, grouped by size on the harness; long slings are worn doubled over and clipped with a carabiner; quick draws are preset carabiner-and-runner combinations for fast clipping; other items (stitch plate, rappel gloves, chock pick) are placed at accessible points on the harness.

## Belaying for Party Climbing

**Concept:** belaying is the procedure by which the belayer controls the rope so a falling climber is caught — the belayer establishes an anchor system, pays out rope keeping minimum slack, and applies the brake with a belay device if the climber falls. When the leader reaches the top he establishes a belay stance to top-rope the second up.

**Types of belay anchors:** indirect belay (the belayer's position is between the anchor and the climber — the fall load passes through the belayer's body); direct belay (the impact force of a falling climber goes directly to the anchor, not through the belayer).

**Elements of a belay chain:** the anchor (the attachment to the mountain); the belayer (the static climber who manages the rope); the climber (who climbs the route and also places protection, manages the rope, and communicates).

**Belayer's responsibilities:** ensure anchors are sound and aligned with the direction of pull; locate the exact belay position; when possible, offset the stance slightly from the leader's intended line; back-stack the climbing rope near the stance; tie in with a retrace figure eight to the harness hard point.

**Establishing the belay:** natural anchor belay stance using the rope; natural anchor belay stance using slings/runners (two natural anchors); artificial anchor belay stance; combined natural and artificial anchor belay stance. In each case the anchor is equalized, the belayer is tied in, and the belay device is placed where the brake hand can hold a fall.

**Methods of belaying a fall:** static belay (hold fast — the full impact force goes to the anchor, the rope, and the climber); dynamic belay (give with the fall to absorb energy — reduces peak force but lets the climber fall farther).

**Disassembly:** on "Off belay", remove the live rope from the belay device; the leader pulls up rope until taut against the #2's MAC; #2 calls "That's me"; the leader secures the rope to his belay device and calls "On belay"; #2 disassembles the stance in reverse order, stows gear, calls "Climbing"; the leader answers "Climb, climber"; #2 climbs and the roles switch. **Retrieving protection:** #2 calls "Point" at each piece, the belayer answers "Point", #2 removes and stows the piece, calls "Climbing", the belayer answers "Climb, climber" — repeated to the top.

**Hanging belay stance:** on multi-pitch climbs, when the belayer has about 25 feet of live rope left, the leader locates a position, calls "Point" (answered "Point"), and builds a stance: a piece of protection placed to take a downward pull, the rope from the retrace figure eight attached to it, a MAC clipped to the retrace figure eight, an around-the-object clove hitch tied taut from the protection to the MAC, second and third pieces placed in the downward direction of pull and clipped through, a non-locking carabiner on the MAC, and a fourth piece placed in the upward direction of pull and clipped in. The leader then calls "Off belay", clips a steel locking carabiner into the MAC as the belay point, pulls up the rope and back-stacks it, and on "That's me" attaches the live rope and calls "On belay". **Changing over gear:** the most efficient multi-pitch method is for the leader and #2 to exchange only what is needed at the stance rather than re-racking everything.

## Party Climbing

**The belay:** where the belayer establishes the belay matters — position, anchor quality, and line of the route are chosen together. **Dangers when selecting a route:** wet or icy rock; rock overgrown with moss, lichen, or grass; tufts of grass and small bushes growing from loosely packed soil; gullies subject to rockfall; the most common danger is overestimating your own ability.

**Lead climber's responsibilities:** pre-select the probable route; ensure he has the proper equipment; ensure climber and #2 are tied into their respective ends of the rope; ensure the #2 has selected proper anchors for the belay; construct a gear rack to carry the equipment.

**Rules for placing protection:** first-runner rule — a good piece of protection as high as possible just above the belay; every 10 to 15 feet, to prevent a long fall; before and after a hard move; whenever the climber feels protection is needed — when in doubt, stitch it up; when placing a #1 or #2 stopper, always back it up. **Preventing rope drag:** place protection so the rope follows as straight a line as possible; use runners and quick draws of the correct length to reduce friction — drag can hold the leader back, pull protection out, and make the belay unmanageable.

**Considerations for party climbing:** a two-man climbing team is faster than a three-man team; the strongest climber always takes the hardest pitch; climbers tie in with a retrace figure eight and do not untie until the climb is finished; use the correct climbing commands to prevent confusion; where possible, use natural anchors for protection; the leader must not climb to the length of the rope before selecting a good belay stance — stop with rope in hand and a stance available; a traverse is protected so the second is covered in both directions (protection placed to hold a fall in either direction of travel).

**Climbing commands:** verbal commands as listed in the manual (including "On belay", "Climbing", "Climb", "Slack", "Tension", "Watch me", "Falling", "Rock", "Off belay", "That's me", "Point"); non-verbal commands — rope tugs and visual signals — for tactical cliff assaults where silence is required.

**Fall forces:** impact force (shock force) is the force the belayer has to sustain in a fall. Fall factor is the length of the fall divided by the length of rope out — a factor-2 fall (falling twice the rope length, e.g., falling past the belay) is the worst possible. Relationship of fall factor and impact force for a 180 lb climber: 0.0 → 360 lbs; 0.1 → 683 lbs; 0.2 → 868 lbs; 0.4 → 1,137 lbs; 0.6 → 1,347 lbs; 0.8 → 1,521 lbs; 1.0 → 1,676 lbs; 1.2 → 1,817 lbs; 1.4 → 1,947 lbs; 1.6 → 2,067 lbs; 1.8 → 2,181 lbs; 2.0 → 2,288 lbs. The less a climber weighs, the less impact force; the more, the more. To minimize the seriousness of a fall: the belayer must always be in a position to withstand an impact force of 2,300 lbs; place protection more frequently during the first half of the climb; belay after a series of cruxes rather than before them (belays after cruxes minimize fall factors); always use a seat harness to distribute the forces of a fall; any fall involving a fall factor in excess of 1.0 should be regarded as serious and the rope retired from lead climbing duties.

**Night party climbing:** get eyes on the cliff and complete a cliff sketch before nightfall; all party-climbing considerations apply at night; use non-verbal commands; get rid of all belay devices to reduce noise — use the Münter hitch for belaying; tape carabiners to reduce noise (without interfering with gate operation); tape the sides and ends of size 6–11 hexcentrics to muffle them; use 550 cord attached to the nut pick to extend it from the chest pocket; use NVGs at the bottom and top of the cliff to help with spotting and route-finding.

## Military Aid Climbing

**Introduction:** a free climber depends entirely on hands and feet; an aid climber uses equipment to make upward progress. **Reasons for aid:** the route is above your ability (the recon picked a route that looked climbable but is not); fatigue or injury (a simple 5.6 becomes impossible when it is sustained, or after a long approach); weather (rain or snow arrives at the cliff); save energy for the mission or for rescues (a cliff assault is a means to an objective, not a climbing exercise).

**Gear:** etriers, also called aiders — ladder-like slings that let the climber step up to the next piece of protection; daisy chain — webbing or cordage with clip-in loops along its entire length, used to connect the climber to protection and to adjust position; fifi hook, the "arm saver" — a hook the lead climber uses to rest while placing the next piece.

**The rack:** for a full pitch or more of aid, the amount of protection needed for a 50-meter pitch may be large — rack accordingly; slings add weight and bulk, so carry them efficiently.

**Techniques:** the simplest forms are "hang dogging" and French free — using protection to pull up or stand up on to pass one or two hard moves. Aiding through multiple moves is more difficult and time-consuming: place a piece, clip in the etrier and daisy chain, step up, place the next piece, bounce-test each placement (a sharp downward bounce on the etrier) before trusting it, rest on the fifi hook when needed. Due to the extreme time direct aid takes, the leader, once at the belay, should bring the second up as quickly as possible and keep the team moving.

## Rescue Techniques for Party Climbing

**Assessing the situation:** a party-climb rescue can become time-consuming and dangerous — assess before acting. Is the climber conscious or unconscious? How severe is the injury? Where is the injured climber — less than half the rope length away, or more? Do you have enough gear to conduct the rescue? Can other climbing teams in the area assist? How familiar are the climbers with the rescue techniques?

**Escaping from the system:** lock the climber off; pass a bight through the belay device carabiner; tie the belay device off around the load rope with two half hitches; untie your retraced figure eight and pull the end of the rope out through your harness. You are now free to move while the injured climber remains secured.

**Rescue an injured lead climber:** when conducting a two-party climb, the method of lowering depends on two factors — the distance to the injured climber and the gear available. Lower the injured climber to the ground or to a stance where first aid can be given; assistance from other climbing teams in the area can provide extra rope, gear, and hands.

**Rescue an injured #2:** assisted hoist (most frequently used — the leader hauls the #2 up with a mechanical advantage system while the #2 assists by climbing) or unassisted hoist (the leader hauls alone — a 3-to-1 hoist built from the rope, Prusiks, and carabiners).

## Steep Earth Climbing

**Equipment:** each climber needs standard climbing gear; the #2 carries a five-point hand grapnel with at least 20 feet of knotted 7 mm line; an earth ax — a short ice ax used to cut steps or as a handhold in steep earth.

**Climbing procedures:** recon the route — steepness, soil composition, hazards; normal belay procedures are used, the belayer establishing the bottom of the climb; the lead climber ties in and begins, digging steps and handholds; the lead digs a belay platform at the end of his rope if required and plants protection; the #2 climbs toward the belay stance, collecting all unused protection; the procedure continues until one climber reaches the top and establishes a belay stance.

**Use of the grapnel:** a five-point grapnel can be useful in steep earth. Unwind the grapnel line, secure it to yourself, throw the grapnel to catch above, then climb the difficult section assisted by the grapnel line, keeping the line taut. Once at the top, check the security of the grapnel and change its position if needed.

**Duties on the bottom:** troops at the bottom should not be directly under the climbing site because of loose soil and rock. **Duties once on top:** establish security; select a site with a knotted hand line pre-rigged and brought up by the #2; assault leaders and automatic weapons teams go up first; the assistant leader and automatic weapons team are last, providing 180-degree security; pull up and coil the ropes; police all evidence — anchors, indentations, rope marks on the edge of the dirt, and anything else that would reveal the climb.

## Tree Climbing Techniques (TRAP)

**Mission:** the Tactical Recovery of Aircraft and Personnel (TRAP) is an ACE (Aviation Combat Element) mission; for an assault climber it may require a lead climber to climb a tree to recover a downed pilot.

**Equipment:** harness or sling rope; static rope, 165–300 ft; ten aluminum non-locking carabiners; six steel locking carabiners; fifteen assorted runners (longer the better); optional items as the situation requires.

**Ascending techniques:** lead climbing — the lead climber ascends the tree using one of four methods (branch climbing, using the rope as a handline, etc.); **body thrusting** — very tiresome but requires little gear (arms pull, legs thrust the body up the rope); **footlocking** — also tiresome, little gear (feet lock the rope and stand in it); **Jumar/Texas kick** — the easiest method but requires the most gear (mechanical ascenders, one on the rope and one on a foot loop); **haul system** — the most desired method, requires very little gear but needs a second man to haul.

**Lowering technique:** once the leader has ascended to the appropriate height, anchor the rope at height; feed the rope through the anchor for lowering; tie a figure eight into one end; double-check the system — all slack out, a belay on. **Other considerations:** a corpsman should be present to provide medical attention; DOD Form 1833 (the classified report with key casualty identification information) must be completed.

## Cliff Reconnaissance

**Reconnaissance assets:** Force Reconnaissance Company (MEF level asset); Reconnaissance Company (division); Surveillance and Target Acquisition (STA) platoon (BLT level); qualified small-boat company scout swimmers; SEALs (NAVSPECCWARGRP); other units in the theater as available.

**Cliff reconnaissance:** determines mission feasibility at the mission-planning stages (METT); determines equipment requirements and the assault force's current capability; requests aerial reconnaissance; at a minimum conducts a detailed map reconnaissance; the recon unit must be thoroughly familiar with the assault techniques to be used; gathers essential data on site (route, anchors, rock, hazards, enemy) and by observation.

**Operational security:** of utmost importance. The reconnaissance element should not climb the intended cliff — that would compromise it. If the recon unit remains in place, it maintains surveillance of the breach site and facilitates the arrival of the assault force.

**Cliff sketch:** a pictorial representation of the cliff. Equipment: map, compass, protractor, pencil, paper, and optics. Marginal information is placed on the sketch (units, scale, orientation, location, date). Construction: draw the cliff face from map reconnaissance and on-site observation, marking routes, hazards, and features. The manual states seven basic symbols but lists ten: thin crack, thick crack, left-facing crack, right-facing crack, overhang, ledge, chimney, slab, ramp, chute. Ramp, slab, and chute are written on the sketch with dimensions (width, depth, height) so the number of lanes possible can be read off. Only details of military importance are added.

**Cliff report — thirteen lines, Alpha through Mike:** Alpha — units of measure (code for feet or meters). Bravo — date/time the report was completed. Charlie — cliff location, given at cliff center and expressed as a grid. Delta — width of the cliff head, in the units stated in Alpha. Echo — cliff height, in the same units. Foxtrot — obstacles at the base of the cliff (coded by type). Golf — rock type, if known. Hotel — military classification of climbs, if determined. India — hazards on the cliff face (coded). Juliet — number and types of lanes (coded by lane type). Kilo — is an A-frame needed: yes or no. Lima — enemy situation, in SALUTE-R format. Mike — remarks/comments, any data not covered by the other lines. Example in the manual: a cliff in feet reconnoitered at 0800 on 18 January 1998, 250 feet wide, with obstacles coded 2, 4 A and 2 B, and 6 C-lanes, 3 D-lanes, 1 F-lane.

## Cliff Assault

**Planning framework:** a cliff assault is a thoroughly planned action on a vertical obstacle. Surprise is paramount and silence must be kept to attain it. Speed is essential, and all ropes available must be used. The cliff head must be well organized. Initially the raiding party is very vulnerable.

**Reconnaissance considerations:** recon teams identify climbing points, suitable top and bottom anchors, and are able to direct assault climbers to the routes.

**Tactical considerations:** if an enemy objective is in close proximity to a cliff, the enemy could assume the cliff is not crossable and therefore leave it lightly defended; the cliff-edge-to-objective distance should be within mortar range, so the assault force has fire support from the moment it tops out; enemy security forces may not be comfortable looking over the edge of a cliff, limiting their observation downward.

**Deception plan:** use noise factors and diversionary attacks. Rivers or ocean waves breaking at the base of cliffs mask sound; weapons fire and supporting-arms impacts mask movement; shelling on or near enemy positions on a regular basis desensitizes the enemy; diversionary attacks by ground, air, or indirect fire from multiple directions draw attention away; the cliff assault itself can be used as the diversionary attack for a main effort elsewhere.

**Communications:** wire as the primary mode, radio as the alternative — intra-squad radios have no encryption and can be intercepted, so caution is required. A no-comms plan is designed on standard signals per the unit's SOP. Key personnel have a radio operator and/or a runner.

**Fire support plan:** use the attacking unit's organic mortars first; artillery if in range of the cliff assault; the forward air controller for rotary and fixed-wing close air support; naval gunfire can be utilized but be aware of its limitations near a cliff face (flat trajectory and danger close).

**Amphibious considerations:** advance-force reconnaissance operations; hydrographic surveys and confirmatory beach reports; all landing vehicles/craft spread-loaded (so the loss of one craft does not lose a capability); debarkation done quickly.

**Organization:** the assault force is organized into climbing teams, the raid force, security, and support as the situation requires; the cliff head is organized so each element knows its position and task on arrival.

**Assault climbing techniques:** two-party climb for assault climbers, all other personnel top-rope; two-party climb for assault climbers, all other personnel go up fixed rope installations; two-party climb for assault climbers, all other personnel/equipment utilize vertical hauling lines; two-party climb for assault climbers, all other personnel utilize cable ladders.

**Assault sequence, five phases:** Phase One — the first wave arrives at the cliff base and organizes. Phase Two — begins as soon as the first lead climber begins to climb; assault climbers lead, the rest of the force follows on the installations. Phase Three — arrival of the second wave for their ascent of the cliff. Phase Four — departure of the raid force from the cliff head toward the objective. Phase Five — withdrawal: if the mission dictates a withdrawal via the cliff face, the force descends the same installations in reverse.

**General points:** the second wave should precede the first wave with ample time to establish all installations; three climbing lanes per platoon for the quickest ascent; cable ladders utilized to the maximum for all climbing lanes; communications between the cliff point NCO and the beachmaster via landline; communications between the cliff head officer, cliff assault coordinator, and assault climbers at the cliff base by the means in the SOP.
