# Anchors, Mechanical Advantage, and Rope Installations — Assault Climbers Handbook

## Anchors

**Two types:** natural (preferred) and artificial. General considerations for both: the anchor must be suitable for the load; its position must suit the direction of pull; **the angle between anchor points must not exceed 90 degrees** — a wider angle puts added stress on the anchors and the equipment. To decrease the angle, extend the anchor with sling ropes, web runners, or Prusik cord.

**Natural anchors:**
- **Trees:** select a tree that has not been chopped, burned, or is rotten; at least 6 inches in diameter and strong enough for the intended load. Trees growing on rocky terrain have shallow roots — treat with suspicion. Pad soft, sap-producing trees to keep sap off ropes and slings.
- **Shrubs and bushes:** select one that is alive, not brittle, charred, or loose; locate the central root and build the anchor as near to the base as possible to avoid leverage.
- **Rocks and boulders:** stability is prime — strong and secure enough for the load; inspect all surfaces for rough or sharp points and pad them to protect the rope.
- **Spikes and flakes:** thump with the heel of the hand — anything that sounds hollow is suspicious; check for cracks and weathering; pad sharp edges.
- **Threads and chockstones:** a thread is a hole weathered or cracked through the rock; a chockstone is a rock wedged in a crack. Check a thread by thumping and visual inspection. A chockstone must have substantial contact with the crack and symmetry matching the intended direction of pull. Do not wedge slings between a chockstone and the rock wall.
- **Natural high-tension anchors:** for rope bridges and other high-tension installations, use primary and secondary natural anchor points — two independent anchor points, each capable of holding the load.

**Artificial anchor systems:**
- **Single timber deadman:** constructed in the ground — a timber buried in a trench, the rope attached at its center, backfilled and tamped.
- **Picket holdfast:** easier to construct than a deadman; pickets driven into the ground at an angle, lashed together, the load rope attached low.
- **Equalized anchor:** built with a minimum of three pieces of protection, equalized so the load is shared.

## Mechanical Advantage

**Theory:** a load suspended from two ropes shares its weight between them; a pulley changes the direction of pull and, rigged as a system, multiplies the force applied. The work principle: effort × effort distance = load × load distance — you trade distance pulled for force gained.

**Systems (construction and use):**
- **1:1 ratio:** direct pull, no mechanical advantage — one unit of effort moves one unit of load one unit of distance.
- **2:1 ratio (C-pulley):** the rope runs from the anchor, around a pulley attached to the load, back to the puller — half the effort, twice the rope pulled.
- **3:1 ratio (Z-pulley):** the rope runs anchor → load pulley → anchor pulley → puller, forming a Z; a Prusik on the bridge rope acts as the traveling pulley's progress capture so the system does not slip back between pulls.
- **9:1 ratio (Z-Z pulley):** two 3:1 systems in series — the workhorse for tightening rope bridges and heavy hauling. Build it with a 3-foot Prusik (16-inch loop) tied as a French Prusik on the bridge rope, a bight clipped into a carabiner at the anchor, a second French Prusik on the bottom end of the bight, and the running end pulled through — approximately 9:1.

## One-Rope Bridge

**Site selection:** two criteria — suitable anchors on both sides and a crossing point the unit can reach and use. Other considerations: the gap's width, current, bank conditions, and concealment.

**Distance estimation:** the azimuth method (shoot an azimuth to a point on the far side and compute the distance) or the unit-average method (three Marines judge the distance; average the guesses).

**Organization:** bridging team (bridge NCO + one Marine); safety line team (lead swimmer + belay man); mule team (3 Marines for a 9:1, 6 Marines for a 3:1); security team (the rest of the party).

**Construction:** the safety line team gets the lead swimmer across on a belay; the swimmer establishes the far-side anchor and secures the bridging line. On the near side, the bridging team builds the mechanical advantage (9:1 as above) and the mule team tensions the rope as much as possible; the bridge NCO sets the brake knot — a bight with a complete round turn on the body of the rope, locked off — and the bridge is tight and secured.

**Crossing:** the horizontal traverse. Rappel seat method (rappel seat and carabiner on the bridge rope, pulled across hand over hand) or pulley method (a pulley on the bridge rope — used when the bridge is long, uphill, or speed matters). Helmets worn for all methods; gloves optional.

**Rescue, in order:** **Reach** (pole, hand, anything within reach); **Throw** (throw a rope to the victim); **Tow** (pull the victim in with a rope); **Go** (a rescuer goes after the victim). **Cut** — if a man goes underwater on a rope over water and no other option exists, cut the rope. Cutting is a last resort.

**Retrieval:** before the last Marine crosses, make the bridge retrievable — break down the mechanical advantage with the braking knot set, take the rope around the back of the anchor and tie a slip figure eight, pull the running end until it bites, untie the swami wrap, bring the safety line up and attach it to the end of the bridging line, cross the last two Marines, bring the safety line up from downstream, take off the tree wrap, and pull the safety line — the bridge is retrieved.

## A-Frames

**Definition:** an artificial device used to gain height in a rope installation, built from two poles when no natural height (tree, rock) is available.

**Materials:** two sturdy poles of approximately the same length; a sling rope; a spreader rope/bar; anchor ropes.

**Construction:** place the two poles side by side with butt ends flush. Take a sling rope and come down 18 inches from the top of the shortest pole, leaving an 18-inch pigtail. Wrap the sling rope 6–8 times horizontally around both poles, hitch down toward the butt ends, then wrap 4–6 times vertically between the poles and around the horizontal wraps. Tie off with the 18-inch pigtail from the clove hitch. Install a spreader rope/bar between the poles to hold them apart, anchor the butt ends so they cannot slip, and anchor and tie off the A-frame. Task-organize: a team builds the A-frame while others prepare the installation it will serve.

## Vertical Hauling Lines

**Purpose:** move equipment and troops over vertical obstacles. **Site selection:** a suitable top anchor; good loading and unloading platforms; sufficient clearance for the load at all points; if using an A-frame, the butt ends must be anchorable.

**Construction with an A-frame:** build the A-frame to support the load and provide adequate height; anchor it with one static rope; build the hauling line (two methods — a fixed hauling line or a moving hauling line) and a knotted handline for personnel. **Using a tree to gain height:** select a branch that has not been chopped, burned, or is rotten; at least 6 inches in diameter and thick enough for the load; high enough for clearance; tie a loose swami wrap around the branch and insert two steel locking carabiners, gates opposite and opposed; attach the installation through the carabiners.

**Operation:** personnel or equipment are secured to the hauling line and raised or lowered by a team pulling the rope. A braking/belay device is constructed and operated because the A-frame/vertical hauling line system is very strong — an uncontrolled load can injure the team or damage equipment. **Using personnel to gain height:** the edge must be buffed to prevent rope abrasion; the load must not exceed 100 lbs; personnel handling the load must be secured to the cliff with a safety line.

## Suspension Traverse

**Definition:** a high-tension rope installation established to move personnel and equipment across a gap, top to bottom or bottom to top.

**Site selection:** suitable upper and lower anchors; good loading and unloading platforms; sufficient clearance for the load.

**Construction:** top to bottom (anchor at the top, run the rope down, tension from the bottom) or bottom to top. Tension with a mechanical advantage system. **Safety note: no more than two men tension the installation at any time when using pulleys.** An A-frame or a tree (branch at least 6 inches in diameter, sound, anchor point behind the branch) gains height at the top when no natural height exists.

**Belay line:** established at the top with an adequate anchor; tie a figure-eight loop at the end of the belay line and attach it with a carabiner to the load or person, and tie a second figure-eight loop 12–18 inches from the end. Web runners are girth-hitched to the belay line with aluminum carabiners as attachment points.

**Operation:** the point NCOIC has overall responsibility. Lowering: a belayer and a belay line control the load, using a carabiner brake, figure-eight descender, or stitch plate as the braking device. Raising: a hauling system lifts heavy weapons, ammunition, or other logistical loads. **Load considerations:** weapons are secured through their slings or frames; ammunition and equipment are secured through carabiners and runners; litters are the collapsible and Stokes types; personnel wear a rappel seat clipped into the belay line.

## Rappelling

**Inherent danger:** rappelling is inherently dangerous. At MWTC two ropes are used for training; all rappels taught can be completed with a single rope if necessary. With a one-rope carabiner-wrap system the rope is attached to the anchor as taught.

**Site selection:** a good anchor; a rappel route as free of obstacles (vegetation, debris) as possible; suitable loading and unloading platforms.

**Rappel point NCOIC duties:** ensures anchors are sound and knots properly tied; clears loose rock and debris from the loading platform; allows only one man on the loading platform at a time; ensures each man is properly prepared (gloves on, equipment secured); attaches the rappeller to the rope and ensures he knows the proper braking position; ensures proper commands or signals are used; dispatches each man; is the last man down; inspects ropes after every 50 rappels; maintains the rope log.

**Types:** hasty rappel (moderate slopes, carrying loads — easiest to prepare); seat-shoulder rappel (heavily laden troops, vertical to near-vertical faces — friction across the shoulder and seat); seat-hip rappel (most commonly used — carrying loads over vertical to near-vertical faces, friction across the hip and seat). Braking in each: the brake hand controls the rope; to stop, bring the brake hand across the body and lock off. Tying off (stopping mid-descent) follows a fixed procedure of wrapping the rope to lock the brake.

**Safety of the first man down:** before deploying the ropes, tie the two ends together with an overhand knot; tie a friction knot (Prusik) on the standing end of the rappel rope; attach the rappel ropes to the harness hard point with a locking carabiner and the Prusik cord to the hard point with a locking carabiner — the safety Prusik follows the rappeller down and locks if he loses control.

**Duties of the first man down:** selects a smooth route down; clears loose rocks and debris; unties the overhand knot and straightens the ropes once down; belays the next man down (two methods — from below with a belay, or by holding the rope); takes charge of personnel at the bottom, including appointing a belay man.

**Rappelling commands:** standard calls for "on rappel", "off rappel", "tying off", and "off belay" as listed in the manual; in tactical situations a series of rope tugs may substitute for voice commands.

**Rescue techniques:** self-rescue (the rappeller frees himself from a jam or a locked brake); buddy rescue (a second rappeller descends to assist a rappeller who cannot continue); rappelling casualty rescue — three methods, including the rescuer presetting a system on a separate rappel rope, attaching the casualty's hard point to a sling rope/Prusik cord with figure-eight knots and a rappel device, maneuvering under the casualty, and rappelling both down together; and the last-resort method in which the rescuer cuts the casualty's rappel rope. **This is the only time a knife is used during a rappel. Extreme caution must be used. It is not done in training, only during an actual rescue and as a last resort.**

**Retrievable rappels:** one-rope retrievable rappel and two-rope retrievable rappel — the rope is rigged so the last man down can pull it free from below, leaving no rope on the cliff.

## Fixed Rope Installations and Cable Ladders

**Types:** simple fixed ropes (anchored at the top end only — quickest and easiest); semi-fixed ropes (anchored top and bottom, with slack — for troops who need a safety line while moving); fixed ropes (anchored at both ends and at intermediate points — most time-consuming). An assault lane is an alternative to a fixed rope for moving troops up a cliff face.

**Construction:** route selection considers the climbing unit's experience and ability and its load. Simple fixed rope: anchor at the top, rope hangs free. Semi-fixed: same as simple, plus a bottom anchor. Fixed rope: anchor top and bottom and at intermediate anchors (natural and artificial anchor points as shown in the manual).

**Ascending:** simple fixed ropes — hand over hand. Semi-fixed ropes — safety Prusik method. Fixed ropes and assault lanes — safety line and Prusik. **Maintenance:** buff all points of abrasion; inspect anchors periodically; report all discrepancies to the assault climbers immediately.

**Clearing the route:** rappel method (from the top); safety Prusik method (from the bottom); top-rope method (from the bottom).

**Cable ladders:** stainless steel cables with aluminum crossbars. Site selection: suitable anchors, good loading and unloading platforms, clearance, and a route the ladder can serve. Installation with natural anchors (tree wrap, boulder) or artificial anchors (deadman, pickets). Ascending: climb as any ladder — one arm and one leg on each side, or hand over hand with legs straddling on steep slopes, using leg muscles more than arms — always protected by a safety line or belay.
