# Loads, Training, Foot Care — FM 21-18 (Chapter 5, ¶¶5-1–5-14; Appendix C)

## Load Management (Chapter 5)

- **Principle:** ability to march and fight is directly related to load; the maximum individual load limit cannot be exceeded or the infantryman will not accomplish his mission. Fight light — only equipment for the immediate mission; receive additional weapons/materiel when required. Patton: "No soldier should be compelled to walk until he actually enters battle. From that point forward he should carry nothing but what he wears, his ammunition, his rations and his toilet articles."
- **Limits:** fighting load ≤ **48 lb**; approach march load (incl. fighting load) ≤ **72 lb**. Effective loads require safeguarding/transporting part of the load — commanders tailor loads by risk analysis; use CLOHE (company level) and SLOHE (battalion level).
- **Expedients (¶5-2):** helicopters to free HMMWVs (one dedicated helicopter per brigade can release three HMMWVs + trailers per company); corps plug transportation under battalion control; airdrop/steerable parachutes; host nation support (vehicles, tractors, beasts of burden and handlers, porters — via support, renting, capture; know commandeering law and funding; one general-purpose driver per platoon).
- **Factors:** physical limitations, stress, weight of equipment/munitions. Emergency approach march loads may exceed limits — cost is combat effectiveness, accepted knowingly. ARTEP load test example: 70-lb average load, 12 miles + tactical exercise, then 12 more miles + exercise, in 24 hours. After 30 days' preparatory training, soldiers can march 12 miles in under 3 hours at ~60 lb — a rate exhausting untrained soldiers in 2.5 hours.
- **Training:** progressive conditioning — increase distance day by day; marching strengthens feet/legs; running alone will not suffice. Leader training: aimed fire and fire discipline reduce ammunition depletion — itself a load problem.

## Foot Care (Appendix C)

- **Hygiene:** feet in heavy rigid footwear most working hours, constantly in action; neglected minor conditions have caused hospitalizations. Condition by progressively increasing distance; arch/ankle/calf exercises (rise high on toes; roll a towel under the arch with the toes).
- **Before marches:** trim toenails every 2–3 weeks, short and square, straight across; clean dry feet, foot powder; clean dry unmended good-fitting socks (cushion-soled preferred), seams/knots outside; nylon/polypropylene liner reduces friction; carry an extra pair; fit new boots carefully, alternate pairs while breaking in, tape hot spots.
- **During halts:** lie down, feet elevated; massage, powder, change socks, medicate blisters if time; cover open blisters/cuts/abrasions with absorbent adhesive bandages; loosen bootlaces over the arch for swelling.
- **After marches:** repeat care; wash/dry socks, dry boots; medicate blisters, abrasions, corns, calluses; inspect painful feet for sprains and fit; aerate, elevate, rest; daily foot bath if possible (cool water reduces heat/irritation), dry well.
- **Blisters (C-3):** friction/pressure from unconditioned feet, bad fit, heat, moisture. Wash gently around with soap/water without breaking skin; if unbroken, prick lower edge with flame-sterilized needle/knifepoint to drain; do not remove skin; cover with absorbent adhesive bandage beyond the edge; dust dressing and foot with powder (sparingly — excess hardens/irritates); check periodically; remove plaster after drying. Emergency: medical personnel may inject tincture of benzoin.
- **Perspiration (C-4):** secretion decomposes → odor; toe skin white, soft, abrades. Wash/dry; paint entire foot (ankle, heel, instep, between toes) with 1 part formalin : 9 parts rubbing alcohol, once daily until perspiration halts and skin hardens; keep out of cuts (severe pain); wash off if burning.
- **Athlete's foot (C-5):** between toes, soles, contact points; daily powder + fungicidal ointment if available, as directed, at rest; otherwise consult aidman/surgeon.
- **Frostbite (C-6):** cold → numbness → tingling/stinging/aching/cramping; skin red → yellowish, pale gray, waxy white. Prevent: enough clothing, face protected in wind, stay dry (shed layers during heavy work, change damp socks), never touch cold metal bare-skinned, shelter when inactive, exercise/massage face/fingers/toes/ears, buddy system, thaw small spots at once with body heat. Superficial: warm hands on cheeks; fingers in opposing armpits; feet against a companion's chest under clothing; never massage, open fire, cold-water soak, or snow rub; expect pain on thawing. Deep: do not treat in the field — evacuate fast; protect the part, no rubbing/bending/massaging, no snow, no water, no hot air/fire, no ointments; do not walk on thawed feet (safer on frozen feet). Prior cold injury increases recurrence risk.
- **Trenchfoot (C-7):** 32–50°F, damp/wet, immobility, insufficient clothing, constriction. Early: pale, cold, numb, stiff feet/toes, labored walking; then swelling, pain; extreme cases: flesh dies, amputation. Prevent: clean/dry socks and boots daily, dry feet soon after wetting, wiggle toes/bend ankles and warm with hands if wet boots must be worn, then powder and dry socks. Treat: handle gently, never rub/massage; clean with plain white soap/water, dry, elevate, leave exposed; warm the patient but keep feet at room temperature; carry, do not let him walk.
- **Immersion foot (C-8):** prolonged immersion in water not cold enough to freeze (can occur in subtropical waters); same causes and treatment as trenchfoot.
- **Stress fractures/muscle injuries (C-9):** rest 2–3 weeks until pain gone, then slow return; prevention is conditioning plus stretching/warming major muscle groups before marching.
