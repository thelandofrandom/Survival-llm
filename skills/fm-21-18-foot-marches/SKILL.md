# FM 21-18 Foot Marches

## Purpose

FM 21-18, *Foot Marches* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 1 June 1990, 130 pages) — the guide for commanders and their staffs in the procedures and techniques of foot marches: the march mission, characteristics and types of foot marches, and march training, including planning procedures, duties of commanders, march discipline, march hygiene, and march safety. It supersedes FM 21-18 of 29 January 1971. The proponent is HQ TRADOC; changes go on DA Form 2028 to the Commandant, U.S. Army Infantry School, Fort Benning, GA. The term "company" as used herein can mean battery or troop.

Five chapters and four appendices: introduction (Ch. 1), factors (Ch. 2), movement planning (Ch. 3), execution (Ch. 4), load management and training (Ch. 5); Appendix A — unit SOP; Appendix B — example battalion road movement OPORD; Appendix C — foot care; Appendix D — example road movement order. Use with regional manuals as required: desert (FM 90-3), jungle (FM 90-5), northern operations (FM 31-71), mountain (FM 90-6); tactical foot marches are in FMs 7-10 and 7-20. Chapter 3 implements STANAG 2154, Edition Five.

**Supersession note — read this first.** For current Army doctrine, FM 21-18 (1990) is superseded by **ATP 3-21.18, *Foot Marches* (July 2025)**, which is already a skill in this vault. ATP 3-21.18 is the current, authoritative treatment; where the two disagree on a rate, a load, or a procedure, follow ATP 3-21.18. FM 21-18 remains valuable because it is the fuller treatment: its march-planning mathematics (time-distance, pass time, road movement tables), its worked forced-march schedule, its battalion SOP extract, and its foot-care appendix give the working detail behind the current manual's shorter prescriptions. Use ATP 3-21.18 as the authority and FM 21-18 as the deep planning reference.

## 1. Introduction — What a Foot March Is (Chapter 1, ¶¶1-1–1-5)

**1.1. Definition.** Foot marches are the movement of troops and equipment mainly by foot, with limited support by vehicles. They are characterized by combat readiness, ease of control, adaptability to terrain, slow rate of movement, and increased personnel fatigue. Foot marches do not depend on the existence of roads.

**1.2. Historical examples (¶1-1).** Sicily, 20–21 July 1943: 3d Battalion, 30th Infantry, marched 54 miles cross-country in 33 continuous hours and attacked two hours after arrival, capturing San Stefano. Battle of the Bulge, December 1944: 3d U.S. Army shifted ~250,000 men and 25,000 vehicles 100 road miles in damp cold over snow and ice. The lesson: the movement worked because smaller units could move themselves, with SOPs and experience tables tested and proved.

**1.3. Fundamentals and the march mission (¶¶1-2–1-3).** Troop movement is transporting troops from one place to another by any available means; a successful move places troops and equipment at the destination at the proper time, ready for combat. Detailed plans are required, units must be notified early, and control during movement runs through the chain of command. A foot march succeeds when troops arrive at the prescribed time **and** are physically able to execute their tactical mission immediately. Commanders must ensure that the amount and type of equipment carried, the rate of march, and the length and number of rests equate with the physical endurance of the men. **1.4. Classification (¶1-4).** Movements are tactical or administrative; both apply in most movements but one is normally dominant. **Tactical** movements are conducted in the combat zone, emphasizing security and combat-ready formations, anticipating ground contact en route or after arrival — including over unsecure routes where no friendly forces stand between the foremost elements and the enemy. **Administrative** movements are conducted in the COMMZ and zone of the interior, emphasizing the best method of movement, with ground contact remote; normally over secure routes, but units should maintain integrity and practice tactical road-march security techniques. **1.5. Types of marches (¶1-5).** **Day marches** are preferred when there is no enemy threat: faster, less tiring, dispersed formations, easy control and reconnaissance — but more vulnerable to observation and air attack. **Limited visibility (night) marches:** closed formations, difficult control and reconnaissance, slow rate, good concealment; they exploit darkness for surprise and to avoid extreme heat. If concealment is required, movement before dark is restricted to small detachments, the march should be completed by daybreak with troops concealed, and noise suppression matters near the enemy. **Forced marches** require speed, exertion, and more hours marched — normally by increasing marching hours per day rather than the rate; employed only when needed, since they decrease unit effectiveness; troops should be told the reason, to ensure maximum effort. **Shuttle marches** alternate riding and marching when there are not enough vehicles for the whole unit, by a series of round-trips with the same vehicles. ## 2. Factors Affecting the March (Chapter 2, ¶¶2-1–2-7)

**2.1. Tactical considerations (¶2-1).** "Soldiers should not be required to walk until they enter battle. From that point on, they should be required to carry only what they wear, weapons, ammunition, water, and minimal rations." A moving force protects itself with all-round security elements, employed during all types of movements: a covering force (self-contained, directly under the senior commander; develops the situation, seizes key terrain, defeats resistance; typically divisions and larger) precedes large units as ground contact becomes imminent; each column commander also establishes an advance guard to protect the main body from surprise and ensure uninterrupted advance; flank and rear guards protect unsecured flanks and rear. Before the march, routes should be reconnoitered and marked, bridge capacities indicated, underpass dimensions compared to vehicle dimensions (if the tactical situation permits), and obstacles reduced. Combat support assets sit in the column for quick availability; sustainment loads not immediately needed can be left behind and moved forward after troops and essential equipment. Each grouping for movement corresponds with the organization for combat, including weapons, ammunition, and rations needed at the destination.

**2.2. Terrain, weather, enemy (¶2-2).** The factors that greatly influence the conduct of the march are the location of enemy forces, the nature of terrain and weather, and the activity of enemy aviation. Contact with the enemy should be expected from any direction not protected by friendly forces or terrain barriers. Other common factors: distance to be marched; planning effectiveness; march discipline and supervision; time available; and the physical condition, training status, and attitude of the soldier.

**2.3. March discipline (¶2-3).** March discipline is observing and enforcing the rules governing a unit on a march: formation, distances between elements, speed, effective use of concealment and cover — plus specific controls and restrictions: water, light, noise, and communication disciplines. It is the culmination of effective training, resulting in enthusiastic teamwork.

**2.4. Water discipline (¶2-4) — the rules.** Drink plenty of water before each march. Drink only treated water from approved sources. Drink water often — before, during, and after the march. Drink small quantities rather than gulping. Drink even when not thirsty. Drink slowly to prevent cramps or nausea. Avoid spilling water. Refill canteens at every opportunity. Strenuous activity loses water and electrolytes through perspiration, plus normal losses through respiration and urination; deficient liquid and salt intake in hot weather causes heat injuries. Dehydration is as prevalent in cold regions as in hot — in cold, perspiration is absorbed by heavy clothing or evaporated and rarely visible. Salt in food compensates for the daily salt requirement; additional salt only under a physician's direction. If pure water is unavailable, treat canteen water with purification tablets (see FM 21-10). In an NBC-contaminated area, water consumption increases and forced hydration becomes necessary. **2.5. Acclimatization and morale (¶¶2-5–2-6).** Mountain operations normally need 10 to 14 days of high-altitude training before full-scale mountain marches. Gradual introduction to unfamiliar terrain and climate removes preconceived fears of height, cold, or isolation. Morale is a marching factor: low morale is contagious and magnifies discomfort. Leaders improve it by giving advance warning; not forming the unit too early; avoiding standing delays (route reconnaissance prevents them); prescribing a steady rate; holding passing vehicles to reasonable speed and moving troops upwind in dust; not letting straggler trucks overtake the column unless unavoidable; ensuring rear soldiers receive full break time; marching with the soldiers at every echelon — "soldiers quickly detect the presence or absence of their leaders"; and ensuring adequate water at rest stops.

**2.6. Individual load (¶2-7) — the limits.** The combat load is the mission-essential equipment the commander determines the soldier needs to fight and survive immediate combat operations. It divides into an **approach march load** and a **fighting load**: the fighting load should not exceed **48 pounds**, and the approach march load (which includes the fighting load) should be **less than 72 pounds**, based on individual abilities. The primary consideration is not how much a soldier can carry but how much he can carry without impaired combat effectiveness, mentally or physically. Soldiers become exhausted quickly under combat stress, so they must carry less into battle than they are conditioned to carry in training. The load must not be based on gear for every contingency — items are based on realistic expectations. The commander is responsible for obtaining the means to carry additional gear: usually a rifle company or smaller unit requires one truck and one trailer in support (more in cold weather), and the supply system must provide the balance of essential supplies not carried by the unit. In training, instill pride in operating under austere conditions and train field craft, caches, and field expedients. (Chapter 5 develops this in full; see §5.) ## 3. Movement Planning (Chapter 3, ¶¶3-1–3-5)

**3.1. Planning sequence (¶3-1).** Battalion-level planning, in this sequence: (a) **Receive the mission** — hasty mission analysis for critical times and tasks. (b) **Warning order** — gives subordinates time to prepare. (c) **Estimate of the situation** — the S3 weighs mission, weather, terrain, time and space, routes, transportation, enemy capabilities, own forces, and troop condition, and recommends a course of action; the staff then prepares the movement order (see FM 101-5-1). (d) **Detailed movement plans** — an OPORD covering column organization, order of march, and the elements of §3.4. (e) **Road movement orders** — as an OPORD or annex, written or oral, with a road movement table, operation overlay, or strip map; Appendix D gives an example. (f) **Reconnaissance party** — ground reconnaissance is the basis of every march plan; map and aerial reconnaissance help but are not substitutes. The party reports routes and conditions, recommended rate, SP/RP suitability, assembly/bivouac confirmation, checkpoints and distances, obstacles, and guides required. (g) **Quartering party** — prepares the new area for systematic arrival: marks unit locations, plans reception and guiding from the RP, selects CP, administrative, feeding, and latrine sites, and rehearses the guide plan; medical advises on sanitation and selects the aid station site.

**3.2. Column organization.** A **march unit** moves and halts at the command of a single commander and normally corresponds to a squad, section, platoon, or company. A **march serial** is one or more march units under the senior officer, given a numerical or alphabetical designation; a serial is usually a battalion or larger unit but can be a company marching alone. A **march column** is elements of a command moving over the same route, of one or more serials, under a designated column commander; normally a brigade or larger unit but can be a battalion marching alone. **Order of march** in a tactical march depends on mission, terrain, probable order of commitment, and mobility: tanks and infantry interspersed; artillery and mortars forward and throughout; air defense throughout or moved by bounds over critical points; engineers well forward; antitank weapons throughout, some with the security forces. **3.3. Rates, distances, and halts — the numbers.** The unit SOP usually states the rate for roads and cross-country, normal terrain, day or night (see Appendix A); the column commander modifies it for mountain, jungle, desert, or arctic areas, where physical effort increases, the load should decrease, and injury potential increases. Rates for normal terrain (Appendix A, ¶3f — the Chapter 3 table itself did not survive text extraction, but Appendix A restates the same figures):

| | Roads | Cross-country |
|---|---|---|
| Day | 4.0 kph | 2.4 kph |
| Night | 3.2 kph | 1.6 kph |

Normal pace: **30 inches at a cadence of 106 steps per minute**. Normal length in 24 hours: **20 to 32 km**, marching 5 to 8 hours at 4 kph. Normal formation: column of twos; 2 to 5 meters between soldiers (1 to 3 meters at night), 50 meters between platoons (25 meters at night), 100 meters between companies (50 meters at night). (Appendix A's distance table gives 5 m day / 3 m night between men — the two statements agree on units and differ slightly on men at night; use the SOP figure for the march in question.) **Halts (Appendix A, ¶3j):** 15-minute halt after the first 45 minutes; thereafter 10 minutes every 50 minutes; observation posts and local security during halts; halfway, a 15-minute rest stop with sock change. At halts soldiers move to the sides of the road, seek cover and concealment, face outward, loosen equipment; aidmen treat as needed; squad leaders check feet, ensure powdering and sock change. Meal halts: A-rations 1 hour, MREs 30 minutes. **3.4. Time-distance terms and march math (¶¶3-2–3-3).** Any two of distance, rate, and time give the third: R = D/T; D = R×T; T = D/R. **Time distance (TDIS)** = distance ÷ rate, excluding long delays or extended scheduled halts. **Pass time (PST)** = time between the first element passing a point and the last passing it; for foot columns, PST (minutes) = column length × factor — **0.0150 for 4.0 kph, 0.0187 for 3.2 kph, 0.0250 for 2.4 kph, 0.0375 for 1.6 kph** (e.g., a 1,500-meter column at 4 kph = 22.5 minutes). **Arrival time** = SP time + TDIS + scheduled halts (SP 0800, TDIS 6 h 45 min, halt 1 h → arrival 1545). **Completion time** = arrival time + pass time (0800 + 6 h 45 min + 41 min PST + 1 h halt = 1626). **Road clearance time** = TDIS + PST. **Checkpoint** — reference/timing point. **Start point (SP)** — a common, easily recognized point, never in a defile, on a hill, or at a sharp curve, far enough from assembly areas that units are at prescribed speed and interval when they reach it. **Release point (RP)** — the common point for reverting to parent-unit control, easily recognized, avoided on hills, defiles, and sharp curves, with guides meeting units; units do not stay at the RP. **Column gap / road gap** — space (or time) between elements, rear of one to front of the next; **traffic density** — vehicles per mile/kilometer of road; **time gap** — the time aspect of column gap, with no prescribed values. The **road movement table** (usually an annex to the movement order) transmits the time schedule. The **strip map** is a schematic of the route with SP, RP, restrictions, and critical points with distances between them; an **operation overlay** shows friendly forces, route, critical points, and the destination. Experience tables from previous movements can replace fresh computation. ## 4. Execution (Chapter 4, ¶¶4-1–4-20)

**4.1. Organization for the march (¶4-1).** The **reconnaissance party** reconnoiters the route (travel time, underpass/bridge capacity, culverts, ferries, fords, critical points, obstacles) and reports as instructed. The **main body** moves at the constant speed designated in the order, maintaining interval and column gap; erratic speed changes — especially at hills — create an accordion or whipping effect that forces tail elements to move at increased and unsafe speeds; a unit behind schedule uses only the designated catch-up speed. Arrival at the SP on time is critical: each march unit reconnoiters its route to the SP and determines its time to reach it. March units report crossing each control point as directed; air and ground security is maintained throughout. The **trail party** — the last march unit in a battalion serial, usually led by the BMO with maintenance and medical personnel — recovers disabled vehicles and stragglers. **4.2. Security and reaction to contact (¶¶4-2–4-3).** Continuous all-round security during the march and at halts; passive and active measures against air attack and long-range weapons; reconnaissance elements identify critical points where security increases. On enemy contact, the unit reacts per the march order and SOP — the security forces (§2.1) give early warning and develop the situation while the main body prepares to fight or bypass. **4.3. Halts, night marches, safety (¶¶4-4–4-8).** Halts follow the schedule of §3.3, with security posted and the column cleared off the road. **Night marches** increase control by closing intervals, using connecting files and messengers, assigning colors to march units (usable on flashlights for recognition), and using flashlights, lanterns, luminous markers, and pyrotechnics consistent with light discipline. Safety: use off-road trails to keep foot troops off vehicle roads; place marked, equipped guards with reflective or luminous materials at front and rear (and flanks where vehicles could approach); equip guards with reflective fabric or tape, vests, caps, mittens, hat bands, signs, red lights, flashlights, batons, flags, or lanterns; warn vehicle operators of troops on or near the roadway and restrict speeds; instruct them on the meaning of luminous warning devices; enforce safety measures even on routes reserved exclusively for foot soldiers. **4.4. Duties (¶¶4-9–4-18).** **Company commander:** warning order in time; reconnoiter route to the SP; march plan and road movement order; inspect before marching; head of company to the SP, then supervise rate, formation, and stragglers. **Executive officer:** leads when the commander is not at the head; supervises the pace setter; assists at halts; posts/supervises traffic guards and guides; supervises CP and latrines on arrival. **First sergeant:** NCO inspections; formation, pace, intervals, noise/light/water/rest discipline. **Platoon leader:** briefs and inspects the platoon; maintains distances; enforces discipline; at halts clears the road, checks condition, enforces water/food discipline and sanitation, reforms ~1 min before marching; leads into the assembly area from the RP. **Platoon sergeant:** halt inspections; straggler control (examines casualties with the aidman before recommending fall-out); resupply of water, rations, and medical supplies. **Squad leader:** inspects boots and socks; extra pair of socks per soldier; foot kit (powder, gauze, adhesive tape); full canteens; shifts heavy loads from tired to rested soldiers; foot inspection after the march. **Medical aidman:** treats march casualties; advises on evacuation. **Guides:** lead route and area entry/exit, posted at critical points with instructions and a strip map. **Road guards:** ~50 m front and rear, at intersections while crossing; flashlights/lanterns at night. **Pace setter:** experienced soldier, same load as the majority, 4–10 m at the head; maintains the ordered rate by pace and cadence; pace governed by the most heavily loaded element. **4.5. Special march operations (¶¶4-19–4-20).** **Limited visibility marches:** disadvantages — navigation, slower speed, checkpoint recognition, control, intervals; advantages — security, tighter formations, less traffic, surprise/stealth. Control is increased by reducing distances, adding guides and connecting files, and using visual communications. **Forced marches:** a normal foot march day is 8 hours for 32 km at 4 kph; the maximum distances recommended are **56 km in 24 hours; 96 km in 48 hours; 128 km in 72 hours**. A forced march usually exceeds the normal day by increasing hours marched rather than rate — though the rate must sometimes be increased. Rest periods should avoid the hottest time of day and ensure arrival in combat-ready condition; commanders should consider increasing the rate when soldiers are most rested. Worked 52-km schedule from first light: 20 km @ 4 kph (5.0 h) → noon meal/rest 2.0 h → 20 km @ 4 kph (5.0 h) → supper/rest 6.0 h → 12 km @ 3.2 kph (3.84 h) → total 21.84 h. ## 5. Soldier's Load Management and Training (Chapter 5, ¶¶5-1–5-14; Appendix C)

**5.1. The load problem.** "The ability of a soldier to march and fight is directly related to his load. The maximum individual load limit cannot be exceeded as an infantry soldier will not accomplish his mission." Soldiers fight light, with only the equipment required for the immediate mission, and receive additional weapon systems and materiel when required. Patton: "No soldier should be compelled to walk until he actually enters battle. From that point forward he should carry nothing but what he wears, his ammunition, his rations and his toilet articles." Effective loads are achieved only by safeguarding and transporting portions of the load — commanders tailor loads from risk analysis, using CLOHE (company level) and SLOHE (battalion level) transportation. **5.2. Load figures and expedients (¶¶5-1–5-2).** Table 5-1 gives planning figures for the load-handling lift each infantry company requires. If extra transportation is not given to battalions, rely on: helicopters to free HMMWVs for CLOHE/SLOHE (one dedicated helicopter per brigade can release three HMMWVs and trailers per company); corps plug transportation under battalion control; airdrop or steerable parachutes; and host nation support — vehicles, tractors, beasts of burden and handlers, porters (know the law on commandeering and the funding for renting; one general-purpose driver per platoon). **5.3. Factors affecting the load (¶5-3 ff.).** Commanders must understand the physical limitations of individual soldiers, stress, and the weight of equipment and munitions. The fighting load for a conditioned soldier should not exceed 48 pounds and the approach march load should not exceed 72 pounds. Emergency approach march loads may exceed the limit, but the cost is combat effectiveness, accepted knowingly. Load test example for ARTEP: 70-lb average load, 12 miles + tactical exercise, then 12 more miles + exercise, in 24 hours. After a 30-day preparatory training period, soldiers can march 12 miles in under 3 hours at ~60 lb — a rate exhausting untrained soldiers in 2.5 hours. **5.4. Training (¶¶5-12–5-14).** Conditioning is progressive: increase distance day by day; marching itself strengthens feet and legs — running alone will not suffice. Leader training matters as much as soldier training: improved aimed fire and fire discipline decrease the risk of depleting ammunition, which is itself a load problem. **5.5. Foot care (Appendix C).** Foot hygiene is critical — many hospitalizations began as neglected minor foot problems. **Conditioning:** progressively increase distance; marching strengthens feet and legs — running alone will not suffice; strengthen arch, ankle, and calf. **Before marches:** trim toenails every 2–3 weeks, short and square; clean dry feet, foot powder; clean, dry, unmended, good-fitting socks (cushion-soled preferred), seams and knots outside; a nylon or polypropylene liner reduces friction; carry an extra pair; fit new boots carefully, alternating pairs while breaking in, taping hot spots. **During halts:** lie down with feet elevated; massage, powder, change socks; cover open blisters, cuts, or abrasions with absorbent adhesive bandages; loosen bootlaces over the arch for swelling. **After marches:** repeat care, wash and dry socks, dry boots; medicate blisters, abrasions, corns, calluses; inspect painful feet; aerate, elevate, rest; a daily foot bath if possible, then dry well. **Blisters (C-3):** friction/pressure from unconditioned feet, bad fit, heat, moisture. Wash gently without breaking the skin; if unbroken, prick the lower edge with a flame-sterilized needle to drain; do not remove the skin; cover with an absorbent adhesive bandage extending beyond the edge; dust dressing and foot with powder sparingly. **Perspiration (C-4):** paint the whole foot with 1 part formalin to 9 parts rubbing alcohol, once daily until perspiration halts and skin hardens; keep out of cuts. **Athlete's foot (C-5):** daily powder plus fungicidal ointment if available; otherwise consult an aidman or surgeon. **Frostbite (C-6):** cold, then numbness, then tingling/stinging/aching pain; skin red, then yellowish, pale gray, or waxy white. Prevent with enough clothing, dryness, and the buddy system; thaw small spots at once with body heat. Superficial: warm hands on cheeks, fingers in opposing armpits, feet against a companion's chest; never massage, open fire, cold-water soak, or snow rub. Deep: do not treat in the field — evacuate fast; protect the part, no rubbing, bending, water, hot air, fire, or ointments; do not walk on thawed feet. Prior cold injury increases recurrence risk. **Trenchfoot (C-7):** 32–50°F, damp/wet, immobility, constriction. Early: pale, cold, numb, stiff feet, labored walking; then swelling and pain; extreme cases may require amputation. Prevent by drying socks, boots, and feet daily; treat gently, never rub or massage; clean with plain white soap and water, dry, elevate, leave exposed; warm the patient but keep feet at room temperature; carry, do not let him walk. **Immersion foot (C-8):** prolonged immersion in water not cold enough to freeze; same causes and treatment as trenchfoot. **Stress fractures (C-9):** rest 2–3 weeks until pain is gone, then slow return; prevention is conditioning plus stretching and warming major muscle groups before marching. → `references/loads-training-foot-care.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Arrive ready to fight.** The march succeeds only if troops arrive on time and able to execute the mission immediately.
2. **Steady rate, no accordion.** Constant speed, designated catch-up speed only — erratic speed at hills exhausts the tail.
3. **Drink before, during, and after.** Small quantities, often, even when not thirsty; only treated water from approved sources.
4. **Forty-eight and seventy-two.** Fighting load 48 lb, approach march load under 72 lb — the commander's problem is what to leave behind, not what to carry.
5. **Feet are the march.** Powder, clean dry socks, an extra pair, halt inspections — neglect a blister and you lose a soldier.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-21.18 (2025) supersedes FM 21-18 where they conflict; FM 21-18 is the deep planning reference, not the authority.
