# Sniper Training

## Purpose

FM 23-10 *Sniper Training* (Department of the Army, August 1994) is the U.S. Army's classic sniper manual — the doctrine that trained a generation of snipers before the M24 was replaced and the manual itself was superseded by TC 3-22.10. It is for the two-man sniper team (sniper and observer), their trainers, and the commanders who employ them. Its core idea: sniping is not just long-range shooting. It is a system — weapon, ballistics, fieldcraft, observation, planning, and sustainment — in which the shot is the smallest part. The manual's own framing is that a sniper is a force multiplier whose value comes from discriminating, highly accurate rifle fire against selected targets, plus the intelligence his observation produces. Everything in this skill is distilled from that manual; where the hardware is dated (the M24/M21 era, the AN/PVS-4), the fieldcraft, ballistics method, and team procedures still transfer directly to any precision rifle.

Key grounding facts: the M24 Sniper Weapon System is a 7.62-mm bolt-action rifle with a maximum effective range of 800 meters (effective hits to 1,000 meters are the qualification confidence challenge); the standard round is the 173-grain M118 special ball; the M3A day optic adjusts in 1-minute-of-angle clicks, with the elevation dial marked 1–10 for 100–1,000 meters. The team is two men, and the observer is not a junior partner — he selects targets, estimates range, computes weather effects, calls the shot, and critiques it.

## 1. The Team and the Mission

**1.1 Team composition and roles.** The sniper team is a sniper and an observer. The sniper fires; the observer does almost everything else: selects appropriate targets, assists in range estimation, calculates the effect of weather on ballistics, reports sight-adjustment data, uses the M49 observation telescope for shot observation, and critiques performance. The two men swap roles in training and, when the situation allows, on operations — the observer must be able to shoot, and the sniper must be able to observe. The team is employed by the commander through the Sniper Employment Officer (SEO), who coordinates insertion, execution, and extraction.

**1.2 What snipers do.** The manual lists the sniper's jobs as: engaging selected targets (officers, crew-served weapons crews, radio operators, and other high-value personnel); observing and reporting (SALT reports — Size, Activity, Location, Time); collecting battlefield information; and, in the defense, dominating no-man's-land and denying the enemy freedom of movement. Target selection is often forced on the team — a rapidly moving target may be lost while obtaining positive identification — so the team pre-decides its priorities: any enemy threatening the team's position is a high-value target.

**1.3 METT-T planning.** Arms, ammunition, and equipment are METT-T dependent: Mission, Enemy, Terrain and weather, Troops available, Time available. Mission preparation (Chapter 5) uses planning factors to estimate the time, coordination, and effort a mission needs. The team plans for three phases — insertion, execution, extraction — and coordinates air support, fires, communications, and recovery before leaving friendly lines. The planning checklist lives in `references/mission-planning.md`.

## 2. The Weapon Systems: M24 and M21

**2.1 M24 Sniper Weapon System.** A 7.62-mm, bolt-action, magazine-fed rifle built as a dedicated sniper system. Vital numbers: muzzle velocity 2,794 feet per second with M118; maximum effective range 800 meters; overall length 44.8 inches; barrel length 21.7 inches (heavy contour). It is issued with a deployment kit (operator repair/maintenance tools and parts) and its own technical manual, TM 9-1005-306-10, which the sniper must know. The M24 is designed for use with the M3A telescope (day optic) or metallic iron sights as backup; the iron sights can be installed quickly and the scope removed and replaced with less than 1/2 minute of angle change in zero — but the scope should be left on the rifle.

**2.2 M3A scope.** The M3A is a fixed-power day optic. Its elevation dial is marked 1–10, each number representing the elevation setting for that hundred meters (1 = 100 m, 3 = 300 m, 7 = 700 m). Each click of the elevation dial equals 1 minute of angle; the windage dial works the same way. The sniper zeroes on a known-distance range (preferably 900 meters long) with bull's-eye-type targets, and records the zero and every subsequent sight change in the sniper data book — a new data-book entry is required whenever the team fires a different type or lot of ammunition.

**2.3 Ammunition.** The standard round is the **M118 special ball**: a 173-grain boat-tailed bullet (tapered rear) with a gilding-metal jacket and lead-antimony core; the tip is uncolored and the cartridge base is stamped with the year of manufacture and a circle with vertical and horizontal lines. M118 is a match-grade round, not ordinary 7.62-mm ball, and lot-to-lot variation is real — hence the data book. The team carries 100 rounds of M118 as a planning figure, plus the M9 pistol and M9 bayonet.

**2.4 M21 (Appendix B).** The M21 is the semi-automatic 7.62-mm sniper system the M24 replaced as the Army's primary sniper rifle — a accurized M14 with a scope. Know it exists and how it differs: semi-automatic (faster follow-up shots, slightly less inherent precision than the bolt-action M24), still effective in trained hands, and the system many allied and National Guard snipers still fielded. Do not confuse its zeroing and maintenance procedures with the M24's.

**2.5 Care, maintenance, and malfunctions.** Maintenance is inspection, repair, cleaning, and lubrication. In garrison, inspect the M24 often even when infrequently used — dirt and corrosion do not wait for range day. Salt water and saltwater atmosphere have extreme and rapid corrosive effects on the SWS; during exposure, check and clean as often as possible, even if that only means lubricating. New barrels get a formal break-in procedure to increase barrel life, accuracy, and reduce cleaning requirements. Malfunctions the sniper cannot correct mean the complete weapon system goes to the proper maintenance/supply channel for return to the contractor — there is no field-expedient rebuild of a sniper rifle. Ammunition in direct sunlight heats up: powder burn rate rises, muzzle velocity rises, and the point of impact rises with it. Keep ammo shaded and at a stable temperature.

**2.6 Night vision.** The AN/PVS-4 is the manual's night sight: a portable, battery-operated electro-optical instrument, hand-held for observation or weapon-mounted for precision fire at night. It lets the observer detect and resolve distant targets in darkness, extending the team's effectiveness into continuous operations. Modern image intensifiers and thermals replace it, but the employment logic is unchanged: night optics are a team asset, managed like the day optic, and the team trains with them rather than discovering them on the mission.

## 3. Marksmanship and Ballistics

**3.1 Fundamentals.** Sniper marksmanship is an extension of basic rifle marksmanship, focused on the techniques needed to engage targets at extended ranges. The fundamentals are the same — steady position, aiming, breath control, trigger control, follow-through — but the tolerances shrink with distance. The butt of the stock sits firmly in the pocket of the shoulder; a pad on the ghillie suit where the butt contacts the body reduces the effect of pulse beat and breathing transmitted to the weapon. Bolt operation is conducted under a veil or equivalent camouflage to preserve concealment. The steady firing position uses maximum cover and concealment for the terrain, vegetation, and tactical situation.

**3.2 Ballistic vocabulary.** Muzzle velocity is the speed of the bullet as it leaves the barrel, in feet per second; it varies with ammunition type and lot, temperature, and humidity. Trajectory is the bullet's curved path. The sniper must know: zero (the sight setting that puts the bullet on the point of aim at a given range); minute of angle (MOA — 1 MOA is roughly 1 inch at 100 yards, 1.047 inches exactly, and about 29 mm at 100 meters); come-ups (elevation changes between ranges); and holdoff (aiming off the target to compensate for wind or movement).

**3.3 Effects of weather and environment.** Temperature is the biggest atmospheric variable: as temperature rises, air density falls, resistance falls, velocity is preserved longer, and the point of impact rises — relative to the temperature at which the rifle was zeroed. If you zero at 50°F and shoot at 90°F, you will hit high. Altitude works the same way: at higher altitudes the air is less dense, the bullet is more efficient, and it impacts higher due to less drag (Table 3-1 in the manual gives approximate corrections). Humidity has a small effect. Light matters too: changing light conditions shift the apparent position of the target and the sniper's perception of it — record light conditions in the data book alongside every shot.

**3.4 Angle fire (slope dope).** When shooting uphill or downhill, gravity acts on the horizontal component of the range, not the slant range — so the bullet hits high relative to a flat-range zero at the same slant distance. The amount of elevation change applied to the telescope for angle firing is called slope dope (Table 3-3). Rule of thumb: for angles up to about 15°, ignore it; beyond that, correct — and the correction grows fast with angle and range. Always range the horizontal distance when you can, or apply the manual's slope-dope table.

**3.5 Wind.** Wind is the sniper's hardest variable because it changes along the bullet's whole flight. The manual's method: estimate wind speed (by observing grass, leaves, smoke, mirage, or a flag — the manual gives observation-based speed bands), determine wind value by the clock method (a wind from 3 or 9 o'clock is full value; from 1, 5, 7, or 11 o'clock is half value; from 12 or 6 o'clock is no value), and apply the wind formula (Table 3-4): the correction in MOA is roughly (range in hundreds of meters × wind speed in mph) divided by a constant of 10–15 for the M118, the constant shrinking as range grows. A wind blowing against a moving target's direction of movement requires less lead than a wind blowing with it — wind and lead interact, and the observer computes both before the shot.

**3.6 Moving targets.** Engaging moving targets requires the target distance, wind effects, the lateral and speed angle of the target, the bullet's time of flight, and a proper lead. These added variables increase the chance of a miss, so the manual's guidance is blunt: engage moving targets when it is the only option. Two methods: tracking (follow the target with the reticle, maintaining the lead, and fire while moving) and trapping/ambushing (set the reticle ahead of the target and fire as it walks into the aim point). Tracking is preferred for slow, steady movement; trapping works for targets moving at a predictable speed and path.

**3.7 Range estimation.** The observer's core skill, and the manual treats it as a discipline with five methods, cross-checked against each other:
- **Mil-relation formula.** Measure the target's angular size in mils through the scope or M49, and compute range from known target size: range (meters) = target height (meters) × 1,000 ÷ mils observed. A 1.8-meter man measuring 2 mils is 900 meters away. This is the primary method — but it is only as good as the size estimate.
- **Appearance of objects.** At known distances, detail disappears in predictable ways (a man's face is visible at 100 m, his outline at 400 m, etc.). Fast but coarse.
- **100-meter unit of measure.** Mentally lay 100-meter segments along the ground to the target. Works to about 500 meters; beyond that, error compounds.
- **Bracketing.** Estimate a maximum and a minimum possible range and take the mean.
- **Range card and map.** Pre-measured reference points on the range card, or map distance, confirm or correct the estimate.
The data book records every estimate against the confirmed range afterward — the team calibrates its eye the same way it calibrates its rifle. Full method detail and the mil-relation worked examples are in `references/ballistics-and-range.md`.

## 4. Field Techniques: Move, Hide, Observe, Detect

**4.1 Camouflage and concealment.** The sniper's survival depends on not being seen, and the manual treats camouflage as a craft, not a coat of paint. Principles: blend with the background (color, texture, shape), break up the outline (the human silhouette and the rifle's straight lines are what the eye catches), avoid shine (optics, skin, metal), and stay still — movement is what the eye detects first. The ghillie suit is the sniper's signature tool: a loose, shaggy over-garment built on a base of netting and burlap, garnished with local vegetation, so the wearer reads as a bush rather than a man. Garnish is always local — vegetation from the hide area, replaced as it wilts, because a suit garnished in one place looks wrong in another. The weapon is camouflaged too, and the veil over the scope kills glint.

**4.2 Movement.** The sniper moves slowly, deliberately, and only when observation allows. Movement techniques scale with exposure: the low crawl, high crawl, and the sniper's crawl (dragging the body flat, rifle cradled) for the final approach; walking upright only where cover allows. Route selection uses dead ground, vegetation, and shadow; the team never skylines itself on a ridge and never moves in the open when a covered route exists. Noise discipline and light discipline are absolute — no white light, no talking above a whisper, no metal-on-metal contact.

**4.3 The hide site.** A hide is a prepared firing position, not a convenient bush. The team selects it for: a field of fire covering the target area; concealment from ground and air; multiple avenues of escape; and protection from the environment. The weapon stays in place; the optics are handed between team members; the sniper data book, observation logs, range cards, and radio sit between the two men where both can reach them. A rest, eating, and latrine system is arranged — latrine calls are done in a way that leaves no sign. A good hide is occupied before first light and left after dark; a hasty hide is built in minutes but still follows the same logic.

**4.4 Observation.** The observer works systematically, not by staring. The manual's method: a slow, overlapping scan of the target area in sectors, near to far, using the naked eye first (optics narrow the field of view and tire the eye), then the M49 observation telescope for detail. Observation is recorded as it happens — the observation log is a written record of what was seen, when, and where, and it is the team's intelligence product. Target detection is trained as a skill: look for the things that betray a hidden man — shine, shape, shadow, silhouette, spacing, movement, and color contrast (the "7 S's" family of indicators, though the manual frames them as what to look for rather than a mnemonic).

**4.5 Target detection and the Kim's game.** Sustainment training (Chapter 9) includes observation exercises that train the eye: Kim's game (study a set of objects, cover them, recall them in detail) builds the visual memory the observer needs to notice what changed in a scene. Repetitive training in long-range marksmanship and fieldcraft is what keeps the skills alive — the manual is explicit that sniper skills decay fast without practice, and sustainment training is a command responsibility, not a personal hobby.

**4.6 Stalking.** The stalk is the final approach to the firing position under observation — the hardest field skill and the one the manual tests most severely. A stalk is judged on whether the team is detected, not on whether it arrives fast. Movement is inches at a time when under observation; the team freezes on any sign of detection; routes use every fold in the ground. A failed stalk is a training failure, not a reason to rush the next one.

## 5. Mission Preparation and Operations

**5.1 The planning sequence.** The sniper team uses planning factors to estimate the time, coordination, and effort a mission needs, then works the sequence: receive the mission; study METT-T; coordinate with the SEO and the supported unit; draw arms, ammunition, and equipment (METT-T dependent — a planning figure is 100 rounds M118, M9 pistol, M9 bayonet); rehearse; and plan insertion, execution, and extraction as three separate problems. The team briefs back: the commander must hear the plan, not assume it.

**5.2 Insertion, execution, extraction.** The three phases each get their own coordination: insertion (how the team gets in — foot, vehicle, helicopter — and what support it needs); execution (the firing position, the target set, the engagement criteria, the observation and reporting task); and extraction (the route out, the rally points, the recovery plan, what happens if the team is compromised). The SEO aids the team in coordinating air support for all three phases. The team never plans a mission it cannot extract from.

**5.3 Communications.** The basic requirement is rapid, reliable, secure interchange of information. The team carries a man-portable radio giving secure communications with the units involved in the mission. Radio discipline is strict: short transmissions, prearranged brevity, and an understanding that every transmission can be direction-found. The team also knows and uses non-radio signals and the reporting formats — SALT spot reports and the standard intelligence report formats (STANAG 2020/2022) the manual cites.

**5.4 Reporting.** What the team sees is often more valuable than what it shoots. Observation is reported in SALT format (Size, Activity, Location, Time), with the location given precisely enough for fires or maneuver to act on it. The observation log is the raw record; the spot report is the product. Timely reporting of a column moving or a command post located can matter more than a single kill.

## 6. Tracking and Countertracking

**6.1 Tracking.** When a sniper follows a trail, he builds a picture of the enemy in his mind by asking: How many persons am I following? What is their state of training? How are they equipped? Tracking reads sign — footprints, broken vegetation, displaced stones, litter, scent — and ages it: fresh sign is sharp-edged and moist; old sign is weathered and dry. The tracker works in a team: one man reads sign, the other provides security and records. The tracker never outruns his security, and never follows a trail into an ambush — a trail that gets too easy is a trap until proven otherwise.

**6.2 Countertracking.** The sniper team is itself tracked, so it learns to defeat tracking: move on hard ground, step on rocks and roots, brush out sign, use streams, split up and rejoin, lay false trails, and move at night or in rain when sign degrades fastest. Countertracking is not a single trick — it is a mindset applied to every movement: leave nothing, and where you must leave something, make it misleading. Full tracking/countertracking procedure, sign-aging indicators, and the tracker-team drill are in `references/fieldcraft-and-tracking.md`.

## 7. Sustainment Training and Qualification

**7.1 Qualification.** The sniper qualifies on firing tables No. 1 and No. 2 (Figure 9-13 scorecard). Although the M24's maximum effective range is 800 meters, it can effectively hit targets at 1,000 meters — engaging at 1,000 meters is a confidence builder in the sniper's ability, and qualification standards require the sniper to meet the tables' standards, not merely to shoot well on a calm day.

**7.2 Sustainment.** Repetitive training in long-range marksmanship and fieldcraft ensures the best probability of effective engagement and the sniper's survival. Sustainment includes: known-distance and unknown-distance firing; range estimation drills; observation exercises (Kim's game); stalking lanes; tracking lanes; night firing with the AN/PVS-4; and team drills (hide occupation, target handover between sniper and observer). Skills decay in weeks, not years — the manual treats sustainment as a scheduled program with standards, not as range time when convenient.

**7.3 The data book as a training tool.** The sniper data book is the thread through all of it: every zero, every sight change, every range estimate against confirmed range, every shot with its weather and light conditions. A sniper who does not keep a data book is guessing; a team that reviews its data book is improving.

## Output Contract

When asked about sniping, long-range shooting, or sniper employment, answer from this manual's system: weapon and ammunition first (M24/M118 numbers), then ballistics (zero, MOA, wind, angle), then fieldcraft (hide, stalk, observation), then the mission (METT-T, three phases, SALT reporting). Give numbers where the manual gives them (800 m max effective range, 2,794 fps, 173-grain M118, 1 MOA clicks, 100-round planning figure) and say so when a number is a rule of thumb rather than a table value. Never present modern hardware (M2010, .300 Win Mag, current optics) as if it were in this manual — if the question needs modern kit, say the manual is the 1994 M24/M21 baseline and bridge explicitly. Never present the wind formula's constant as exact without the range-dependent caveat. For any question about employing a sniper team, include the extraction plan — a mission plan without one is incomplete by this doctrine.

## Operating Rules

1. **The team is two men, and the observer is half the weapon.** Never answer a sniper question as if the shooter works alone.
2. **The data book is the memory.** Every zero, estimate, and shot is recorded; unrecorded shooting is unrepeatable shooting.
3. **Range estimation is cross-checked.** Never trust a single method — mil-relation against appearance, bracketing, and the range card.
4. **Wind and angle are computed before the shot, not excused after it.** Temperature, altitude, slope dope, and wind value are part of the firing solution.
5. **Concealment is a discipline, not a garment.** Ghillie, hide, movement, noise, and light discipline all have to hold — one failure compromises the team.
6. **Plan extraction before insertion.** A mission without an extraction plan is not a plan.
7. **This is a 1994 manual.** M24/M21, M118, M3A, AN/PVS-4. The fieldcraft and ballistics method transfer; the hardware baseline does not. Say which is which.
