# Findings by Category — ARI Technical Report 970 (1993)

Distilled from Keene and Halpin, *How Well Did the Combat Training Centers Prepare Units for Combat?*, ARI Technical Report 970, January 1993. Questionnaire data from Desert Storm participants: 10,000 questionnaires distributed April–June 1991, 2,463 returned, 1,318 answered the surprise question, 837 had at least one CTC rotation.

## The Twelve Categories

964 responses (multiple per person): Methods and Procedures 156; POWs/EPWs 107; CTC Training-Related 107; Logistics/Supply 101; Weapons/Equipment Effectiveness 100; Mobility/Navigation 94; Morale/Attitudes 84; Communications 78; Maintenance 58; Enemy 35; Casualties–Friendly 34; Hazards 10.

**Reading the numbers.** The counts are of responses, not people — one person could report several surprises, and categories overlap. A high count means many respondents independently named the same surprise; a low count does not mean the issue was unimportant, only that fewer respondents named it unprompted.

## Speed and Tempo

The dominant surprise was the speed and distance of the advance. Units outpaced their planning — "fight on the move," the TOC never set up, FRAGOs only, "mission upon mission with no planning time." Sleep and rest plans failed: catnaps were insufficient, and one battalion commander was "near combat ineffective near the ceasefire." Intelligence dried up once the force was moving. Units were used in untrained roles — the 82nd Airborne moved by vehicle, not light-infantry tactics; an ADA command sergeant major served as transportation. The CTCs had not trained the 50–60 km per day advance the ground war demanded.

## EPWs Overwhelmed the System

A CTC squadron processes 5–10 prisoners per rotation; in Desert Storm, units took **300–500 prisoners per day**. At the CTCs the EPW problem was handled by a "magic wand" — in combat it pulled leaders out of the battle to manage prisoners.

## NBC Training

NBC training had been "viewed as a joke" for 10 years — and the chemical threat was real. Units were not prepared for chemical operations, and chemical casualty care was not trained.

## Logistics

CSS was not stressed at the CTCs: fuel consumption was a surprise, units outran their logistics, Class IX (repair parts) shortages hit, and the forward support battalion had only a truck platoon and no air assets. Units begged, borrowed, and scrounged.

## Weapons and Equipment

84 of 100 weapons responses were positive: the M1A1, M2, and M3A2 were "awesome"; the 25mm penetrated the T-54/55; the TOW 2A defeated the T-72 beyond 3,000 meters; DPICM was phenomenal. The negatives: the SAW in sand and its penetration; the CUCV M1008/M1009 useless off-road with cooling problems; the NBC warning system; the MICLIC; thermal-sight failure and IFF — "THE biggest problem"; M16 cleaning in sand.

## Mobility and Navigation

The flat, featureless desert made map navigation "virtually impossible." GPS was a must: "If we had not had Magellan GPSs, squads and platoons would have been lost." Under 20% illumination it was "like walking through a closet with the light out." Respondents said more GPS units belong in the TOE.

## Morale

15% reported morale surprisingly good, 12% low. Boredom, poor mail, unknown redeployment date, the rumor mill, and "real fear" dragged it down — but morale was higher in combat than in NTC simulation.

## Communications

79% of communications respondents reported significant problems (62 of 78): extended distances and outdated equipment. "Commo stunk." Respondents called for a secure on-the-move system.

## Maintenance

55% reported maintenance problems, 36% said it was surprisingly good, and some units reported operational readiness above 90%. Sand and heat took their toll, and Class IX shortages bit — while stockpiles and hoarding at the CTCs had masked the problem in training.

## Enemy, Casualties, Hazards

**Enemy (35 responses):** all 35 were surprised at the enemy's lack of will to fight and poor equipment; the T-72 was "vastly overrated." **Casualties (34):** the casualty system was "broken" — the first report was always incorrect; a division casualty section of 2 enlisted men and 1 officer was "a disgrace"; units were not prepared for mass casualties — though far fewer casualties occurred than expected or than NTC had simulated. **Hazards (10):** unexploded DPICM and CBU submunitions — soldiers picked them up, and DPICM caused friendly casualties.
