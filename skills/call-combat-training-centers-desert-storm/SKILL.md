# CALL: How Well Did the Combat Training Centers Prepare Units for Combat?

## Purpose

ARI Technical Report 970, *How Well Did the Combat Training Centers Prepare Units for Combat? Questionnaire Results from Desert Storm Participants* (S. Delane Keene and Stanley M. Halpin, U.S. Army Research Institute, January 1993, 31 pages) — a survey-results report, not a lessons-learned manual. It asks one question: "Describe any major surprises that your previous experience at combat training centers did not prepare you for." The answers are questionnaire data — self-reported surprises from Desert Storm participants — and must be read as such, not as verified lessons.

**The survey.** 10,000 questionnaires distributed April–June 1991; **2,463 returned; 1,318 answered the surprise question; 837 had at least one CTC rotation** (85% officers, 15% warrant officers, NCOs, and enlisted; CPT 387, MAJ 138, LTC 68, 1LT 84, SGM 40). Average rotations: NTC 2.51, JRTC 1.29, CMTC 1.75, BCTP 1.75. The four CTCs: NTC at Fort Irwin, CA (heavy, early 1980s); JRTC at Fort Chaffee, AR (light); CMTC at Hohenfels, Germany (Europe heavy); BCTP at Fort Leavenworth, KS (division and corps staff). Appendix A covers the 481 respondents with no CTC experience.

**Source quality note.** Scattered OCR errors in the extraction ("desert" as "dessert," "unlimnited") — the narrative is readable throughout.

**Reading the numbers.** 964 responses came from 1,318 people who answered the surprise question, and one person could report several surprises — so the counts are of responses, not of people, and the categories overlap. A high count means many respondents independently named the same surprise; a low count (Enemy 35, Hazards 10) does not mean the issue was unimportant, only that fewer respondents named it unprompted. Percentages in this skill are of the respondents who answered that category, not of the whole survey.

## 2. Speed and Tempo — the Advance Outran Everything

The dominant surprise was the speed and distance of the advance. Units outpaced their planning — "fight on the move," the TOC never set up, FRAGOs only, "mission upon mission with no planning time." Sleep and rest plans failed: catnaps were insufficient, and one battalion commander was "near combat ineffective near the ceasefire." Intelligence dried up once the force was moving. Units were used in untrained roles — the 82nd Airborne moved by vehicle, not light-infantry tactics; an ADA command sergeant major served as transportation. The CTCs had not trained the 50–60 km per day advance the ground war demanded.

## 3. EPWs Overwhelmed the System

A CTC squadron processes 5–10 prisoners per rotation; in Desert Storm, units took **300–500 prisoners per day**. At the CTCs the EPW problem was handled by a "magic wand" — in combat it pulled leaders out of the battle to manage prisoners. EPW handling was the second-most-reported surprise category (107 responses).

## 4. NBC Training Was "Viewed as a Joke"

NBC training had been "viewed as a joke" for 10 years — and the chemical threat was real. Units were not prepared for chemical operations, and chemical casualty care was not trained (Appendix A respondents raised this too).

## 5. Logistics — CSS Was Never Stressed at the CTCs

Logistics and supply drew 101 responses. CSS was not stressed at the CTCs: fuel consumption was a surprise, units outran their logistics, Class IX (repair parts) shortages hit, and the forward support battalion had only a truck platoon and no air assets. Units begged, borrowed, and scrounged.

## 6. Weapons — Mostly Awesome, With Real Failures

84 of 100 weapons responses were positive: the M1A1, M2, and M3A2 were "awesome"; the 25mm penetrated the T-54/55; the TOW 2A defeated the T-72 beyond 3,000 meters; DPICM was phenomenal. The negatives: the SAW in sand and its penetration; the CUCV M1008/M1009 useless off-road with cooling problems; the NBC warning system; the MICLIC; thermal-sight failure and IFF — "THE biggest problem"; M16 cleaning in sand.

## 7. Mobility and Navigation — GPS Was a Must

The flat, featureless desert made map navigation "virtually impossible." GPS was a must: "If we had not had Magellan GPSs, squads and platoons would have been lost." Under 20% illumination it was "like walking through a closet with the light out." Respondents said more GPS units belong in the TOE. The 50–60 km per day advance was not trained at NTC.

## 8. Morale — Higher in Combat Than at NTC

15% reported morale surprisingly good, 12% low. Boredom, poor mail, unknown redeployment date, the rumor mill, and "real fear" dragged it down — but morale was higher in combat than in NTC simulation. → `references/findings-by-category.md`

## 9. Communications — "Commo Stunk"

79% of communications respondents reported significant problems (62 of 78): extended distances and outdated equipment. "Commo stunk." Respondents called for a secure on-the-move system.

## 10. Maintenance — Sand, Heat, and Hoarding

55% reported maintenance problems, 36% said it was surprisingly good, and some units reported operational readiness above 90%. Sand and heat took their toll, and Class IX shortages bit — while stockpiles and hoarding at the CTCs had masked the problem in training.

## 11. The Enemy, Casualties, and Hazards

**Enemy (35 responses):** all 35 were surprised at the enemy's lack of will to fight and poor equipment; the T-72 was "vastly overrated." **Casualties (34):** the casualty system was "broken" — the first report was always incorrect; a division casualty section of 2 enlisted men and 1 officer was "a disgrace"; units were not prepared for mass casualties — though far fewer casualties occurred than expected or than NTC had simulated. **Hazards (10):** unexploded DPICM and CBU submunitions — soldiers picked them up, and DPICM caused friendly casualties.

## 12. The Verdict on the CTCs

23% said the CTCs were harder than combat — "NTC rotations are harder than this war." 17% reported no surprises. 36% named specific changes: long-range communications and transport, host-nation support, OPFOR tactics, EPW numbers, and fratricide — "CMTC doesn't teach us that we kill more of our own than the enemy does." The CTCs proved their worth: realism and intensity, low casualties, morale, weapons confidence, and teaching units to "think on their feet."

## 13. Conclusions and Recommendations

The report's negative themes: the long rapid advance; communications and supply failures; the EPW burden; desert maintenance; fratricide, IFF, and navigation; unexploded ordnance; morale, sleep, intelligence, and NBC. Recommendations: communications and navigation are equipment-acquisition problems (including a GPS procurement contingency); logistics and EPW handling need contingency plans and analysis — a potential "show stopper"; training solutions for UXO handling, IFF, NBC, and sleep/rest discipline; and Reserve/Active integration — "One Army" — through education. → `references/conclusions-recommendations.md`

## Appendix A — Respondents With No CTC Experience

481 respondents had no CTC rotation. Their top categories: Methods 89, Morale 73, Comms 70, Logistics 67, Maintenance 53, Mobility 35, Training 35, Weapons 27, POW 23, Casualties 18, Enemy 12, Hazards 7. Unique themes: "No one followed doctrine"; Reserve/Active friction — "Poor attitude of Active army towards USAR/NG troops"; too-old Reservists; joint-service friction — the Air Force "made their own rules"; cold-weather-only training; Scuds; mass casualties from a Scud rear-area attack; chemical casualty care not trained.

## Output Contract

When answering a question from this report, give the finding first, then the numbers behind it (respondent counts, percentages), then the caveat: this is questionnaire data — self-reported surprises from 1991, not verified lessons learned. Cite the category and the count. Where the report's recommendations touch current doctrine or equipment (GPS, IFF, EPW handling), say what the report recommended and note that current practice may differ.

## Operating Rules

1. **Questionnaire data, not lessons.** Report what respondents said; never present a survey response as a verified lesson.
2. **Counts are the evidence.** Give the numbers (2,463 returned, 1,318 answered, 837 with CTC rotations; category counts) — they are what makes a finding strong or weak.
3. **The CTCs worked — and had gaps.** Hold both: 23% said CTCs were harder than combat, and the same force reported comms, EPW, logistics, and fratricide surprises.
4. **Name the "show stoppers."** Logistics and EPW handling were the report's candidate show stoppers — contingency plans, not just training fixes.
