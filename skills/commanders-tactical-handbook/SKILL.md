# Commander's Tactical Handbook

## Purpose

MCRP 3-11.1A *Commander's Tactical Handbook* (U.S. Marine Corps, 30 July 1988, 287 pages) is a loose-leaf field reference that compresses the working knowledge of a Marine company-level commander into one book: troop-leading steps, the five-paragraph order, offensive and defensive operations, military operations other than war, specific tactical operations, patrolling, and fire support — plus eighteen appendices covering machine guns, weapons handling, NBC defense, communications, landing zones, fire plan sketches, roadblocks, prisoners of war, patrol coordination, close air support, weapons capabilities, threat weapons, antiterrorism, symbology, tactical terms, metric conversion, and a glossary.

Core idea, stated in the foreword: **make orders concise, clear, and simple; give subordinates the intent; integrate subordinate elements in coordinated action; and allow subordinates freedom of action consistent with coordination.** The handbook is a guide, not a replacement for doctrine — it tells a leader what to consider and in what order, and points to the doctrinal publication for the detail. Its epigraph, from General A. M. Gray, frames the whole book: *"In tactics, the most important thing is not whether you go left or right, but why you go left or right."*

This skill distills the handbook's structure: troop-leading steps and the five-paragraph order; Part I maneuver (offensive, defensive, operations other than war, specific tactical operations, patrolling); Part II fire support; and the appendices most useful in the field.

> **Note on vintage.** This is a 1988 publication. Some specifics are dated — NBC defense procedures, communications equipment, aircraft capabilities in Appendix K, threat weapon characteristics in Appendix M, and some organizational references reflect the late Cold War. The planning procedures, order format, tactical fundamentals, and fire support procedures are the durable core. Verify equipment and threat data against current references.

## 1. Troop-Leading Steps — The Leader's Planning Sequence

The handbook opens with the six troop-leading steps, the sequence a leader follows from receipt of mission to execution:

1. **Begin planning.** Plan the use of available time. Estimate the situation using **METT-T**: Mission, Enemy, Terrain and weather, Troops and support available, Time available.
   - *Mission:* the task assigned — who, what, where, when — and the commander's intent (why).
   - *Enemy:* composition and disposition by **SALUTE** (Size, Activity, Location, Unit, Time, Equipment) and strength; capabilities and limitations to **DRAW-D** (Defend, Reinforce, Attack, Withdraw, Delay); the enemy's most probable course of action; vulnerabilities to exploit.
   - *Terrain and weather:* evaluated by **KOCOA-W** — Key terrain, Observation and fields of fire, Cover and concealment, Obstacles, Avenues of approach, and Weather/astronomical data.
   - *Troops and support available:* people, human factors, equipment, logistics, fire support, space.
   - *Time available:* the **1/3–2/3 rule** (the leader uses one-third of the available time for his own planning and gives two-thirds to subordinates), backward planning, and parallel/concurrent planning.
   - Then issue the **warning order**.
2. **Arrange.** Movement of the unit (where, when, how); reconnaissance (select route, personnel, and method — route, aerial, map, vantage point); issue of the order (notify subordinate leaders of time and place); coordination with adjacent and supporting units.
3. **Make reconnaissance.** Update METT-T; develop the enemy's most probable course of action; confirm enemy vulnerabilities.
4. **Complete the plan.** The concept of operations is driven by METT-T, with emphasis on the mission (including intent) and the enemy's most probable course of action. Develop the scheme of maneuver to exploit the enemy's vulnerability by placing him in a dilemma.
5. **Issue the order.** Use the five-paragraph order format (see §2), with a terrain model, overlays, and a vantage point when possible.
6. **Supervise.** Use brief-backs, rehearsals, and inspections. Supervision is not micromanagement — it is confirming that subordinates understand the intent and are ready to execute.

## 2. The Five-Paragraph Order Format (Company Level)

The order format is the skeleton every operation hangs on. A map and/or terrain model orientation is normally given before the order is issued.

1. **Situation.**
   - *Friendly forces:* higher unit mission (task and intent); adjacent unit missions (left, front, right, rear); supporting unit missions; attachments and detachments with effective date-time.
   - *Enemy forces:* composition, disposition, and strength by SALUTE; capabilities and limitations by DRAW-D; most probable course of action.
2. **Mission.** The task to be accomplished and its purpose — who, what, when, where, and why — in one clear sentence.
3. **Execution.**
   - *Commander's intent and concept of operations:* intent first, then the scheme of maneuver and the fire support plan.
   - *Subordinate element missions:* main effort, supporting efforts, reserve.
   - *Coordinating instructions:* instructions common to two or more elements.
4. **Administration and Logistics.** Administration; logistics — the handbook's mnemonic is **"beans, bullets, bandages, bad guys, and batteries"** (rations, ammunition, medical, prisoners, and power).
5. **Command and Signal.** Signal (communications plan); command (location of key leaders, chain of command and succession).
   - Close with: *"Any questions? The time is now ______."*

## 3. Offensive Operations

The handbook's offensive section gives general coordinating instructions for attack orders, then covers each operation type in the same practical format — considerations, planning factors, and coordinating instructions:

- **Movement to contact** — gaining contact with the enemy on favorable terms; the form of maneuver is chosen by the situation, and the unit must be ready to fight the moment contact is made.
- **Attack of a fortified position** — deliberate preparation, suppression, breaching, and assault; fortifications demand more fire support, more time, and more rehearsal than any other attack.
- **Mechanized attack** — mounted maneuver with infantry and armor integrated; speed and shock, but with the coordination burden of two arms moving together.
- **Night attack** — darkness is a weapon for the attacker who rehearses and a disaster for one who does not; control measures, lighting, and recognition signals are the difference.
- **Helicopterborne operations** — air assault planning: landing zones, air movement, and the ground tactical plan as one operation.
- **Military operations on urbanized terrain (MOUT)** — room-by-room fundamentals, including the fundamentals of securing a room (see references). Urban combat is slow, casualty-heavy, and unforgiving of sloppy clearance.
- **Amphibious helicopterborne and surface assault** — the ship-to-shore movement as a tactical operation, with its own planning and coordination demands.

**Attack order general coordinating instructions** (common to two or more elements): order of movement and formations; consolidation instructions — security, automatic weapons, fields of fire, entrench (the handbook's **SAFE** mnemonic: Security, Automatic weapons, Fields of fire, Entrench), and other measures; displacement instructions.

## 4. Defensive Operations

The defense section is the handbook's longest, and it is built around the same discipline as the offense: plan by METT-T, then build the position to the plan.

- **General information and general coordinating instructions for defense orders** — the defense is planned as a system: positions, obstacles, fires, and the reserve, mutually supporting.
- **Reserve considerations** — the reserve is the commander's tool for the unexpected: its composition, location, and commitment criteria are decided in the plan, not in the crisis.
- **Counterattack considerations** — the counterattack is planned before the battle, with triggers, routes, and fire support already coordinated.
- **Defensive MOUT** — defending in cities: buildings as strongpoints, the canalizing effect of streets, and the difficulty of disengaging.
- **Countermechanized operations** — defeating armor: the engagement area is the key tool (see below).
- **Engagement areas** — the handbook's most detailed defensive concept, and worth studying closely. An engagement area is a piece of ground where the commander intends to destroy the enemy with massed fires. Building one requires: (a) analyzing the enemy (size, configuration, distribution between echelons, composition of each, and time available); (b) analyzing terrain and weather (identify restricted avenues of approach that limit the enemy's ability to deploy; find the avenue that gives the best cover and concealment, avenues of egress for disengagement, and employment of weapons at maximum range; decide whether terrain and weather favor massed surprise or concentric fires); (c) troops and support available — overwhelm the enemy with different threats to put him in a dilemma; (d) time available — how fast the enemy moves, how many shots can be taken into the engagement area, how fast disengagement and egress can be accomplished, and how much ammunition is required (pre-staged at subsequent positions). Refinement: obstacles are used to stop, split, or divert the enemy column and reduce the width of the engagement area; direct-fire weapons are positioned with the knowledge that the enemy will target the ideal positions first.
- **Retrograde operations** — delay, withdrawal, and retirement, each with its own purpose, planning factors, and risks. A delay trades space for time; a withdrawal breaks contact; a retirement moves away from the enemy to a new mission. All three are harder to execute than an attack and demand more control measures.
- **Rear area security** — protecting the sustainment base, lines of communication, and command posts against infiltration, sabotage, and bypassed enemy forces.

## 5. Military Operations Other Than War

- **Counterinsurgency operations** — the handbook's treatment is compact but pointed: the population is the objective, and the force that alienates the population loses regardless of tactical success.
- **Noncombatant evacuation operations (NEO)** — planning and executing the evacuation of civilians from a hostile or deteriorating environment: assembly areas, screening, movement, and security.
- **Crowd and mob control** — techniques and escalation discipline for controlling crowds without turning a crowd into a riot.
- **Cordon and search operations** — isolating an area and searching it systematically: the cordon must be complete before the search begins, or the search is a sieve.

## 6. Specific Tactical Operations and Patrolling

- **Relief in place** — both sides of the relief (relieving unit and unit to be relieved), with the coordination, timing, and handover procedures that prevent a gap in the defense.
- **Passage of lines** — passing unit and stationary unit responsibilities; a passage is a vulnerable moment, and both units must know the plan.
- **Linkup operations** — joining two forces, with recognition signals and coordination as the critical factors.
- **Infiltration** — moving through enemy-held ground undetected; planning factors mirror the reconnaissance infiltration problem.
- **Convoy operations** — organization, security, and the response to ambush.

**Patrolling** gets its own section, and it is one of the handbook's most immediately usable parts:

- **Patrol steps** — the sequence from planning through debrief, with the patrol order information specified in the handbook's format.
- **Ambush patrol** — planning and executing an ambush: site selection, the kill zone, security, support, and assault elements, and the withdrawal.
- **Urban patrolling** — patrolling in cities, with its own techniques and risks.
- **Urban patrol tips** — the compact field wisdom: vary routes and times, watch rooftops and windows, control the high ground, and never become predictable.

**Relief in place, passage of lines, and linkup — the vulnerable moments.** The handbook treats these three operations together because they share one danger: two units in the same piece of ground, one of them moving, both of them exposed. In a relief in place, the relieving unit must know the position, the fire plan, the obstacle plan, and the enemy situation before it takes over — the handover is done by daylight where possible, by overlapping positions rather than by a clean swap, and the relieved unit does not leave until the relieving unit confirms it is ready to fight the position. In a passage of lines, the passing unit and the stationary unit agree in advance on the passage point, the route, the recognition signals, and the fire support that covers the passage; the stationary unit's fires cover the passing unit while it is most vulnerable, and the passing unit clears the passage point as fast as it can. In a linkup, recognition is the critical factor: two friendly forces approaching each other must identify each other before they shoot each other, and the handbook's linkup procedures — signals, checkpoints, and a defined linkup point — exist because fratricide at a linkup is a recurring failure in military history.

**Convoy operations** close the section: organization of the convoy (serials, march units, and the order of march), security (advance, flank, and rear guards; air cover where available), and the response to ambush — the convoy's drill is to drive through the kill zone where possible, return fire from the vehicles, and dismount only on command, because a stopped convoy in a kill zone is a destroyed convoy.

**Using the handbook as a loose-leaf reference.** The handbook was issued in loose-leaf format deliberately: a leader pulls the pages he needs for the operation at hand and leaves the rest behind. The practical way to use it is to treat the troop-leading steps and the five-paragraph order as the always-carried core, the maneuver sections as the planning reference, and the fire support section (with the call-for-fire format card) as the pocket reference carried on every operation. The appendices are the deep reference — pulled when the mission calls for them (an LZ brief before a helicopterborne operation, the EPW appendix before a mission that will take prisoners, the metric conversion chart when working with allied units). A leader who tries to memorize the whole book has missed its point; a leader who can find the right page in under a minute has understood it.

## 7. Fire Support — Part II

The handbook's second half is a fire support reference, and it is the part a company commander reaches for most often.

- **General fire support** — general information, company fire support planning, the suggested fire support coordinator SOP, fire support techniques, and the company fire support board.
- **Quick fire support plan** — a pre-formatted plan for rapid use: target list (target number, description, location, altitude, remarks) and a schedule (firing unit, organization, fire timings, remarks) — the "APPLE" fire plan format in the handbook's example.
- **Call for fire elements** — the components of a call for fire: observer identification, warning order, target location, target description, method of engagement, and method of fire and control.
- **Call for fire procedures** — the sequence from initial call through adjustment to fire for effect, including the format card (pages 183–184 of the handbook) that a forward observer carries.
- **Close air support (CAS)** — control procedures, the CAS briefing form, and the aircraft capabilities and munitions in Appendix K. The briefing form standardizes what the aircrew needs: target, location, friendly positions, threats, and attack heading.
- **Appendices A–R** — machine gun employment; weapons handling; NBC defense; communications and information systems; landing zone brief and markings; sample fire plan sketches; roadblocks and searches; enemy prisoners of war; patrol coordination; example patrol overlay; CAS aircraft capabilities and munitions; weapons capabilities; common threat weapon characteristics; antiterrorism procedures; common military symbology; common tactical terms, definitions, and symbols; metric conversion charts; glossary.

## Output Contract

When asked about a tactical problem, answer with the handbook's structure: (1) run METT-T and state the estimate; (2) give the operation type and the planning sequence (troop-leading steps → five-paragraph order); (3) list the coordinating instructions and the fire support plan; (4) state the control measures and the abort/decision criteria. If the user asks for a call for fire, give the full format in order. If the user asks for an engagement area, walk the build sequence (enemy, terrain, troops, time, refinement). Flag any 1988-era equipment or threat data as dated and point to current references.

## Operating Rules

1. **Intent before detail.** Subordinates get the mission and the intent first; detailed instructions for every contingency are a failure of command.
2. **METT-T drives everything.** No plan survives contact with a situation that was never estimated.
3. **1/3–2/3 rule.** The leader's planning time is one-third; subordinates get two-thirds.
4. **Supervise by brief-back and rehearsal.** If a subordinate cannot brief the plan back, the plan is not understood.
5. **Fires are planned, not wished for.** A fire support plan has targets, timings, and a board — not a hope that artillery will be available.
6. **The handbook is a guide, not doctrine.** For detailed tactics, go to the doctrinal publication; for the checklist in the field, use this book.
