# First Aid

## Purpose

FM 4-25.11, *First Aid* (U.S. Army, December 2002; joint publication FM 4-25.11 / NTRP 4-02.1 / AFMAN 44-163(I), superseding FM 21-11) — the standard first-aid manual for every soldier, sailor, and airman. This skill is for anyone who may be the first person on the scene of an injury: a soldier treating a buddy, a parent treating a child, a hiker treating a companion, or a civilian at a car crash. It assumes no medical training, no equipment beyond a first-aid kit and what can be improvised, and no help coming soon.

The core idea: **first aid is the care given before medical help arrives, and its purpose is to save life, prevent further injury, and preserve the casualty for evacuation.** The manual's sequence never changes — check the casualty, open the airway, restore breathing, stop the bleeding, check for shock — and it is built around vital body functions that fail in a fixed order. Most first-aid failures are failures of order, not of knowledge.

**Currency note:** this manual is from 2002. Its fundamentals — airway, breathing, bleeding, shock, splinting, bandaging, heat and cold injury — remain sound, but combat casualty care has moved on. Where this manual conflicts with the current standard, **ATP 4-02.11 (March 2026), distilled in the `tccc-casualty-response` skill, wins** — most importantly on tourniquet use (the 2002 manual treats the tourniquet as a last resort; current TCCC doctrine applies it early and aggressively for life-threatening extremity bleeding) and on hemorrhage control (hemostatic dressings, junctional tourniquets, and blood-product resuscitation, none of which existed in this manual's doctrine).

## 1. Fundamental Criteria — Vital Functions and the First Assessment

**1.1. The vital body functions.** Three systems keep a person alive, and first aid protects them in this order:

- **Respiration** — breathing. Without oxygen, brain damage begins in about 4–6 minutes. An open airway is the single most time-critical first-aid act.
- **Circulation** — the heartbeat and blood volume. Severe bleeding can kill in minutes; shock is the failure of circulation to deliver oxygen.
- **Nervous system** — consciousness and the ability to respond. Level of consciousness is your best quick measure of how bad things are.

**1.2. Normal vital signs (adult, at rest).** Pulse 60–100 beats per minute (tachycardia is over 100; bradycardia is under 60). Respirations 12–20 per minute. Body temperature about 98.6°F (37°C). Blood pressure is not measurable in the field without equipment — use pulse quality, skin color, and mental state instead. A casualty who is alert, warm, and pink is perfusing; a casualty who is confused, cool, pale, and sweaty is in trouble.

**1.3. Evaluating a casualty — the sequence.** (1) Check responsiveness: speak, then touch, then a painful stimulus. (2) Check breathing: look, listen, feel for 10 seconds. (3) Check for severe bleeding — look for blood pooling, spurting, or soaking through clothing. (4) Check for shock. (5) Check for fractures and other injuries, head to toe. (6) Treat in priority order: airway, breathing, bleeding, shock, then everything else. Never move a casualty with a suspected spine injury unless the scene is unsafe.

**1.4. Adverse conditions.** First aid happens in bad light, bad weather, under fire, and with the wrong tools. Protect yourself first — you cannot help anyone if you become a casualty. Use improvised materials freely: a shirt is a bandage, a stick is a splint, a belt is a sling. And remember the rule that runs through the whole manual: **do no further harm.** → `references/fundamentals-airway-bleeding-shock.md`

## 2. Basic Measures — Airway, Bleeding, Shock

**2.1. Open the airway and restore breathing.** A casualty who is unconscious and not breathing needs an airway first. Clear the mouth of blood, vomit, broken teeth, or debris with a finger sweep (only if you can see the object). Open the airway with the head-tilt/chin-lift method — one hand on the forehead tilting the head back, fingers of the other hand lifting the chin — or the jaw-thrust method if a neck injury is suspected (thrust the jaw forward without moving the head or neck). If the casualty is breathing on their own, place them in the recovery position on their side so fluids drain and the tongue cannot block the airway.

**2.2. Rescue breathing.** If the casualty is not breathing, give rescue breaths: pinch the nose, seal your mouth over theirs, and breathe in slowly until the chest rises. Give **one breath every 5 seconds for an adult — about 12 breaths per minute** — and recheck for breathing and pulse after about a minute. For a child, one breath every 3 seconds (about 20 per minute); for an infant, gentle puffs every 3 seconds. Stop and reassess if the chest does not rise — reposition the head and try again; a blocked airway may be the problem.

**2.3. Airway obstructions.** A conscious casualty who is choking will clutch at their throat (the universal sign), cannot speak or cough effectively, and turns blue. Give abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver): stand behind, fist just above the navel and below the ribs, quick inward-and-upward thrusts until the object is expelled or the casualty collapses. For a casualty lying down or unconscious, straddle the hips and deliver abdominal thrusts with the heel of the hand; follow with a finger sweep only if you can see the object. Chest thrusts replace abdominal thrusts for a pregnant casualty or a very obese casualty.

**2.4. Stop the bleeding and protect the wound.** Bleeding control in order: (1) **direct pressure** on the wound with a dressing; (2) **pressure dressing** — a field dressing bound firmly over the wound; (3) **digital pressure** on the pressure point upstream (brachial artery in the upper arm, femoral artery in the groin); (4) **tourniquet** — last resort in this manual's doctrine, for an extremity when all else has failed and life is at stake. Expose the wound first — cut clothing away; never probe a wound, never remove an impaled object, and never remove a dressing once applied (add more on top). Elevate the limb if no fracture is suspected. Check entrance and exit wounds on gunshot injuries and treat both.

**2.5. The field dressing and pressure dressing.** Apply the dressing directly to the wound, wrap the tails firmly around the limb, and tie. A pressure dressing adds a pad over the wound and wraps it tight enough to control bleeding but not so tight that it cuts off circulation below — check fingers or toes for color, warmth, and feeling after application, and loosen if they turn blue, cold, or numb.

**2.6. Shock — recognize it early.** Causes: blood loss, severe pain, burns, crushing injuries, dehydration. Signs and symptoms: **sweaty but cool (clammy) skin; paleness of skin** (in dark-skinned casualties, look at the lips, nail beds, and inside of the mouth); weak and rapid pulse; rapid shallow breathing; thirst; nausea; restlessness or confusion; dilated pupils. Treatment: lay the casualty flat, elevate the legs 6–12 inches (unless head, chest, or abdominal injury forbids it); keep warm — insulate from the ground; loosen tight clothing; reassure; give nothing to eat or drink if evacuation and possible surgery are ahead; treat the cause (stop the bleeding). Shock kills quietly and late — a casualty who looks "okay" after major blood loss is often in compensated shock and can crash suddenly. → `references/fundamentals-airway-bleeding-shock.md`

## 3. First Aid for Specific Injuries

**3.1. Head, neck, and facial injuries.** Suspect a spine injury with any head injury, any fall from height, or any casualty found unconscious. Do not move the casualty; stabilize the head and neck in the position found. Control scalp bleeding with direct pressure (the scalp bleeds heavily — a small cut can look catastrophic). Do not press on a depressed skull fracture or an eye that has been penetrated. Clear blood from the mouth so the casualty does not choke on it. A casualty with a head injury who becomes drowsy, vomits repeatedly, or has unequal pupils is deteriorating — evacuate urgently.

**3.2. Chest wounds.** A sucking chest wound — air bubbling or hissing through a hole in the chest wall — is life-threatening: air enters the chest and collapses the lung. Seal it immediately with an airtight material (plastic, foil, a field dressing wrapper) taped on three sides so air can escape but not enter, and place the casualty on the injured side so the good lung is uppermost. Watch breathing constantly; if breathing worsens after sealing, lift a corner of the seal to let trapped air out.

**3.3. Abdominal wounds.** Do not push protruding organs back in. Cover them with a clean, moist dressing, then a dry one over it; keep the casualty flat with knees slightly bent to relax the abdominal muscles; give nothing by mouth. Bleeding inside the abdomen cannot be stopped in the field — evacuate fast.

**3.4. Burns.** Cool with clean water; cover with a clean dry dressing; do not break blisters; do not apply grease, butter, or ointments. Estimate the burn area with the **Rule of Nines** — head and neck 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, front of torso 18%, back of torso 18%, perineum 1% — to judge severity and fluid needs. Burns over 20% of the body, burns of the face, hands, feet, or genitals, and any burn with breathing difficulty are urgent evacuations. Treat for shock: a burn casualty loses fluid fast. (Current doctrine in the TCCC skill adds fluid resuscitation rules and modern burn dressings.)

**3.5. Dressings and bandages.** The manual's bandaging chapter is a full catalog — shoulder, elbow, hand, leg, knee, and foot bandages — with a single governing principle: a bandage must be snug enough to hold the dressing and control bleeding, loose enough to preserve circulation, and it must be checked and rechecked. Always check circulation below a bandage (color, warmth, feeling) after applying it and every 15 minutes thereafter. → `references/specific-injuries-fractures.md`

## 4. Fractures — Splint as Found

**4.1. Kinds and signs.** Closed (skin intact) or open (bone through skin). Signs: deformity, swelling, pain on movement, tenderness, inability to use the limb, crepitus (grating), and a limb that looks wrong. Treat every suspected fracture as a fracture.

**4.2. Purposes of immobilizing.** Prevent further damage, reduce pain, prevent shock, and make transport possible.

**4.3. Splinting procedure.** (1) Remove jewelry and loosen clothing over the injury. (2) Splint the fracture **as found** — do not try to straighten it. (3) Immobilize the joint above and below the fracture. (4) Pad the splint (clothing, leaves, moss) so it does not press on the bone. (5) Tie snugly but not tight — check circulation below the splint after tying. (6) If the fracture is open, cover the wound with a clean dressing first, and do not push bone back in. Improvised splints: sticks, boards, rolled newspapers, a rifle, a folded blanket; slings from a shirt, belt, or bandage; swathes to bind an arm to the chest.

**4.4. Special fractures.** Jaw: support with a bandage under the chin, casualty leaning forward so blood drains from the mouth. Collarbone: sling and swathe. Shoulder: sling and swathe. Spine: do not move the casualty unless the scene is unsafe; if you must move them, log-roll as a unit with several people, keeping the spine straight; a rigid litter or board is ideal. Neck: stabilize in the position found, improvise a collar from a rolled towel or clothing, evacuate flat. → `references/specific-injuries-fractures.md`

## 5. Climatic Injuries — Heat and Cold

**5.1. Heat injuries (progressive).** Heat cramps → heat exhaustion → heatstroke. The progression is a warning system — treat early.

| Injury | Signs | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Heat cramps | Muscle cramps (legs, abdomen), sweating | Rest in shade; sip water with salt |
| Heat exhaustion | Heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, dizziness, cool clammy skin | Rest in shade; loosen clothing; cool with water and airflow; sip fluids |
| Heatstroke (emergency) | **Hot, dry skin — sweating stops**; confusion, seizures, unconsciousness; body temperature very high | **Cool immediately and aggressively** — immerse in water if possible, or pour water over the body and fan; evacuate urgently; do not give fluids to an unconscious casualty |

Heatstroke is a medical emergency that can kill in under an hour. It occurs more rapidly in people who are unacclimatized, dehydrated, or working hard in heat. Prevention: acclimatize gradually, drink on schedule (not just when thirsty), rest in shade, wear loose light clothing.

**5.2. Cold injuries.** Frostbite: pale, waxy, numb skin (fingers, toes, nose, ears). Rewarm in warm — not hot — water, only if refreezing is impossible; never rub frostbitten tissue; never use dry heat. Hypothermia: shivering, then confusion, clumsiness, slurred speech, then shivering stops — rewarm gradually with insulation, warm sweet liquids, and body heat; never give alcohol. Trench foot / immersion foot: prolonged wet cold — pale, cold, numb feet that swell and blister when rewarmed; prevention (dry socks, foot movement, changing socks) matters more than treatment. Snow blindness: severe eye pain and tearing after sun on snow — rest the eyes in darkness, cover with a cool damp cloth, improvise slit goggles from cardboard or bark. → `references/climatic-bites-stings.md`

## 6. Bites and Stings

**6.1. Snakebites.** Identify the snake if it can be done safely — do not risk a second bite to catch it. Pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths) cause severe local pain, swelling, and bruising; coral snakes cause little local pain but dangerous systemic effects (numbness, difficulty breathing). First aid: keep the casualty calm and still, keep the bitten limb at heart level or slightly below, remove rings and watches before swelling starts, immobilize the limb with a splint, do NOT cut the wound, do NOT suck out venom, do NOT apply a tourniquet, do NOT apply ice. Evacuate — antivenin is the treatment, and it is a hospital treatment.

**6.2. Human and animal bites.** Clean thoroughly with soap and clean water; cover with a dressing; evacuate for evaluation — human bites and animal bites (especially rabies risk) need medical care and often antibiotics.

**6.3. Marine animals.** Jellyfish: rinse with salt water (not fresh), remove tentacles with a stick or gloved hand, apply vinegar if available. Stingray: immerse in hot water (as hot as tolerable) to break down the venom; remove the spine only if it is superficial and safe. Stonefish and cone shells: evacuate urgently.

**6.4. Insects and arthropods.** Remove stingers by scraping (not squeezing — squeezing injects more venom). Apply cold; watch for anaphylaxis — difficulty breathing, swelling of the face and throat, rapid pulse, collapse — which needs immediate evacuation and, if available, epinephrine. Ticks: grasp close to the skin with tweezers and pull straight out; clean the bite; watch for a spreading rash or fever in the following weeks. → `references/climatic-bites-stings.md`

## 7. NBC Environment and Psychological Reactions

**7.1. First aid in a nuclear, biological, and chemical environment.** The priorities: protect yourself (mask and protective clothing first — a casualty who is contaminated will contaminate the rescuer), remove the casualty from the contaminated area, and decontaminate skin with water and soap before treating wounds. Chemical agents: nerve agents (signs — pinpoint pupils, runny nose, drooling, difficulty breathing, convulsions; treatment — atropine and 2-PAM autoinjectors per the Mark I kit, repeated as directed), blister agents (reddening and blisters hours after exposure; flush with water; do not break blisters), choking agents (coughing, choking, fluid in the lungs; rest and oxygen), blood agents (rapid collapse; give fresh air and artificial respiration). Biological agents: symptoms appear hours to days later; first aid is supportive and evacuation is the priority. Nuclear: treat burns and wounds first; radiation injury itself has no field first aid beyond decontamination and evacuation.

**7.2. Psychological reactions.** Combat and disaster produce combat stress reactions — shaking, confusion, inability to function, withdrawal, or panic — and these are normal responses to abnormal stress, not cowardice. First aid: keep the casualty safe, warm, and fed; give simple, purposeful tasks; reassure and stay with them; do not leave them alone. Most casualties recover rapidly with rest, food, and reassurance and can return to duty. Those who do not improve need evacuation. (The current standard for this is in the TCCC skill, Chapter 20 — combat and operational stress control.)

**7.3. Rescue and transportation (Appendix B).** Improvised litters from poles and a poncho, blanket, or shirts; carries (two-person, fireman's carry, pack-strap carry) chosen by the casualty's injuries and the terrain. The rule for every move: move the casualty as a unit, protect the spine, and never move a casualty farther or rougher than the situation demands. → `references/nbc-psychological-transport.md`

## Output Contract

When asked a first-aid question, answer in the manual's fixed order: (1) what is immediately life-threatening and what to do first, (2) the step-by-step procedure with the numbers (breath rates, shock signs, splint rules), (3) what NOT to do (the manual's prohibitions are as important as its instructions — no tourniquet for snakebite, no pushing organs back in, no straightening fractures, no rubbing frostbite), (4) when to evacuate and how urgently. Cite the chapter (e.g., "FM 4-25.11, Ch. 2 — Basic Measures"). If the situation is a combat or tactical casualty, say so and defer to the `tccc-casualty-response` skill — it is the current standard and it supersedes this manual where they differ.

## Operating Rules

1. **Airway, breathing, bleeding, shock — always in that order.** Never treat a fracture before a blocked airway.
2. **Do no further harm.** The prohibitions are instructions: never probe a wound, never remove an impaled object, never straighten a fracture, never rub frostbite, never cut or suck a snakebite.
3. **Check circulation after every bandage and splint.** Color, warmth, feeling — below the injury, every 15 minutes.
4. **Treat for shock in every serious injury.** Shock is silent, late, and lethal; lay flat, elevate legs, keep warm.
5. **Evacuate early.** Field first aid buys time; it is not treatment. A casualty who "looks okay" after major injury can crash.
6. **This manual is 2002 doctrine.** Where it conflicts with ATP 4-02.11 (2026), the 2026 ATP wins — especially tourniquets, hemostatic dressings, and fluid resuscitation.
7. **Protect yourself first.** Mask, gloves, and scene safety come before touching a casualty.
