# First-Aid Fundamentals, Airway, Bleeding, and Shock

Source: FM 4-25.11, Chapters 1 (Fundamental Criteria) and 2 (Basic Measures for First Aid), Appendix A (First Aid Case and Kits).

## Chapter outline

- Ch. 1: General; terminology; vital body functions; adverse conditions; basics of first aid; evaluating a casualty
- Ch. 2: Section I — open the airway and restore breathing; Section II — stop the bleeding and protect the wound; Section III — check for shock

## Vital functions and normal values (adult, at rest)

| Function | Normal | Danger sign |
|---|---|---|
| Pulse | 60–100 beats/min | >100 (tachycardia) or <60 (bradycardia); weak and rapid in shock |
| Respirations | 12–20/min | Rapid and shallow in shock; absent = emergency |
| Temperature | ~98.6°F (37°C) | High in heatstroke; low in hypothermia |
| Consciousness | Alert, oriented | Confusion, drowsiness, unresponsiveness = deteriorating |

## Casualty evaluation — the sequence

1. Responsiveness: speak → touch → painful stimulus.
2. Breathing: look, listen, feel for 10 seconds.
3. Severe bleeding: pooling, spurting, soaking clothing.
4. Shock signs.
5. Head-to-toe check for fractures and other injuries.
6. Treat in priority order: airway → breathing → bleeding → shock → everything else.

Never move a casualty with a suspected spine injury unless the scene is unsafe.

## Airway

- Clear the mouth (finger sweep only if you can see the object).
- Head-tilt/chin-lift: one hand on forehead, fingers lifting chin.
- Jaw-thrust: if a neck injury is suspected — thrust jaw forward without moving the head/neck.
- Recovery position (on the side) for a breathing unconscious casualty, so fluids drain and the tongue cannot block the airway.

## Rescue breathing

| Casualty | Rate |
|---|---|
| Adult | 1 breath every 5 seconds (~12/min) |
| Child | 1 breath every 3 seconds (~20/min) |
| Infant | Gentle puffs every 3 seconds |

Breathe slowly until the chest rises. Recheck breathing and pulse after about a minute. If the chest does not rise, reposition the head and try again.

## Choking (airway obstruction)

- Conscious: universal sign (clutching throat), cannot speak or cough effectively. Abdominal thrusts (Heimlich): fist above navel, below ribs; quick inward-and-upward thrusts.
- Lying down or unconscious: straddle hips, heel of hand abdominal thrusts; finger sweep only if the object is visible.
- Pregnant or very obese casualty: chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts.

## Bleeding control — in order

1. Direct pressure on the wound with a dressing.
2. Pressure dressing (field dressing bound firmly over the wound).
3. Digital pressure on the pressure point upstream (brachial artery, upper arm; femoral artery, groin).
4. Tourniquet — last resort in this 2002 manual, for an extremity when all else fails. **Current doctrine (ATP 4-02.11, 2026) applies tourniquets early for life-threatening extremity bleeding — see the `tccc-casualty-response` skill.**

Rules: expose the wound (cut clothing away); never probe a wound; never remove an impaled object; never remove a dressing once applied (add more on top); elevate the limb if no fracture is suspected; treat both entrance and exit wounds.

## Pressure dressing — circulation check

After application, check fingers/toes below the dressing for color, warmth, and feeling. If blue, cold, or numb, loosen. Recheck every 15 minutes.

## Shock

Signs: sweaty but cool (clammy) skin; paleness (in dark-skinned casualties, check lips, nail beds, inside of mouth); weak and rapid pulse; rapid shallow breathing; thirst; nausea; restlessness or confusion; dilated pupils.

Treatment: lay flat; elevate legs 6–12 inches (unless head, chest, or abdominal injury forbids it); keep warm and insulate from the ground; loosen tight clothing; reassure; give nothing by mouth if surgery may follow; treat the cause (stop the bleeding).

## First-aid kit contents (Appendix A)

Individual first-aid kit: field dressings, bandages, triangular bandage/cravat, adhesive tape, tourniquet (in current kits), gloves, and — for chemical environments — atropine/2-PAM autoinjectors. Unit kits add splints, litters, and bulk dressings. Know where your kit is and what is in it before you need it.
