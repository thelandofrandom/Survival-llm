# Mountain Warfare and Cold Weather Operations

## Purpose

ATP 3-90.97 *Mountain Warfare and Cold Weather Operations* (U.S. Army, April 2016, 156 pages) is the Army's operational-level doctrine for fighting in mountains and cold regions — the publication that sits above the small-unit techniques of ATP 3-21.50 and tells commanders and staffs how to plan and sustain operations in the world's most punishing terrain. This skill distills it into usable doctrine for a commander, staff officer, or senior leader: how the mountain environment shapes intelligence, operations, maneuver, engineering, sustainment, aviation, fires, communications, and training.

Core idea: **mountains compartmentalize everything.** Terrain, weather, and altitude break the battlefield into isolated pockets, canalize movement onto a handful of routes, and make every warfighting function slower, colder, and more expensive. The manual's through-line is that mountain operations are won or lost in planning and sustainment — the unit that arrives acclimatized, fed, watered, and warm can fight; the unit that does not is a casualty list before the enemy fires a shot.

This skill covers all ten chapters and five appendices: the environment, intelligence, operations, maneuver and movement, engineering, sustainment, aviation, fires, communications, training considerations, altitude and environmental hazards, mountain weather data, cold-weather uniform and equipment posture, heaters and tents, and military mountaineering equipment.

## 1. The Environment — Cordillera, Slope, and Snow

The principal mountain ranges of the world lie along broad belts called cordillera, encircling the Pacific basin and leading westward across Eurasia into North Africa, with secondary chains along the Atlantic margins of the Americas and Europe. Different chains have different climates: desert ranges swing from extreme summer heat to extreme winter cold; tropical mountains are jungle-covered with deep ravines that flood in the rainy season; high temperate mountains have sparse vegetation above 3,505 meters (11,500 feet) and below-freezing winters. A single area of operations can contain several mountain environments at once — Afghanistan is the manual's example.

- **Slopes** generally vary between 15 and 45 degrees; cliffs and rocky precipices may be near vertical or overhanging. Slope surfaces are relatively firm earth or grass, scattered with rocky debris. **Talus** (extensive boulder fields) is often an easy ascent route; **scree** (fist-sized or smaller loose rock) is difficult to climb because the rocks loosen and give way.
- **Snow** covers slopes in winter and at higher elevations year-round. Some snow conditions aid travel by covering rough terrain with a consistent surface; deep snow impedes movement and requires snowshoes, skis, and over-snow vehicles. Steep snow-covered terrain presents the risk of avalanches — and the manual is blunt: **avalanches have taken the lives of more troops engaged in mountain warfare than all other terrain hazards combined.**
- **Glaciated areas** in arctic, subarctic, and high-mountain regions present hidden crevasses and ice and snow avalanches. Valleys are buried under massive glaciers; slopes are combinations of rock, ice, and snow.
- **Wet cold** leads to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot. Wet cold conditions are not only uncomfortable — they are dangerous, and equipment, training, and leadership are critical. **Wind chill** is a complicating factor: leaders must know the combination of ambient temperature and wind their troops are experiencing (see Figure 1-5, the wind chill chart).

### Operational terrain levels

Mountain operations are carried out at three operational terrain levels, and the level determines what can move, fight, and survive there:

- **Level I — valley floors and main lines of communication.** Armored vehicles can operate, though decentralized into smaller units and complemented by dismounted forces. Civilian population centers are here. Level I is dominated by whoever holds Level II above it.
- **Level II — between valleys and mountain shoulders.** Narrow roads and trails, secondary lines of communication, crossing the ridge system. Enemy positions on Level III dominate Level II. Units expend the energy to occupy Level II because it dominates Level I and influences operations dramatically.
- **Level III — summit regions, the dominant terrain.** Mobility here is the most difficult to achieve and maintain. But Level III provides opportunities for well-trained, acclimatized units to attack the enemy from the flanks and rear. Only acclimatized troops with advanced mountaineering training can operate effectively at this level.

The rule that follows from the levels: **movement in Level II terrain dictates that elements secure the high ground in Level III terrain.** You cannot move through the middle ground without owning the heights above it.

## 2. Intelligence — Seeing in Compartmented Terrain

Mountain terrain degrades every intelligence discipline and makes the few that work more valuable.

- **Terrain and weather dominate intelligence preparation.** The enemy's options are as constrained as yours — the same passes, the same ridgelines, the same water sources. IPOE in mountains is terrain-first: identify the avenues of approach, the key terrain, the observation points, and the weather windows.
- **Collection is hard:** sensors are masked by terrain, weather grounds aerial platforms, and communications are degraded. Human intelligence, patrols, and observation posts become the primary sources.
- **The enemy is as constrained as you are.** His resupply, his movement, and his communications run through the same few routes. Find the routes and you find the enemy's logistics — and his vulnerabilities.
- **Deception works differently:** in mountains, a feint on one pass can fix an enemy while the main effort uses another — but only if the enemy believes both passes are usable, which requires the deceiving force to actually be able to move there.

## 3. Operations — How Mountains Change the Fight

Mountain operations have distinct characteristics that shape every decision:

- **Compartmentalization:** the battlefield breaks into isolated pockets. Units fight semi-independently, often out of sight and out of contact with each other. Command and control must be decentralized.
- **Limited mobility:** movement is slow, canalized, and exhausting. Time and elevation matter more than distance — foot marches in the mountains are measured in time and elevation rather than just distance (see §4).
- **Weather as a weapon:** weather can close a pass, ground aviation, mask fires, and kill more soldiers than the enemy. Plans must include weather branches and sequels.
- **Logistics dominance:** sustainment is the limiting factor on operations. A unit that cannot be fed, watered, warmed, and resupplied cannot fight, no matter how good its tactics.
- **Civilian population:** population centers are at lower elevations, in Level I terrain, close to lines of communication. Operations must account for civilians, their infrastructure, and their needs — and the enemy will use them.
- **Training is the prerequisite:** mountain operations require specialized skills (mountaineering, cold-weather survival, avalanche awareness) that cannot be improvised. Units must train for mountain operations before they deploy to them — two to three weeks of collective training, concurrent with acclimatization, is the manual's planning figure.

## 4. Maneuver and Movement — Time, Not Distance

### The time-distance formula

The tool available to commanders during the planning process is the **time-distance formula, which assumes acclimatized forces**. Planners determine time requirements using: altitude of operations, time the unit has had to acclimate, weight each individual carries, fitness level, size of the unit, and altitude gain or loss.

- **Foot marches in the mountains are measured in time and elevation rather than just distance.**
- For a map reconnaissance: **map distance plus one-third** is a good estimate of actual ground distance covered.
- **One hour is planned for each 3 kilometers** (about 2 miles) of distance.
- Add **one additional hour for each 305 meters (1,000 feet) of ascent** or each **610 meters (2,000 feet) of descent**.
- **Place the shortest man in the unit in the front of the formation** — his shortened gait sets a uniform march rate the whole unit can maintain.

### Dismounted movement rates

The same table as ATP 3-21.50: on foot with no snow cover 2–3 kph cross-country, 3–4 kph on trails; on foot in more than a foot of snow 0.5–1 kph unbroken, 2–3 kph broken; snowshoe 1.5–3 kph unbroken, 3–4 kph broken; skiing 1.5–5 kph unbroken, 5–6 kph broken; skijoring 8–24 kph broken (15 kph maximum recommended for safety). Dismounted movement is slow and requires skilled mountaineering teams to secure the advance. If dismounted troops deploy into column (wedge) formation outside the file formation, the unit moves more slowly — with no one breaking the trail, all personnel must move through undisturbed snow.

### Route planning and obstacles

- Assess roads, terrain, and enemy threat for mine and IED locations — likely at chokepoints, compartmented terrain, and restricted roads. Use host-nation route clearance units when available. Mitigate reseeding by providing overwatch (pickets) to the main supply route, alternate supply routes, and critical ground lines of communication such as bridges and tunnels.
- Movement in Level II terrain dictates securing the high ground in Level III terrain. Constant security to avoid unplanned enemy contact is a key to successful dismounted movement.

## 5. Engineering — Building in the Mountains

Engineering operations in mountains and cold regions face the same constraints as everything else: compartmented terrain, bad weather, limited access, and frozen ground.

- **Mobility:** route clearance, road and trail improvement, bridging (including rope bridges and improvised crossings), and avalanche control. Engineers must be prepared to build and maintain landing zones for aviation.
- **Countermobility:** obstacles in mountains are mostly natural — cliffs, rivers, avalanche paths — but engineers emplace mines, demolitions, and wire to canalize the enemy onto the routes the defender has chosen.
- **Survivability:** digging in frozen ground is slow and difficult; fighting positions, shelters, and command posts must be planned with the ground conditions in mind. Explosives may be needed to break frozen ground.
- **Cold effects on equipment:** fuels do not completely freeze but will be the same temperature as the air — handle with gloves to prevent frostbite from contact. Batteries lose capacity in the cold; keep them warm. Lubricants thicken; use cold-weather lubricants.

## 6. Sustainment — Calories, Water, and Warmth

Sustainment is the limiting factor on mountain operations. The manual's numbers are planning factors, not suggestions.

### Food

- **High altitude increases caloric requirements while reducing appetite.** Soldiers need more calories per day at altitude but want to eat less — leaders must enforce eating.
- A standard MRE contains approximately **1,250 calories** — insufficient at high altitude.
- The **meal, cold weather ration** contains menu bags of **1,540 calories each**.
- Plan on adding **one MRE per day** to increase calories and menu variety at altitude. A diet high in carbohydrates is often the best way to maintain the calories necessary to function.
- Keep food warm where possible, such as by carrying it inside clothing — frozen food cannot be eaten and frozen water cannot be drunk.

### Water

- Plan on **four quarts of water per day per soldier when stationary** and up to **eight quarts per day when moving**.
- In high mountains, increase these planning factors by about **two quarts per individual**.
- Water sources in mountains are often frozen, contaminated, or far below the position — plan water resupply as carefully as ammunition resupply. Melting snow for water costs fuel and time; plan for both.

### Cold-weather clothing and equipment

- The Army cold-weather uniform and equipment posture (Appendix C) is a layered system — leaders enforce layering discipline and the buddy system.
- Heaters and tents (Appendix D) are Army-compatible systems for warming and shelter; plan fuel for heaters as a sustainment item, not an afterthought.
- Military mountaineering equipment (Appendix E) — ropes, carabiners, snowshoes, skis, crampons, ice axes — is specialized, limited, and must be accounted for and maintained. A unit without its mountaineering equipment is a unit that cannot move in Level III terrain.

## 7. Aviation — Helicopters at Altitude

Military aviation is essential in mountains and severely limited by them, both at once.

- **Lift is reduced at altitude.** Thin air reduces helicopter lift capability and payload. Altitudes restrict helicopter lift and decrease aircraft payloads — plan for fewer soldiers and less cargo per lift than at sea level.
- **Weather** grounds aircraft for hours or days. Landing zones must be planned, prepared, and secured — and the weather window to use them may be short.
- **Landing zones** in mountains are scarce, small, and exposed. Engineers prepare them; security forces protect them; and the LZ must be on ground the unit can hold.
- **Attack and reconnaissance helicopters** provide fires and surveillance, but are limited by the same altitude and weather constraints. Their effectiveness depends on suitable, secure landing zones and favorable weather.

## 8. Fires — Shooting Over Ridges

Indirect fires in mountain operations are complicated by masking, observation, and friendly-fire risk.

- **Masking:** ridges block trajectories. Guns and mortars must be sited to cover the approaches, and many targets will be masked from any given firing position — plan multiple firing positions.
- **Observation:** targets are hard to see and hard to adjust on. Forward observers must be positioned on high ground with observation of the avenues of approach.
- **Reverse slopes:** the enemy on a reverse slope is shielded from direct fire and often from indirect fire as well. Fires must be planned to cover the crest and the approaches, not just the visible ground.
- **Aviation fires** supplement artillery but are weather-dependent. Plan fires early, register targets on the approaches, and never assume a fire mission will be available when needed.

## 9. Communications — The Mountain Eats Radios

The mountainous environment poses unique challenges to communications: terrain masking, weather, altitude, and cold all degrade radio performance.

- **Terrain masking** creates dead zones — a ridge between two stations is a wall. Plan retransmission sites on high ground.
- **Weather** (snow, rain, fog, electrical storms) degrades propagation and can knock out links entirely.
- **Cold** kills batteries. Keep batteries warm, carry spares, and plan for reduced battery life.
- **Redundancy is the plan:** radio, runner, visual signal, and wire — never depend on a single means. A plan that depends on one radio link working in mountains is a plan that will fail.

## 10. Training and Acclimatization — The Non-Negotiable

Training for mountain and cold-region operations, individually and collectively, is the subject of Chapter 10 — and the manual treats it as the foundation of everything else.

- **Mountaineer levels:** Level I Basic Mountaineer, Level II Advanced Mountaineer, Level III Master Mountaineer. Level III master mountaineers are critical elements of a mountain unit — they plan and supervise operations, advise the commander, and perform high-angle rescue. Training is progressive and takes **two to three weeks**, accomplished concurrent with acclimatization.
- **Training schools and courses** include the Army Mountain Warfare School, the Special Operations Mountain Warfare Training Center, the Navy Sea-Air-Land Team Cold-Weather Maritime Course, the Army National Guard High Altitude Aviation Training Site, and coalition mountain warfare training.
- **Acclimatization (Appendix A):** required before extensive operations. Even the most physically fit soldier experiences physiological and psychological degradation at high elevation. **80 percent of the respiratory component of acclimatization occurs in 7 to 10 days, and 80 to 90 percent of overall acclimatization is generally accomplished within two weeks to one month.** Maximum acclimatization may take months to years. Acclimatization cannot be accelerated — some troops acclimate faster, but all personnel lose their acclimatization in a matter of days at low altitude. Use a graded ascent that limits daily altitude gain to allow partial acclimatization. **The altitude at which troops sleep is critical — work high, sleep low.** Stopping the ascent to rest and acclimatize at the same altitude helps relieve symptoms; descent is not always necessary for mild AMS, but it is mandatory for HAPE and HACE.

## Output Contract

When asked about mountain or cold-weather operations at the operational level, answer with the manual's logic: (1) name the terrain level (I, II, or III) and what it permits and forbids; (2) give the planning numbers (time-distance formula, movement rates, calories, water, acclimatization timeline); (3) state the sustainment and communications constraints that bound the operation; (4) cite the chapter or appendix. Never recommend an operation above the unit's acclimatization and mountaineer level without flagging the gap. If the user asks about a specific warfighting function (fires, aviation, sustainment), lead with how the mountain environment degrades it before giving the procedure.

## Operating Rules

1. **The mountain compartmentalizes everything.** Plan for isolated units, degraded communications, and canalized movement.
2. **Time and elevation, not distance.** Map distance plus one-third; one hour per 3 km; one hour per 305 m of ascent or 610 m of descent.
3. **Level III owns Level II, and Level II owns Level I.** Secure the high ground before moving through the middle ground.
4. **Sustainment is the operation.** Calories, water, warmth — a unit that cannot be fed, watered, and warmed cannot fight.
5. **Acclimatization cannot be accelerated.** Two weeks to one month for 80–90 percent; work high, sleep low; descent is the treatment.
6. **Avalanches kill more troops than any other terrain hazard.** Respect snow, forecast it, and route around it.
