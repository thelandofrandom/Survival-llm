# Warfighting Functions in Mountains — ATP 3-90.97

Source: ATP 3-90.97, Chapters 2, 5, 7, 8, and 9. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Intelligence (Chapter 2)

- Terrain and weather dominate IPOE. The enemy's options are as constrained as yours — the same passes, ridgelines, and water sources.
- Collection is hard: sensors are masked by terrain, weather grounds aerial platforms, communications are degraded.
- Human intelligence, patrols, and observation posts become the primary sources.
- The enemy's resupply, movement, and communications run through the same few routes — find the routes and you find his logistics and his vulnerabilities.
- Deception: a feint on one pass can fix an enemy while the main effort uses another — but only if the enemy believes both passes are usable.

## Engineering (Chapter 5)

- **Mobility:** route clearance, road and trail improvement, bridging (including rope bridges and improvised crossings), avalanche control, landing-zone preparation.
- **Countermobility:** natural obstacles (cliffs, rivers, avalanche paths) do most of the work; engineers add mines, demolitions, and wire to canalize the enemy onto the routes the defender has chosen.
- **Survivability:** digging in frozen ground is slow and difficult — explosives may be needed to break frozen ground. Fighting positions, shelters, and command posts must be planned with ground conditions in mind.
- **Cold effects on equipment:** fuels at air temperature (gloves required to handle), batteries lose capacity, lubricants thicken.

## Aviation (Chapter 7)

- **Lift is reduced at altitude.** Thin air reduces helicopter lift capability and payload — plan for fewer soldiers and less cargo per lift than at sea level.
- **Weather grounds aircraft** for hours or days. Landing zones must be planned, prepared, and secured — and the weather window to use them may be short.
- **Landing zones** in mountains are scarce, small, and exposed. Engineers prepare them; security forces protect them.
- **Attack and reconnaissance helicopters** provide fires and surveillance but are limited by the same altitude and weather constraints. Effectiveness depends on suitable, secure LZs and favorable weather.

## Fires (Chapter 8)

- **Masking:** ridges block trajectories. Guns and mortars must be sited to cover the approaches; many targets will be masked from any given firing position — plan multiple firing positions.
- **Observation:** targets are hard to see and hard to adjust on. Forward observers must be positioned on high ground with observation of the avenues of approach.
- **Reverse slopes:** the enemy on a reverse slope is shielded from direct fire and often from indirect fire. Plan fires to cover the crest and the approaches, not just the visible ground.
- **Aviation fires** supplement artillery but are weather-dependent. Plan fires early, register targets on the approaches, and never assume a fire mission will be available when needed.

## Communications (Chapter 9)

- **Terrain masking** creates dead zones — a ridge between two stations is a wall. Plan retransmission sites on high ground.
- **Weather** (snow, rain, fog, electrical storms) degrades propagation and can knock out links entirely.
- **Cold** kills batteries. Keep batteries warm, carry spares, plan for reduced battery life.
- **Redundancy is the plan:** radio, runner, visual signal, wire — never depend on a single means. A plan that depends on one radio link working in mountains is a plan that will fail.

## Environment and Weather Hazards (Chapter 1, Appendix B)

- Mountain slopes generally vary between **15 and 45 degrees**.
- Sparse vegetation above **3,505 meters (11,500 feet)** in temperate mountains; below-freezing winters.
- **Avalanches have taken the lives of more troops engaged in mountain warfare than all other terrain hazards combined.**
- Wet cold leads to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot.
- Wind chill chart (Figure 1-5): leaders must know the combination of ambient temperature and wind their troops are experiencing.
- Mountain weather data (Appendix B) covers temperature, wind, precipitation, and visibility by elevation and season — plan with the data, not with the valley forecast.
