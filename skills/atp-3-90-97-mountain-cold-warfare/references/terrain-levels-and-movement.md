# Terrain Levels and Movement — ATP 3-90.97

Source: ATP 3-90.97, Chapters 1 and 4. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Operational Terrain Levels

| Level | Where | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Level I | Valley floors, main lines of communication | Armored vehicles can operate (decentralized, complemented by dismounted forces). Civilian population centers are here. Dominated by Level II above it. |
| Level II | Between valleys and mountain shoulders; narrow roads and trails, secondary LOCs | Enemy positions on Level III dominate it. Units occupy Level II because it dominates Level I. |
| Level III | Summit regions, dominant terrain | Mobility hardest to achieve and maintain. Only acclimatized troops with advanced mountaineering training operate effectively. Offers flanking and rear attack opportunities. |

**Rule:** movement in Level II terrain dictates that elements secure the high ground in Level III terrain. You cannot move through the middle ground without owning the heights above it.

## The Time-Distance Formula

The planning tool for mountain movement. It **assumes acclimatized forces**. Planners account for: altitude of operations, time the unit has had to acclimate, weight each individual carries, fitness level, size of the unit, and altitude gain or loss.

- **Map distance plus one-third** = good estimate of actual ground distance.
- **One hour per 3 kilometers** (about 2 miles) of distance.
- Add **one hour per 305 meters (1,000 ft) of ascent**.
- Add **one hour per 610 meters (2,000 ft) of descent**.
- **Foot marches in the mountains are measured in time and elevation rather than just distance.**
- Place the **shortest man in the front** of the formation — his shortened gait sets a uniform march rate.

## Dismounted Movement Rates

| Movement mode | Unbroken trail | Broken trail |
|---|---|---|
| On foot, no snow cover | 2–3 kph cross-country | 3–4 kph trail walking |
| On foot, <1 ft of snow | 1.5–3 kph | 2–3 kph |
| On foot, >1 ft of snow | 0.5–1 kph | 2–3 kph |
| Snowshoe | 1.5–3 kph | 3–4 kph |
| Skiing | 1.5–5 kph | 5–6 kph |
| Skijoring | n/a | 8–24 kph (15 kph max recommended) |

- Dismounted movement is slow and requires skilled mountaineering teams to secure the advance.
- Deploying into column (wedge) formation outside the file formation slows the unit — with no one breaking the trail, everyone moves through undisturbed snow.
- Mountain slopes generally vary between **15 and 45 degrees**; cliffs may be near vertical or overhanging.
- Talus (boulder fields) is often an easy ascent route; scree (small loose rock) is difficult to climb but good for descent.

## Route Planning Checklist

1. Identify terrain level for every leg of the route — and the Level III ground that dominates it.
2. Apply the time-distance formula with the actual altitude, load, fitness, and acclimatization state — not the flat-ground rate.
3. Assess roads, terrain, and enemy threat for mine/IED locations: chokepoints, compartmented terrain, restricted roads.
4. Use host-nation route clearance units when available; provide overwatch (pickets) to the main supply route, alternate supply routes, and critical ground LOCs (bridges, tunnels) to prevent reseeding.
5. Identify avalanche paths and route around them — avalanches have killed more troops in mountain warfare than all other terrain hazards combined.
6. Identify water sources and resupply points; plan water as carefully as ammunition.
7. Plan communications retransmission sites on high ground; assume dead zones.
8. Build in weather branches: what happens when the pass closes, the LZ is unusable, or the storm arrives early.
