# Sustainment and Acclimatization — ATP 3-90.97

Source: ATP 3-90.97, Chapters 6 and 10 and Appendix A. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Calories

- High altitude **increases caloric requirements while reducing appetite**. Leaders must enforce eating.
- Standard MRE: approximately **1,250 calories** — insufficient at high altitude.
- Meal, cold weather ration: menu bags of **1,540 calories each**.
- Plan on adding **one MRE per day** to increase calories and menu variety at altitude.
- A diet high in carbohydrates is often the best way to maintain the calories necessary to function.
- Keep food warm where possible (carry inside clothing) — frozen food cannot be eaten and frozen water cannot be drunk.

## Water

| Condition | Planning factor |
|---|---|
| Stationary | 4 quarts per day per soldier |
| Moving | Up to 8 quarts per day per soldier |
| High mountains | Add about 2 quarts per individual to the above |

- Water sources in mountains are often frozen, contaminated, or far below the position.
- Melting snow for water costs fuel and time — plan for both.
- Plan water resupply as carefully as ammunition resupply.

## Cold-Weather Clothing and Equipment (Appendix C)

- Layered cold-weather uniform and equipment posture; leaders enforce layering discipline and the buddy system.
- Wet cold leads to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot — wet cold is dangerous, not just uncomfortable.
- Wind chill is a complicating factor: leaders must know the combination of ambient temperature and wind (see Figure 1-5, the wind chill chart).
- Fuels do not completely freeze but will be the same temperature as the air — handle with gloves to prevent frostbite from contact.
- Batteries lose capacity in the cold; keep them warm and carry spares. Lubricants thicken; use cold-weather lubricants.

## Heaters and Tents (Appendix D)

- Army-compatible heaters and tents for warming and shelter.
- Plan fuel for heaters as a sustainment item, not an afterthought.
- Ventilate for cooking and heating; watch for carbon monoxide.

## Mountaineering Equipment (Appendix E)

- Ropes, carabiners, snowshoes, skis, crampons, ice axes — specialized, limited, and must be accounted for and maintained.
- A unit without its mountaineering equipment is a unit that cannot move in Level III terrain.

## Acclimatization Timeline (Appendix A)

- Acclimatization is required before extensive military operations. Even the most physically fit soldier experiences physiological and psychological degradation at high elevation.
- **80% of the respiratory component** of acclimatization occurs in **7 to 10 days**.
- **80–90% of overall acclimatization** is generally accomplished within **two weeks to one month**.
- Maximum acclimatization may take **months to years**.
- Acclimatization **cannot be accelerated**. Some troops acclimate faster, but all personnel lose their acclimatization in a matter of days at low altitude.
- Use a **graded ascent** that limits daily altitude gain to allow partial acclimatization.
- **The altitude at which troops sleep is critical — work high, sleep low.**
- Stopping the ascent to rest and acclimatize at the same altitude helps relieve mild AMS symptoms; descent is not always necessary for mild AMS, but it is mandatory for HAPE and HACE.
- Training for mountain operations takes **two to three weeks** and is accomplished concurrent with acclimatization.

## Mountaineer Levels (Chapter 10)

| Level | Title | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Level I | Basic Mountaineer | Individual movement on steep, nontechnical and low-technical terrain |
| Level II | Advanced Mountaineer | Leads climbs, builds anchors, runs rope systems, trains Level I |
| Level III | Master Mountaineer | Plans and supervises unit mountain operations, advises the commander, performs high-angle rescue |

Level III master mountaineers are critical elements of a mountain unit. Training is progressive — never assign tasks above a soldier's level.
