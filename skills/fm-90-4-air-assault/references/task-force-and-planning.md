# Task Force and Planning — FM 90-4

## The Air Assault Task Force

An air assault operation requires the total integration of ground and aviation assets into a single mission-tailored force under one headquarters. The **air assault task force commander (AATFC)** is a ground or air maneuver commander. No unit below division can unilaterally conduct an effective air assault operation. The **battalion is the lowest level with sufficient personnel to plan, coordinate, and control** an air assault operation; company-size operations are planned mostly at battalion level and above.

Task organizing is mission-tailored: the AATF is built for the specific mission, and its composition changes with it. The ground maneuver force and the aviation assets that lift, support, and protect it are organized together from the start, with liaison between ground and aviation elements continuous from planning through execution.

## Definition

**Air assault** (para 1-2): assault forces using the firepower, mobility, and total integration of helicopter assets, maneuvering under the control of a ground or air maneuver commander to engage and destroy enemy forces or to seize and hold key terrain. It is **not merely the movement of soldiers, weapons, and materiel by aviation**. It is a deliberate, precisely planned, vigorously executed operation.

**Air movement operations are distinct:** they are airlift only; aviation is not task-organized to engage the enemy; the operation ends when the lift is complete.

## Capabilities, Limitations, Vulnerabilities

**Capabilities (para 1-8):** attack from any direction; delay a much larger force without decisive engagement; overfly or bypass barriers and obstacles; conduct deep attacks and raids beyond the FLOT or line of contact; rapidly concentrate, disperse, and redeploy forces; provide responsive reserves; react to opportunities; exploitation and pursuit; place forces at decisive points; conduct surveillance and screening; react to threats in the rear area; secure and defend key terrain; bypass and surprise the enemy; operate in adverse weather and at night; maintain a fast tempo over extended distances; economy of force; reinforce committed units.

**Limitations (para 1-9):** adverse weather, extreme heat and cold, blowing snow and sand; reliance on air lines of communication; hostile aircraft, ADA, and EW; reduced ground mobility once inserted; availability of suitable LZs and PZs; limited NBC protection and decontamination capability; reduced vehicle-mounted AT capability (except in air assault units); battlefield obscuration; high JP4 fuel and ammunition consumption.

**Vulnerabilities (para 1-10):** attack by aircraft and ADA during movement; NBC attack; ground, air, and artillery attack during loading and unloading, and while forces are not dug in; air strikes — only limited ADA can be deployed with the AATF; EW and jamming — the operation relies heavily on radio communications; artillery destroying helicopters and forces at PZs and LZs; small-arms fire against helicopters.

## Preparation for Combat (Chapter 2)

**Estimate process.** Planning uses **METT-T** (mission, enemy, terrain, troops, time available). The **division is the lowest echelon that can allocate assets, assign missions, and gather the data** needed for an air assault operation. Planning is centralized and precise, and normally takes **more time than other operations**; compressed planning relies on SOPs and training.

**Terrain analysis** uses **OCOKA** (observation and fields of fire, cover and concealment, obstacles, key terrain, avenues of approach). **IPB** (intelligence preparation of the battlefield) drives PZ/LZ selection, route selection, and the threat picture.

**Weather factors:** fog, low clouds, and heavy rain limit visibility; illumination and moon angle matter for night-vision goggles; ice, sleet, and freezing rain degrade aerodynamic performance; high temperatures and density altitude degrade lift; darkness is normally an advantage; high winds and blowing dust, sand, or snow on PZs and LZs are hazards.

**Allowable weather limits** (combat and tactical training at a military airfield): visibility **½ mile**, ceilings clear of clouds; maximum winds **OH 30 kt, UH 40 kt, CH 60 kt**; gusting more than 15 kt over the lull may preclude UH operations. TACAIR (A-10) ceiling **1,000 ft**, visibility **2 mi**. **Aircrew endurance planning: 8 hours per day and 4 hours per night** as the safe limit.

**Threat.** The enemy threat is assessed by capability: air defense (ADA and aircraft), artillery, EW, NBC, and ground forces able to react to the LZ. The threat determines the degree of surprise required, the fires needed, and the security force.

**Command and control / communications.** The operation is radio-intensive. Frequencies, code words, and reporting procedures are fixed in the air mission briefing and the OPORD. All watches are synchronized by a **time hack**.

## Operations Planning (Chapter 3)

Planning produces five interrelated plans, briefed together in the **air mission briefing** (OPORD format):

1. **Ground tactical plan** — what the ground force does once on the ground: mission, scheme of maneuver, objectives, security, and the concept for fighting from the LZ to the objective.
2. **Landing plan** — how the force gets onto the ground: LZ selection, landing sequence, formations, and the fires that support the landing. It must support the ground tactical plan. The AATF is most vulnerable during landing; it lands with tactical integrity, prepared to fight in any direction. **An alternate LZ is designated for each primary LZ.**
3. **Air movement plan** — how the force moves from PZ to LZ: flight routes, corridors, control points, formations, timing, and en route fires.
4. **Loading plan** — how men and equipment are assigned to aircraft: the airloading table, cross-loading, bump plan, and slingload/external loads.
5. **Staging plan** — how the force assembles and moves to the PZ: assembly areas, staging areas, movement schedules, and PZ control.

**Air mission briefing** covers the mission, abort criteria, downed-aircraft procedures, vertical helicopter instrument flight recovery, weather decisions in 1-hour increments plus a weather abort time, the passenger briefing, code words (PZ secure / hot / clean, abort, alternate PZ/LZ, fire preparation, extraction, alternate route), and the time hack.

**Security and control** (Ch 3, section II) covers OPSEC, deception, and the control measures that keep the operation's timing and routes concealed until execution.
