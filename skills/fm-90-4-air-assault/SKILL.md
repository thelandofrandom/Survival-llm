# FM 90-4 Air Assault Operations

**Source:** FM 90-4 *Air Assault Operations*, Department of the Army, March 1987 (supersedes FM 90-4, 8 October 1980). 223 pages. **Status: historic doctrine** — superseded by later air assault doctrine (FM 3-99 and subsequent publications); use for understanding the 1987 doctrinal framework, not as current tactics, techniques, and procedures.

## What This Manual Covers

Air assault operations in the AirLand Battle; the air assault task force (AATF) — its organization, capabilities, limitations, and vulnerabilities; preparation for combat; the five plans plus the air mission briefing; combat operations; combat support and combat service support; and the conduct of an air assault operation, planning forms, PZ and LZ operations, training, airspace management, and moving vehicle-mounted TOW by helicopter.

## 1. Definition and the Air Assault Task Force

**Air assault** (para 1-2): assault forces using the firepower, mobility, and total integration of helicopter assets, maneuvering under the control of a ground or air maneuver commander to engage and destroy enemy forces or to seize and hold key terrain. It is **not merely the movement of soldiers, weapons, and materiel by aviation**. It is a deliberate, precisely planned, vigorously executed operation.

**Air movement operations are distinct:** they are airlift only; aviation is not task-organized to engage the enemy; the operation ends when the lift is complete. Air movement is covered in FM 55-450-1 and FM 55-30.

**The air assault task force (AATF):** an air assault operation requires the total integration of ground and aviation assets into a single mission-tailored force under one headquarters, commanded by the **air assault task force commander (AATFC)** — a ground or air maneuver commander. No unit below division can unilaterally conduct an effective air assault operation. The **battalion is the lowest level with sufficient personnel to plan, coordinate, and control** an air assault operation; company-size operations are planned mostly at battalion level and above. Task organizing is mission-tailored: the AATF is built for the specific mission, and its composition changes with it.

### Capabilities (para 1-8)

- Attack from any direction.
- Delay a much larger force without decisive engagement.
- Overfly or bypass barriers and obstacles.
- Conduct deep attacks and raids beyond the forward line of own troops (FLOT) or line of contact (LC).
- Rapidly concentrate, disperse, and redeploy forces.
- Provide responsive reserves; react to opportunities; exploitation and pursuit.
- Place forces at decisive points; conduct surveillance and screening.
- React to threats in the rear area; secure and defend key terrain.
- Bypass and surprise the enemy; operate in adverse weather and at night.
- Maintain a fast tempo over extended distances; economy of force; reinforce committed units.

### Limitations (para 1-9)

- Adverse weather, extreme heat and cold, blowing snow and sand.
- Reliance on air lines of communication.
- Hostile aircraft, air defense artillery (ADA), and electronic warfare (EW).
- Reduced ground mobility once inserted.
- Availability of suitable landing zones (LZs) and pickup zones (PZs).
- Limited nuclear, biological, and chemical (NBC) protection and decontamination capability.
- Reduced vehicle-mounted antitank (AT) capability (except in air assault units).
- Battlefield obscuration.
- High consumption of JP4 fuel and ammunition.

### Vulnerabilities (para 1-10)

- Attack by aircraft and ADA during movement.
- NBC attack.
- Ground, air, and artillery attack during loading and unloading, and while forces are not dug in.
- Air strikes — only limited ADA can be deployed with the AATF.
- EW and jamming — the operation relies heavily on radio communications.
- Artillery destroying helicopters and forces at PZs and LZs.
- Small-arms fire against helicopters.

## 2. Preparation for Combat

**Estimate process.** Planning uses **METT-T** (mission, enemy, terrain, troops, time available). The **division is the lowest echelon that can allocate assets, assign missions, and gather the data** needed for an air assault operation. Planning is centralized and precise, and normally takes **more time than other operations**; compressed planning relies on SOPs and training.

**Terrain analysis** uses **OCOKA** (observation and fields of fire, cover and concealment, obstacles, key terrain, avenues of approach). **IPB** (intelligence preparation of the battlefield) drives PZ/LZ selection, route selection, and the threat picture.

**Weather factors:** fog, low clouds, and heavy rain limit visibility; illumination and moon angle matter for night-vision goggles (NVG); ice, sleet, and freezing rain degrade aerodynamic performance; high temperatures and density altitude degrade lift; darkness is normally an advantage; high winds and blowing dust, sand, or snow on PZs and LZs are hazards.

**Allowable weather limits** (combat and tactical training at a military airfield): visibility **½ mile**, ceilings clear of clouds; maximum winds **OH 30 kt, UH 40 kt, CH 60 kt**; gusting more than 15 kt over the lull may preclude UH operations. TACAIR (A-10) ceiling **1,000 ft**, visibility **2 mi**. **Aircrew endurance planning: 8 hours per day and 4 hours per night** as the safe limit.

**Threat.** The enemy threat is assessed by capability: air defense (ADA and aircraft), artillery, EW, NBC, and ground forces able to react to the LZ. The threat determines the degree of surprise required, the fires needed, and the security force.

**Task organizing.** The AATF is organized around the ground maneuver force and the aviation assets that lift, support, and protect it. The AATFC commands; the aviation commander controls the aviation effort. Liaison between ground and aviation elements is continuous from planning through execution.

**Command and control / communications.** The operation is radio-intensive. Frequencies, code words, and reporting procedures are fixed in the air mission briefing and the OPORD. All watches are synchronized by a **time hack**.

## 3. Operations Planning — the Five Plans and the Briefing

Planning produces five interrelated plans, briefed together in the **air mission briefing** (OPORD format). See `references/task-force-and-planning.md` for the full planning sequence.

1. **Ground tactical plan** — what the ground force does once on the ground: mission, scheme of maneuver, objectives, security, and the concept for fighting from the LZ to the objective.
2. **Landing plan** — how the force gets onto the ground: LZ selection, landing sequence, formations, and the fires that support the landing. It must support the ground tactical plan. The AATF is most vulnerable during landing; it lands with tactical integrity, prepared to fight in any direction. **An alternate LZ is designated for each primary LZ.**
3. **Air movement plan** — how the force moves from PZ to LZ: flight routes, corridors, control points, formations, timing, and en route fires.
4. **Loading plan** — how men and equipment are assigned to aircraft: the airloading table, cross-loading, bump plan, and slingload/external loads.
5. **Staging plan** — how the force assembles and moves to the PZ: assembly areas, staging areas, movement schedules, and PZ control.

**Air mission briefing** covers the mission, abort criteria, downed-aircraft procedures, vertical helicopter instrument flight recovery, weather decisions in 1-hour increments plus a weather abort time, the passenger briefing, code words (PZ secure / hot / clean, abort, alternate PZ/LZ, fire preparation, extraction, alternate route), and the time hack.

## 4. The Landing Plan and LZs

**LZ criteria:** location relative to the objective; capacity (enough room for the aircraft in the lift); alternates; enemy disposition and capabilities; cover and concealment; obstacles — **LZs free of obstacles**, and when attacking, land on the **enemy side** of obstacles; identification from the air (chemical lights, preferably **infrared for NVG**); approach and departure routes that avoid flank exposure; weather.

**Single LZ advantages:** concentration of force, ease of control, concentrated fires and security, fewer routes, centralized resupply, less planning. **Multiple LZ advantages:** avoids presenting a lucrative target, rapid dispersal, the enemy must fight in multiple directions, less congestion, harder for the enemy to gauge force size.

**LZ designation:** LZs are designated by **letter or code word, not the same number as the objective**.

**Landing formations:** land ready to employ fire and movement; select a **SAFE landing area close to concealment**, not a picture-perfect one. The first lift lands ready to fight.

**Fires to support the landing:** the initial assault is often conducted **without preparatory fires**, to preserve surprise. When preparations are used, they are planned to be **intense, short, and high-volume, ending just before the first lift lands**. Deception false preparations may be used. A long preparation loses surprise, and ordnance can crater the LZ, blow down trees, and obscure it.

## 5. The Air Movement Plan

Based on the ground tactical and landing plans. **Flight routes** are chosen for tactical and technical factors; routes are designated by **color or number**. The **flight corridor** extends **200–300 m either side of the route and 500 ft above and below** it; a **flight axis** is a route with width. **Expedient flight routes** are defined by checkpoints. **Air control points (ACPs)** are points where the route changes direction.

**En route formations** and **terrain flight modes**, including **nap-of-the-earth (NOE)** flight, are specified. Supporting fires are planned along the route. **Timing basis: the first aircraft of the first lift touching down (H-hour).** In the manual's example, **SP time = H-10** (8-minute route plus 2 minutes from release point to LZ) and **lift-off = H-12** (including 2 minutes from PZ to SP). Times are recorded in the **air movement table**.

**Short radio reports** keep the net clear — for example, "One, One King" means the first serial of lift 1.

## 6. The Loading Plan, PZ Operations, and Execution

**PZ selection:** multiple PZs avoid concentration of aircraft and troops; a PZ should be sized to accommodate all supporting aircraft at once if possible; proximity to the troops; masked from enemy observation; usable as-is or with limited preparation.

**PZ control officer (PZCO):** organizes and controls the PZ, forms the control group, uses **two radio frequencies** (movement/loading and aviation), plans fires and security, clears obstacles, and marks the PZ. PZs are named by **color**; markers show where aircraft land, matching the landing formation.

**Movement to the PZ** is scheduled so that only the troops to be loaded and the helicopter arrive together; a **staging area** is used if the assembly area is distant.

**Airloading table:** assigns each man and each major item of equipment and supplies to a specific aircraft (**chalk**). It is the accountability tool and the loading manifest; a copy is left with a designated person.

**Load planning:** **tactical cross-loading** — do not put all leaders or all crew-served weapons in one chalk; maintain unit integrity where possible; provide communication between chalks if possible without aircraft radios. **Bump plan:** who and what is left behind if aircraft are unavailable, and in what priority. **Slingload and external loads** follow FM 55-450-1; internal loads, allowable gross weight, and the manifest are part of the plan.

**Execution — hasty attack vs deliberate attack:**

- **Hasty attack:** movement to contact, a modified deliberate plan, exploitation of success, or attack from the defense. Tentative PZs, LZs, and routes are pre-identified; orders are brief; the operation relies on SOPs and training. Suppressive fires are used, with **priority to suppression of enemy ADA**. Smoke may screen aircraft but **must not obscure the LZ**.
- **Deliberate attack:** a detailed plan, additional assets, changed task organization, detailed intelligence. Objectives are normally in the **enemy rear or flank/rear**; the force bypasses main defenses to destroy artillery, command posts, logistics, and communications, or to secure key terrain.

**Defense and other tactical missions** (Ch 4, sections II–III) include air assault in the defense and other missions such as raids, reconnaissance in force, and security operations.

## 7. Combat Support

Fire support delivery (Ch 5):

- **Mortars** — organic to the force, close-in support.
- **Artillery** — positioned well forward or **airlifted with the AATF**; artillery air assault operations are a distinct planning problem (Ch 5, section IV).
- **Air defense artillery** — direct fire when directed.
- **Close air support (CAS)** — USAF via the tactical air control party (TAC CP).
- **Naval gunfire** — Navy cruisers and destroyers, with **ANGLICO** spotters.
- **Attack helicopters** — integrated when field artillery is unavailable.

**Fire support coordination:** the **FSO / FSCOORD** and the **FSE / FIST** coordinate fires. Fire support planning and coordination, artillery air assault, naval gunfire, USAF tactical air support, air defense, and engineer support are covered in Ch 5, sections I–VIII. See `references/combat-support-service-support.md`.

## 8. Combat Service Support

The AATF is normally configured for **1–3 days of accompanying supplies** for self-sustainment. Resupply is by air on a routine basis via the **LOGPAC** — a day's logistics for a company, organized in unit trains by the company supply sergeant and the support platoon leader, per SOP.

**Palletized and external slingloads** reduce ground-time vulnerability at the LZ. The **FARP** (forward arming and refueling point) — primary and alternate — supports aviation. Ammunition and fuel requirements are computed from the mission and the number of lifts.

**Backup aircraft** are planned for. **Health service support and aeromedical evacuation** are planned with the medical platoon and aviation. The aviation unit advises on load size and weight limits, PZ/LZ suitability, and sling recovery; the receiving unit selects, prepares, and controls the LZ, has trained ground crews for hookup and derigging, and coordinates backloads.

## Operating Rules

- The AATF is a single mission-tailored force under one commander; it is never merely a lift.
- The battalion is the lowest level that plans, coordinates, and controls; the division is the lowest echelon that allocates assets and gathers the data.
- Plan centrally and precisely; when time is compressed, fall back on SOPs and training — do not improvise the plan.
- Always designate an alternate LZ for each primary LZ, and an alternate PZ where possible.
- Cross-load leaders and crew-served weapons across chalks; always have a bump plan.
- The AATF is most vulnerable during landing and during loading/unloading — plan fires, security, and timing around those windows.
- Preserve surprise: preparatory fires, when used, are intense, short, and end just before the first lift lands.
- **Historic status:** this is 1987 doctrine. Aircraft types, organizations, and some procedures have changed; verify against current doctrine before applying operationally.
