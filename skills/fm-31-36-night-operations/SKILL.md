# FM 31-36 (TEST) Night Operations

## ⚠️ Mislabeled in the Vault — Read This First

## Purpose

FM 31-36 (TEST), *Night Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 12 April 1968, 280 pages, TAGO 9626A) — interim doctrinal guidance for commanders and staffs in the employment and support of infantry, airborne infantry, airmobile infantry, mechanized infantry, and armor units in night operations, at battalion level and below. Its principal emphasis is Southeast Asia. Its scope covers general war (including nuclear munitions and chemical, biological, and radiological agents), limited war, and cold war including stability operations. It is a TEST edition: the manual states that new and improved night operating aids are continually being developed, and commanders and small-unit leaders must adapt the tactics described as the capabilities and availability of the equipment change.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 31-36 (TEST) is a 1968 interim manual. It is not current doctrine: night operations today are governed by current Army and Marine Corps doctrine (including the night-operations material in current FM/ATP publications and the current jungle manual MCTP 12-10C, 2020, for night work in jungle terrain), and its equipment — first-generation image intensification, infrared illumination, sound ranging sets, and early anti-intrusion devices — is obsolete. What remains durable is its treatment of the night environment (illumination levels, dark adaptation, overcast), its four reasons for fighting at night, its planning prerequisites for a night attack, and its night patrolling procedures. What is dated is every device-specific appendix and every equipment number. Where current doctrine disagrees with FM 31-36 (TEST), follow the current publication.

**Sibling note.** FM 31-36 (TEST) is the odd one out in this batch: the other four manuals are environment manuals (three jungle, one desert). Its connection to them is practical, not topical — night is the other half of jungle fighting. FM 31-30 and FM 31-35 both note that night fighting techniques simulate jungle conditions (reduced ranges of assault, restricted movement, control difficulties), and FM 90-5's tactical chapter assumes units will fight at night. Read this skill for the night; read the jungle skills for the terrain.

## 1. Why Fight at Night

**1.1. The four reasons.** Night operations — all military activity (attack, defense, retrograde, movement, combat support, and combat service support) conducted at night — are undertaken for four fundamental reasons:

- **a.** To attain the initiative by continuing to fight or breaking contact at the most advantageous time and place.
- **b.** To exploit the advantages of darkness, to achieve surprise, and to avoid heavy losses which might be incurred in daylight operations over the same ground.
- **c.** To compensate for advantages held by an enemy with superior forces or air superiority.
- **d.** To counter or neutralize the enemy's night operating capability.

In areas such as Southeast Asia, where the enemy avoids daylight contact and prefers to fight at night, night operating aids improve the effectiveness of night operations — and the enemy's preference for night fighting is itself a reason to master it.

**1.2. The fundamental consideration.** Night operations, regardless of scale, "basically depend on the ability of the individual soldier to perform his assigned task at night." Combat-associated activities such as rest, movement, and preparation for coming combat predominate during darkness, and night combat was treated as a special operation because of individual limitations. The degree to which those limitations can be offset depends almost entirely on how much added ability is gained by the proficient employment of night operating aids — battlefield illumination, night vision devices, sound ranging sets, anti-intrusion devices, radars, and aerial surveillance equipment. Fundamentally, the soldier must move, observe terrain, observe enemy and friendly units, aim and fire weapons while observing their effects, communicate, and perform the other tasks of the unit's mission. → `references/night-environment-and-vision.md`

## 2. The Night Environment

**2.1. Illumination levels decide courses of action.** Illumination levels at night directly affect the selection of courses of action. Night operations should be conducted when light levels provide maximum advantage to friendly units with night vision devices. Passive night vision devices (those that do not use artificial illumination) increase night vision under most illumination levels; under very low levels, infrared, pink, or visible illumination can be used with them. Clear nights let units equipped with night vision devices operate with near-day efficiency against forces not equally equipped. When the capability of two forces is equal, relatively good night visibility favors the defender, by permitting him to observe the approach of the attacker. Vegetation and terrain that give concealment from ground and air observation normally favor an attacker, whether or not the defender has night viewing devices.

**2.2. The numbers of the night.** On the 40° North Latitude line, a **full moon corresponds to about one-hundredth of a foot-candle** of illumination, and a **quarter moon to about one-thousandth of a foot-candle**. The light of the moonless night sky is a comparatively low but significant source of illumination. Full moon levels prevail only **about 30 percent of the time** at middle latitudes in July, and light from the moonless night sky with reduced levels can be expected **approximately 25 percent of the time**. The moon, reflecting light from the sun, approximates a point source; by greatly multiplying existing illumination, passive night vision devices are very effective under moonlight. Overcast reduces light levels by a **factor of 2 for thin overcast to a factor of 10 for dark, heavy storm clouds**.

**2.3. Dark adaptation.** Dark vision — the ability to see unusually well under very reduced levels of illumination — can be achieved by allowing little or no light to enter the eye for approximately **30 minutes**, during which chemical changes in the eye make it unusually sensitive to small amounts of light. But dark vision is rapidly destroyed by exposure to normal and bright light, so as a practical matter the individual soldier on the battlefield cannot acquire and preserve maximum dark-adapted vision — except infrequently, as on patrols where burning and exploding incidents are not encountered, where a higher than normal degree of visual acuity may be achieved. When not equipped with night vision devices, **off-center vision and scanning observation** should be practiced, since off-center vision is more sensitive at low illumination levels. The individual soldier should avoid looking at all light sources if possible.

**2.4. Length of the night.** Anywhere on earth throughout the course of the year, exactly one-half of all hours are hours of darkness — but the distribution, and the length of twilight, vary with latitude and season, and the manual provides figures for planning by latitude and month. → `references/night-environment-and-vision.md`

## 3. Illumination, Night Vision, and Combat Surveillance

**3.1. The toolkit.** Chapter 3 covers planning for night vision; battlefield illumination; night vision devices; sound ranging; anti-intrusion devices; radars; aerial surveillance; and signal intelligence. The principal requirement is to produce a night vision advantage for the unit conducting the operation — near-daylight speed and accuracy — and to coordinate all night vision aids (passive devices, radar, incendiary and other illumination, and aerial surveillance) under the commander's concept.

**3.2. The planning rule.** The use of night operating aids during darkness improves some tactical capabilities more than others; devices should be employed to best facilitate the individual task or unit mission. As the limitations imposed by the environment are reduced or removed by night vision devices or battlefield illumination, night attacks more closely resemble daylight attacks — and as a minimum, the control measures of daytime tactical operations are also employed at night. Separate appendices describe each major item of night vision equipment and devices in use at the time; all of them are obsolete as equipment, and the appendices should be read as history, not as a catalog. → `references/illumination-devices-and-surveillance.md`

## 4. Night Offensive Operations

**4.1. What night combat looks like.** Combat at night or under reduced visibility is characterized by a reduced ability to place aimed fire on the enemy; a corresponding increase in the importance of close combat, volume of fire, and the fires of fixed weapons laid on definite targets or target areas; difficulty in movement; and difficulty in maintaining control, direction, and contact. The considerations for organizing forces at night are no different than in daytime — but the environment punishes every lapse in control.

**4.2. Purposes of the night attack.** Night attacks are used to: hasten accomplishment of the mission; continue an action begun but not finished in daylight; exploit a success made during daylight; retain the initiative; keep continual pressure on enemy forces, avoid loss of contact, and thereby prevent reorganization of his forces, reestablishment of his communications, and preparation of defensive positions; avoid heavy losses by crossing at night terrain that predominately favors the defense; achieve surprise; fix enemy reserves or cause their premature commitment; and compensate for an enemy with air or ground superiority.

**4.3. The nine prerequisites.** As a general rule, the following are necessary for successful night operations: (1) detailed planning and preparation; (2) detailed information of terrain, enemy, weather conditions, and illumination levels; (3) secrecy and surprise; (4) detailed orders and simplified schemes of maneuver; (5) easily identifiable objectives; (6) requirements for close control; (7) special recognition and reporting procedures, particularly where secrecy is to be preserved; (8) logistical support; (9) deception. Surprise and secrecy are attained more easily at night than in daylight, but achieving them requires stricter adherence to secrecy — concealed routes of approach and deception measures are used as much as possible.

**4.4. Maneuver and fire support.** Complicated maneuvers are avoided. Company and platoon objectives are delineated so they are recognizable at night with a medium-range night observation device. The attack should begin early at night if the objective is deep or the mission requires immediate continuation — breach initial defenses with dismounted forces, then exploit with highly mechanized forces during darkness. Where pressure is relentless, rested infantry units are committed around or through units in contact at night to preclude the enemy from gaining the initiative. Fire support plans are similar to daylight attacks; illumination is provided per the night visibility plan; when partial or no illumination is provided, special restrictions govern the movement of weapons and the initiation and shifting of supporting fires, controlled with night viewing devices. When the attack is made to achieve surprise, the commander announces the conditions under which supporting weapons will fire; if the attack is discovered after the assault is launched, supporting fires go on the objective, beyond it, or on its flanks. → `references/night-offensive-operations.md`

## 5. Night Defense, Retrograde, and Stability Operations

**5.1. Night defense.** Part Four covers the night defense, including tank-mechanized infantry task forces in the defense. The same illumination logic applies in reverse: the defender's advantage depends on whether his night vision capability matches or exceeds the attacker's, and on his ability to observe the approach. Anti-intrusion devices, radars, and illumination plans are the defender's tools for turning darkness from the attacker's ally into a killing ground.

**5.2. Night retrograde.** Part Five covers night retrograde operations — breaking contact and withdrawing under cover of darkness, using the same control measures, recognition procedures, and deception that govern the night attack, with the added problem of moving a force rearward through its own positions without fratricide or loss of cohesion.

**5.3. Night stability operations.** Chapter 18 covers night stability operations — the cold-war mission set of the 1968 manual, in which night patrolling, security, and limited offensive action support the political and population mission. The techniques are the same as the rest of the manual; the mission is different, and the manual is explicit that night operations apply across general war, limited war, and cold war alike.

## 6. Night Patrols

**6.1. The most frequently conducted night operation.** The patrol is one of the most frequently conducted night operations in combat. The fundamentals of patrolling in FM 21-75 are unchanged during periods of limited visibility; operational techniques must be modified to take advantage of the concealment of darkness. Proper use of night vision and surveillance devices provides a near-daylight capability for the night patrol, and patrols may be conducted under near-daylight conditions regardless of ambient light levels.

**6.2. Planning and preparation.** At battalion, the S2 plans reconnaissance patrols and the S3 is responsible for combat patrols, coordinating a daily patrol plan sent to higher, lower, adjacent, and supporting elements. Patrol personnel are notified in sufficient time to permit daylight reconnaissance and other preparations; patrols are provided with the latest maps, aerial photos, and sketches; the patrol leader makes a ground reconnaissance and, if possible, an air reconnaissance of routes and objective areas. Night patrols are equipped with night vision devices and augmented with scout dogs, special weapons, and ground or air transport as the commander evaluates METT factors.

**6.3. The individual soldier's preparation.** Camouflage of equipment and exposed body surfaces; a serviceability check of equipment; a test firing of weapons; tying or padding equipment to prevent rattling; sufficient time before departure for eyes to adapt to darkness; no personal effects that would aid the enemy in identifying friendly units. Commanders supervise rehearsals of actions at the objective, using a mockup or terrain board; communications are checked during the rehearsal. On arrival in the forward unit area, a guide leads the patrol to an assembly area or initial rallying point, the patrol leader completes final coordination with the forward unit commander (including changes in recognition signals and friendly and enemy dispositions), and the patrol is guided to the passage point, with a security party observing forward with starlight scopes.

**6.4. Why patrols matter at night.** Patrols conducted during reduced visibility are less likely to be detected. Where the primary mission must be performed in daylight, movement to the objective during darkness is desirable, using the passive protection of night conditions. Combining the favorable influences of daylight and night operations gives a significant advantage over the enemy. → `references/night-patrols-defense-and-retrograde.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **It is a night manual, not a jungle manual.** Correct the label whenever it appears.
2. **The individual soldier is the foundation.** Night operations depend on his ability to perform his task at night — devices help, they do not replace him.
3. **Plan in detail, maneuver simply.** Detailed planning and preparation, simplified schemes of maneuver, easily identifiable objectives.
4. **Secrecy is stricter at night.** Surprise is easier to gain and easier to lose; concealed routes and deception are used as much as possible.
5. **Control is the hard problem.** Close control, special recognition and reporting procedures, and rehearsals on a mockup or terrain board.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** FM 31-36 (TEST) is 1968 interim guidance with obsolete equipment; it is history and principles, not the authority.
