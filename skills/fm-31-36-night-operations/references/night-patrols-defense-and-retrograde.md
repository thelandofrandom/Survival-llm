# Night Patrols, Defense, and Retrograde — FM 31-36 (TEST)

## Night Patrols

The patrol is one of the most frequently conducted night operations in combat. The fundamentals of patrolling in FM 21-75 are unchanged during periods of limited visibility; operational techniques must be modified to take advantage of the concealment of darkness. Proper use of night vision and surveillance devices provides a near-daylight capability for the night patrol.

**Why patrol at night:** patrols in reduced visibility are less likely to be detected. Where the primary mission must be performed in daylight, movement to the objective during darkness is desirable, using the passive protection of night conditions. Combining the favorable influences of daylight and night operations gives a significant advantage over the enemy.

**Planning and preparation:**

- At battalion, the S2 plans reconnaissance patrols and the S3 is responsible for combat patrols; they compile a daily patrol plan sent to higher, lower, adjacent, and supporting elements.
- Patrol personnel are notified in sufficient time to permit daylight reconnaissance and other preparations.
- Patrols are provided with the latest maps, aerial photos, and sketches showing routes and objective areas.
- The patrol leader makes a ground reconnaissance and, if possible, an air reconnaissance of routes and objective areas.
- Night patrols are equipped with night vision devices and augmented with scout dogs, special weapons, and ground or air transport as the commander evaluates METT factors.

**The individual soldier's preparation:**

- Camouflage of equipment and exposed body surfaces.
- Serviceability check of equipment.
- Test firing of weapons.
- Tying or padding equipment to prevent rattling.
- Sufficient time before departure for eyes to adapt to darkness.
- No personal effects that would aid the enemy in identifying friendly units.

**Rehearsal and passage:** commanders supervise rehearsals of actions at the objective, using a mockup or terrain board; communications are checked during the rehearsal. On arrival in the forward unit area, a guide leads the patrol to an assembly area or initial rallying point; the patrol leader completes final coordination with the forward unit commander (including changes in recognition signals and friendly and enemy dispositions); the patrol is guided to the passage point, with a security party observing forward with starlight scopes.

## Night Defense

Part Four covers the night defense, including tank-mechanized infantry task forces in the defense. The defender's advantage depends on whether his night vision capability matches or exceeds the attacker's, and on his ability to observe the approach. Anti-intrusion devices, radars, and illumination plans are the defender's tools for turning darkness from the attacker's ally into a killing ground. When the capability of two forces is equal, relatively good night visibility favors the defender.

## Night Retrograde

Part Five covers night retrograde operations — breaking contact and withdrawing under cover of darkness, using the same control measures, recognition procedures, and deception that govern the night attack, with the added problem of moving a force rearward through its own positions without fratricide or loss of cohesion.

## Night Stability Operations

Chapter 18 covers night stability operations — the cold-war mission set of the 1968 manual, in which night patrolling, security, and limited offensive action support the political and population mission. The techniques are the same as the rest of the manual; the mission is different, and the manual is explicit that night operations apply across general war, limited war, and cold war alike.
