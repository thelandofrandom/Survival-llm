# Foot Care — ATP 3-21.18

Source: ATP 3-21.18, Appendix E (Foot Care). Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Why Feet Decide the March

Foot care is an important ingredient in soldier morale. Soldiers whose feet are dirty and unkempt do not function as well as soldiers who have bathed and put on clean, dry socks. Foot blisters and abrasions are the most common soldier load-related injury. Many major conditions requiring hospitalization and disability have resulted from neglected or maltreated minor conditions.

## Foot Protection

- Bring several pairs of proper-fitting socks.
- Have an extra pair of boots available when possible, to change when wet or worn down.
- Keep socks clean and dry; change wet or damp socks as soon as possible.
- Apply foot powder on feet and inside boots when changing socks.
- Wash feet daily if possible.
- Avoid tight socks and boots; lace boots up as loosely as possible while still supporting the ankle.
- Wear overshoes when appropriate for the mission.

## Foot Conditioning

Conditioning is accomplished by progressively increasing distance marched from day to day. Marching is the best way to strengthen feet and legs. Condition the arch, ankle, and calf with simple exercises: rising high on the toes, and placing feet on towels and using toes to roll the towel back under the arch.

## Before the March

- Trim toenails regularly — short, square, straight across.
- Keep feet clean and dry; use foot powder.
- Wear clean, dry, proper-fitting socks (preferably cushion-soled), seams and knots out, free from holes or signs of wear.
- Nylon or polypropylene sock liners reduce friction and add protection.
- Carefully fit new boots. When breaking in new boots, alternate with another pair and **tape known hot spots before wearing the new pair**.

## During the March

- At halts, remove **only one boot at a time** to massage feet, apply foot powder, change socks, and medicate blisters.
- Cover open blisters, cuts, or abrasions with absorbent adhesive bandages.
- Loosen bootlaces where they cross the arch to relieve swelling feet.
- Unit medics or combat lifesavers help with foot problems; squad leaders ensure soldiers carry extra socks.
- Schedule a halt in which all soldiers alternate changing socks and powdering feet.

## After the March

- Repeat foot care: wash and dry feet, wash and dry socks, dry boots.
- Medicate blisters, abrasions, corns, and calluses.
- Inspect painful feet for sprains and improper fitting of socks and boots.
- Red, swollen, tender skin along the sides of the feet can develop from prolonged marching — treat before it becomes a blister.
- Give feet a daily cleaning when possible; cool water reduces the sensation of heat and irritation; dry feet well afterward.

## Foot Injuries and Treatment

Conditions that can occur from foot marches:

- **Blisters and abrasions** — the most common; result from friction between socks and skin. Causes: improperly conditioned feet, heavy load, ill-fitting footwear and socks, poorly maintained footwear, heat, moisture. Blisters cause extreme discomfort, can prevent soldiers from completing planned actions, and can progress to infection.
- **Foot perspiration** — moisture accumulates in socks, decreasing insulation and increasing cold-injury risk.
- **Athlete's foot** — fungal infection from warm, moist feet.
- **Foot frostbite** — freezing injury to toes and feet.
- **Trench foot / immersion foot** — prolonged wet, cold feet, even above freezing.
- **Stress fractures** — overuse injury from load and distance.

### Blister Treatment (Table E-1 / Figure E-3)

- Treat a friction hot spot **before** it becomes a blister: stop, dry the foot, apply tape or moleskin over the hot spot, change socks.
- A formed blister: clean the area, protect with an absorbent adhesive bandage or moleskin donut; do not pop a blister unnecessarily in the field — if it must be drained, do it cleanly and cover it.
- Red, swollen, tender skin is a blister forming — treat it as one.
- Any blister that shows signs of infection (spreading redness, pus, fever) is a medical evacuation, not a field problem.
