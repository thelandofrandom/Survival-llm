# Hydration and Injuries — ATP 3-21.18

Source: ATP 3-21.18, Chapter 1 and Appendices D and F. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Hydration Protocol (Appendix D)

- Drink **16 ounces of water 2 hours before** the march.
- Drink **8 to 16 ounces of water 15 minutes before** the march.
- Drink **6 to 12 ounces of water every 15 to 20 minutes during** the march.
- Monitor urine during the march — it should be **pale yellow**.
- Drink small quantities rather than gulping; drink when not thirsty; drink slowly to prevent cramps or nausea.
- Refill canteens with water only, or replenish electrolyte and carbohydrate fluids, at every opportunity.
- **Never pour electrolyte and carbohydrate fluids into water canteens or hydration systems.**
- **Water does not replace lost electrolytes.** Consume beverages containing electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) when available — especially in environments that increase sweat rate, such as extreme heat and humidity.
- Do not restrict salt in the diet. **Sodium intake of 1 gram per hour** is recommended during prolonged marches with heavy sweat loss.
- In extreme dry heat, water and sodium needs can be as high as **10 liters and 20 grams** respectively.
- Sweating rates can exceed **1 quart per hour** during strenuous activity.
- Force-hydrating with water alone flushes electrolytes and nutrients out of the body — it can cause hyponatremia (low blood sodium), which is as dangerous as dehydration.

## March Rates and Fatigue (Chapter 1)

- Planning rate: **106 steps per minute, 30-inch stride, 10-minute rest every hour** = 4.8 kph march rate, 4 kph rate of march.
- Normal foot march day (ideal conditions): **8 hours, 32 km, 4 kph**.
- Forced march maximums: **56 km in 24 hours** (14 hours marching), **96 km in 48 hours** (12 hours per day), **128 km in 72 hours** (12 hours day 1–2, 8 hours day 3).
- Adjustment halt during the **first half hour**; short rest halts every **1 to 1.5 hours**; longer halts later in the march as fatigue requires.
- A moderate pace reduces rest-halt needs and overheating risk. Minimizing halts lets units cover distance in minimal time.
- After a climb, rest to revive tired muscles; use rest stops for steep slopes, snowfields, and higher elevations.

## Heat Injuries (Appendix F)

- **Heat cramps:** muscle cramps from salt loss; rest, shade, electrolytes.
- **Heat exhaustion:** mild or more severe — heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache; rest in shade, cool, hydrate with electrolytes.
- **Heat stroke:** a medical emergency — altered mental status, hot dry skin or profuse sweating, high core temperature. Cool immediately (immersion, ice, shade, fan) and evacuate. Heat stroke kills.
- Prevention: acclimatization, hydration protocol above, work-rest cycles, avoiding the hottest time of day for forced marches.

## Cold Injuries (Appendix F)

- **Foot frostbite:** freezing injury to toes and feet; prevention is dry socks, proper-fitting boots, foot powder, and movement.
- **Trench foot / immersion foot:** prolonged wet, cold feet, even above freezing; keep feet dry, change socks, air feet at halts.
- Moisture accumulates in socks, decreasing insulating quality; feet are observed less frequently than the rest of the body — leaders must check them deliberately.

## Other March Injuries

- **Stress fractures:** overuse injury from load and distance; pain that worsens with marching and does not ease at rest is a red flag — evacuate for evaluation.
- **Blisters and abrasions:** the most common load-related injury; see `foot-care.md`.
- **Cognitive load:** leaders reduce extraneous information during the march so soldiers can focus on moving, observing, and fighting.

## Posture and Body Mechanics (Appendix C)

- Proper walking technique: pause briefly after each step forward, relaxing the muscles of the forward leg while resting body weight on the rear leg (the rest step).
- Pack fitted high and tight, weight on the hips, straps adjusted at the adjustment halt.
- Keep the head up and the stride natural — a forced long stride wastes energy and shortens endurance.
