# Foot Marches

## Purpose

ATP 3-21.18 *Foot Marches* (U.S. Army, 24 July 2025, 168 pages) is the Army's current doctrine for moving soldiers on foot — the oldest, most reliable, and most punishing form of military movement. This skill distills it into field-usable doctrine for a leader planning, conducting, or training a foot march: how far a unit can go, how fast, under what load, in what conditions, and what breaks first when the plan is wrong (usually the feet).

Core idea: **a foot march is a logistics problem with legs.** Distance, rate, time, load, water, and foot care are not separate topics — they are one equation, and the unit that gets the equation wrong arrives late, exhausted, and combat-ineffective. The manual's through-line is discipline: march discipline, water discipline, foot discipline, and load discipline. A unit that masters those four can march anywhere; a unit that masters none of them cannot march at all.

This skill covers all three chapters and six appendices: movement fundamentals, march execution, soldier load, march procedures, the movement order and movement table, posture and body mechanics, training for the march, foot care, and related injury and illness awareness.

## 1. Movement Fundamentals — The Three Kinds of March

The Army conducts three types of dismounted movement, and the leader must know which one is being ordered, because each has different rules for speed, security, and load.

- **Tactical road march:** a rapid movement used to relocate units within an assigned area to prepare for combat operations. Used when contact with the enemy is **not expected**. Organized into march columns. Provides excellent speed, control, and flexibility, but sacrifices flank security. The march organization, order, and techniques for the tactical road march are the baseline for the other two types.
- **Approach march:** the advance of a combat unit when direct contact with the enemy is **intended**. The enemy's approximate location is known. Emphasizes speed over tactical deployment and less physical security or dispersion — the unit is moving to fight, and it task-organizes before the march begins so it can transition to an on-order or be-prepared attack without stopping to reorganize. Follow-and-assume and reserve forces may conduct an approach march forward of the main body.
- **Forced march:** an accelerated march to arrive at a destination quickly, normally accomplished by **increasing marching hours per day rather than rate of march** — though sometimes the rate must increase too. Forced marches are employed only when needed, because they decrease combat effectiveness and cohesion, and soldiers experience a temporary deterioration in physical condition immediately afterward. The commander who orders a forced march is spending the unit's fighting power to buy time, and must know the price.

**The march equation:** march rate is the physical speed at a given moment; rate of march is the average speed over time including rest. A march unit on roads in daylight can reasonably expect **106 steps per minute with a 30-inch stride and a 10-minute rest every hour** — a march rate of **4.8 kph** but an overall rate of march of **4 kph**. That 4 kph figure is the planning baseline for a normal foot march day: **8 hours, 32 kilometers, at 4 kph**, under ideal conditions.

**Forced march maximums (ideal conditions):**

| Duration | Distance | Marching hours |
|---|---|---|
| 24 hours | 56 km | 14 hours of marching in 24 hours |
| 48 hours | 96 km | 12 hours of marching each 24-hour period |
| 72 hours | 128 km | 12 hours in the first two 24-hour periods, 8 hours in the last |

Rest periods should be scheduled to **avoid marching at the hottest time of day** so the unit arrives combat-ready. Take full advantage of periods when soldiers are rested to increase the rate of march if necessary.

## 2. General Considerations — What Governs Every March

Before the route is chosen, the leader accounts for the factors that will govern the march whether planned for or not.

- **Visibility:** limited-visibility marches are slower, harder to navigate, harder to control, and harder to secure. Leaders move closer to the front, reduce speed and intervals, use luminescent tape on personnel and equipment, conduct headcounts often, and increase the number of guides and checkpoints. Navigation uses the same techniques as daylight, but with more care. Security measures include forward and flank elements, strict noise and light discipline, radio silence when possible, camouflage, terrain masking from enemy surveillance and night-vision devices, frequent listening halts (stop, look, listen, and smell), and masking movement sounds with rain, wind, or flowing water.
- **Climate, weather, and terrain:** the more adverse the conditions, the slower the pace. Moving too quickly, even under ideal conditions, produces early fatigue, requires frequent rest halts, and results in time loss. Terrain, weather, and light conditions affect movement rates — adjust the rate to the conditions, not to the schedule.
- **March discipline:** following prescribed routes at prescribed march rates, maintaining intervals, enforcing noise and light discipline, and keeping the column closed up. A column that strings out is a column that cannot be controlled or defended.
- **Water discipline:** consume water or electrolyte and carbohydrate fluids before, during, and after foot marches. Drink small quantities rather than gulping; drink when not thirsty; drink slowly to prevent cramps or nausea. Refill canteens at every opportunity. Never pour electrolyte fluids into water canteens or hydration systems. Force-hydrating with water alone flushes electrolytes out of the body — water does not replace lost electrolytes.
- **Acclimatization:** soldiers must be conditioned and acclimatized to the area of operations. Acclimatization is normally done through unit conditioning programs. A unit fresh into a hot, cold, or high environment cannot march at the planning rate.
- **Individual load:** every pound matters (see §4). Load is the single most controllable variable in march performance.
- **Morale:** a unit that believes in the march will march farther than a unit that does not. Leaders who explain the purpose, keep the pace honest, and share the hardship keep morale; leaders who lie about the distance destroy it.

## 3. Planning the March — Distance, Rate, and Time

Planning turns the march equation into a time schedule that regulates departures and arrivals of march elements.

- **Key components of planning:** the march mission, the route, the march rate and rate of march, the length of the column (in kilometers, with its equivalent travel time in minutes), road gaps and time gaps between elements, and the time schedule itself.
- **Environmental conditions:** adjust planning rates for terrain, weather, light, and altitude. See ATP 3-21.50 for dismounted movement rates in mountain environments and ATP 3-90.98 for jungle environments.
- **March distance, rate, and time:** relationships between time and distance are the basis of march planning. Planners determine how far columns travel, how long it takes, and how much space columns occupy on the route. Safety factors of distance (road gap) or time (time gap) separate march columns and their elements.
- **Minimizing fatigue:** a moderate pace reduces the need for rest halts and the chance of overheating. Minimizing halts lets units cover a given distance in minimal time. Take an **adjustment halt during the first half hour** of movement so soldiers can loosen or tighten bootlaces, adjust packs, and add or remove layers. Take **short rest halts every 1 to 1.5 hours** — soldiers lean against a tree, rock, or hillside to relieve pack weight, breathe deeply, hydrate, and snack; keep halts short to avoid muscles stiffening; rest on level ground and avoid steep inclines. Take **longer rest halts later in the march** if fatigue or mission requires — soldiers may need to add clothing to avoid becoming chilled, because it is much easier to keep a warm body warm than to warm up a cold body. After a climb, rest to revive tired muscles; use rest stops for steep slopes, snowfields, and higher elevations.
- **Walking technique:** soldiers employ proper walking techniques and pause briefly after each step forward, relaxing the muscles of the forward leg while resting their entire body weight on the rear leg — the rest step, which conserves energy on long marches and steep ground. See Appendix C for posture and body mechanics.

## 4. March Execution — Conduct, Security, and Duties

### Conduct of the march

- **Limited visibility:** see §2. Disadvantages include difficulty in navigation, slower rate of march, difficulty recognizing checkpoints, difficulty controlling subordinate units, difficulty maintaining intervals, reduced aviation support, difficulty reacting to enemy contact, difficulty with MEDEVAC/CASEVAC, and ineffective unmanned aircraft system coverage.
- **Soldier and leader engagement:** leaders stay engaged with their soldiers throughout the march — checking loads, feet, water, and morale at every halt. A leader who rides in a vehicle while the unit marches has abdicated.
- **March security:** advance, flank, and rear guards; listening halts; noise and light discipline; and a scheme of fires and engineer operations that cover the column. The column is most vulnerable at halts and at chokepoints — plan security for both.
- **Communications:** maintain contact between march elements; use messengers, visual signals, and radio in that order of reliability under march conditions.
- **En route sustainment:** water, food, and ammunition resupply at planned points; foot care and load adjustments occur throughout the march, not just at the end.
- **Actions on contact:** the march column must be able to react to enemy contact without losing control — rehearsed battle drills, pre-planned responses, and leaders who know the plan.
- **Cognitive load:** the combination of relevant, intrinsic, and extraneous information during a task. Leaders reduce extraneous load on soldiers during the march — clear orders, simple drills, no unnecessary information — so soldiers can focus on moving, observing, and fighting.

### Duties and responsibilities

- **Battalion commander and staff:** plan the march, issue the movement order, allocate resources, and control the movement.
- **Company commander and subordinate leaders:** execute the march, enforce discipline, conduct precombat checks and inspections, and keep the unit moving on schedule.
- **Company habitual attachments:** medics, engineers, and other enablers march with the unit and perform their functions en route.
- **Soldiers:** march, maintain interval and discipline, care for their feet and equipment, and be ready to fight at the end of the march.

## 5. Soldier Load — The Weight That Decides Everything

Load is the single most controllable variable in march performance, and the leader's responsibility is to decide what is carried and what is left behind.

- **Fighting load:** the essential items a soldier needs to maneuver on, close with, and destroy enemy forces in direct-fire contact — everything worn or carried. Should average **60 to 80 pounds (ideally about 60 pounds)**.
- **Approach march load:** the fighting load plus additional essential equipment (assault pack or rucksack and items not needed in the fighting load but now required). Should average **80 to 100 pounds (ideally about 80 pounds)** and **should not exceed 100 pounds**. Approach march loads are dropped in a secure location before contact, so soldiers fight in their fighting load.
- **Emergency approach march load:** **100 to 125 pounds**. Used only when necessary — operations requiring it should be limited in distance, and commanders should seek to limit march distances and provide soldiers with recovery time afterward.
- **Load tailoring:** the commander gives guidance, the leader estimates the situation, and decisions to reduce or offset risk are made deliberately — by transferring load to vehicles, to pack animals, to host-nation support, or by leaving items behind. Calculating load requirements, the unit packing list, resupply operations, soldier load transfer (rotating heavy crew-served weapons and equipment between soldiers during the march), and contract, host-nation, and local-national support are all part of the plan.
- **Echeloning loads:** the same principle as in ATP 3-21.50 — fighting load on the soldier, approach march load dropped before contact, sustainment load with vehicles or at the base. → `references/load-tables.md`

## 6. Training for the March — Conditioning Is the Program

Training for the march is progressive: increase distance from day to day, increase load gradually, and train in the conditions the unit will march in.

- **Foot conditioning:** marching is the best way to strengthen feet and legs. The arch, ankle, and calf can be conditioned with simple exercises such as rising high on the toes and rolling a towel under the arch with the toes.
- **Progressive overload:** start short and light, build distance and weight over weeks. A unit that jumps from garrison to a 32-kilometer march will break feet and lose soldiers.
- **Train the whole march:** rehearsals, precombat checks and inspections, equipment maintenance, and foot care are all trained before the march, not learned during it.
- **Recovery:** taper activities gradually and safely after the march to bring the body back to its pre-exercise state. Recovery is part of training, not the absence of it.

## 7. Foot Care — The March Lives or Dies on Feet

Foot care is the most important ingredient in soldier morale and march success. Soldiers whose feet are dirty and unkempt do not function as well as soldiers who have bathed and put on clean, dry socks. Foot blisters and abrasions are the **most common soldier load-related injury** — caused by friction between socks and skin, and driven by improperly conditioned feet, heavy load, ill-fitting footwear and socks, poorly maintained footwear, heat, and moisture. Neglected minor foot conditions have resulted in hospitalization and disability.

### Before the march

- Trim toenails regularly — short, square, straight across.
- Keep feet clean and dry; use foot powder.
- Wear clean, dry, proper-fitting socks (preferably cushion-soled), seams and knots out, free from holes or signs of wear. Nylon or polypropylene sock liners reduce friction and add protection.
- Carefully fit new boots. When breaking in new boots, alternate with another pair and **tape known hot spots before wearing the new pair**.
- Bring several pairs of proper-fitting socks; have an extra pair of boots available when possible.

### During the march

- At halts, when conditions permit, remove **only one boot at a time** to massage feet, apply foot powder, change socks, and medicate blisters. Cover open blisters, cuts, or abrasions with absorbent adhesive bandages.
- Change wet or damp socks as soon as possible; apply foot powder on feet and inside boots when changing socks.
- Avoid tight socks and boots; lace boots as loosely as possible while still supporting the ankle. Loosen bootlaces where they cross the arch to relieve swelling.
- Unit medics or combat lifesavers carry extra foot powder, gauze, adhesive tape, and other foot-care items; squad leaders ensure soldiers carry extra socks.

### After the march

- Repeat foot care: wash and dry feet, wash and dry socks, dry boots.
- Medicate blisters, abrasions, corns, and calluses. Inspect painful feet for sprains and improper fitting of socks and boots.
- Red, swollen, tender skin along the sides of the feet can develop from prolonged marching — treat it before it becomes a blister.
- Give feet a daily cleaning when possible; cool water reduces the sensation of heat and irritation; dry feet well afterward. → `references/foot-care.md`

## 8. Hydration, Electrolytes, and Injury Awareness

### Hydration protocol (Appendix D)

- Drink **16 ounces of water 2 hours before** the march.
- Drink **8 to 16 ounces of water 15 minutes before** the march.
- Drink **6 to 12 ounces of water every 15 to 20 minutes during** the march.
- Monitor urine during the march — it should be pale yellow.
- **Water does not replace lost electrolytes.** Consume beverages containing electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) when available — especially in extreme heat and humidity.
- Do not restrict salt in the diet. **Sodium intake of 1 gram per hour** is recommended during prolonged marches with heavy sweat loss.
- In extreme dry heat, water and sodium needs can be as high as **10 liters and 20 grams** respectively.
- Sweating rates can exceed **1 quart per hour** during strenuous activity.

### Heat and cold injuries

- Heat illnesses: heat cramps, heat exhaustion (mild or more severe), and heat stroke — the most dangerous, and a medical emergency.
- Cold injuries: foot frostbite, trench foot, immersion foot. Moisture accumulates in socks, decreasing their insulating quality; feet are susceptible to cold injury and are observed less frequently than the rest of the body.
- Stress fractures, blisters, and abrasions are the common load-related injuries. Treat the small problem in the field or it becomes the big problem in the hospital. → `references/hydration-and-injuries.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a foot march, answer with the manual's math: (1) identify the march type (tactical road march, approach march, or forced march) and what it implies for speed, security, and load; (2) give the distance-rate-time calculation with the planning rate (4 kph, 106 steps/min, 30-inch stride, 10-minute rest per hour) and the forced-march maximums; (3) state the load (fighting 60–80 lb, approach 80–100 lb, emergency 100–125 lb) and the hydration protocol; (4) state the foot-care check that applies at the current phase (before/during/after); (5) cite the chapter or appendix. Never recommend a distance, rate, or load above the manual's maximums without flagging the cost in combat effectiveness.

## Operating Rules

1. **Four kph is the plan; everything else is an adjustment.** Rate, distance, and time come from the march equation, not from optimism.
2. **Forced marches spend fighting power to buy time.** The commander who orders one must know the price and pay it deliberately.
3. **Feet are the mission.** Foot care before, during, and after the march — a blister ignored in the morning is a casualty by afternoon.
4. **Water is not electrolytes.** Drink small quantities often, replace salt, and never force-hydrate with water alone.
5. **Load is a leader's decision.** Fighting load 60–80 lb, approach march load 80–100 lb, emergency 100–125 lb — and the decision is made before the march.
6. **Slow and steady beats fast and halting.** A moderate pace, an adjustment halt in the first half hour, short halts every 1–1.5 hours — the accordion effect is the enemy of the march.
