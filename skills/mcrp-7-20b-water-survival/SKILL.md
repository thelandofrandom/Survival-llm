# MCRP 7-20B Marine Corps Water Survival


## Purpose


MCRP 7-20B, *Marine Corps Water Survival* (U.S. Marine Corps, 2016; vault lists 2021, 122 pages) — the Marine Corps water-survival manual: techniques, procedures, and training standards for surviving in open water, performing water rescues, treating water casualties, negotiating water obstacles, and fording waterways. It is the foundation publication for the Marine Corps Water Survival Program (MCWSPM) and the refresher Marines use before water operations.

The manual opens with a case that frames everything in it. On 23 November 1995, LCpl Zachary Mayo was thrown from the USS America into the Arabian Sea, 30 feet below, at night, with land at least 100 miles away. He stayed afloat for 34 hours — making a flotation device from his coveralls and relaxing — until fishermen on a Pakistani fishing boat found him. The manual's point: survival in water is training applied under control, not luck.


## 1. Survival in Open Water — Mindset and Abandoning Ship

**Mindset before you board.** Any craft you board becomes a hazard the moment it makes you abandon it. Before embarking, know the life preservers and lifeboats/rafts, unit survival equipment, individual survival kits, how many other personnel are aboard and where, and the egress procedures.

**Abandoning ship.** Report to your designated assembly area and put on a life preserver. From **15 feet (4.6 meters) or less** with a proper flotation device or waterproofed pack, you may keep an essential small survival kit; above 15 feet, pack, gas mask, vest, or rifle risks injury. **Remove your helmet and gas mask first** — the helmet 'cups' air on impact and the chin strap can create a 'hanging effect.' 

**Abandon ship technique.** Hands on opposite shoulders, criss-cross. Check the water below for debris or survivors. **Do NOT hold your nose** (impact can break your arm). Step off with a smooth **30-inch stride. Do NOT dive. Do NOT look down. Look straight ahead.** Bring the trailing leg forward and cross it behind the leading leg; keep your head parallel to the water's surface. **Swim away from the ship. Do NOT look back** — falling equipment and debris, people landing on swimmers, and a sinking ship's suction can pull you under.

**Surface burning oil swim.** Fuel floats, so move clear immediately — swim **upwind (into the wind)** or **against the current** so wind/current pushes fuel past you. To dive through a burning layer, wet your head, face, and uniform; submerge and swim under the fire until you surface in clear water.

## 2. Surviving With a Pack

**Your pack floats — Archimedes' Principle.** A submerged object is buoyed up by a force equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces. Do not try to hold yourself or equipment higher out of the water than they naturally float.

**Waterproofing.** Tape or pad sharp edges and corners. Supply items: waterproof bags — pistol bag #1 (8 by 18 inches), rifle bag #2 (10 by 56 inches), machine gun bag #3 (15 by 56 inches), multipurpose bag #4 (20 by 84 inches); Panama work vest; self-sealing small plastic bags (6 by 6 and 12 by 12 inches); riggers tape, olive drab (2½ inches); waterproof clothing.

**Packing.** Waterproof mission-essential equipment first (maps, communications gear), then your weapon, then personal items.

**Four survival strokes with the pack.** **On the pack:** hug it to your chest, body horizontal, face in the direction of travel; breast stroke kick is most efficient. **Reverse pack travel:** back toward the direction of travel, pack to chest, backward stepping action digging in with boot toes — least preferred (extremely slow). Also: side stroke with the pack and backstroke with the pack.

**Gear shed.** In the water: stay calm, orient and assess, push down with your arms and kick hard to re-surface; if you cannot re-surface, relax. Rehearse shedding your flak jacket with a life preserver on.

## 3. Staying Afloat — Life Preservers and Field-Expedient Flotation

**Life preservers.** Life preservers are best because they let you wear clothes for heat retention and sunburn prevention. **Inflatable** — carried aboard Marine Corps aircraft and amphibious assault vehicles; the issue item is the **life preserver personal (LPP)**. Donning: fasten belt fasteners in front with the pouch in the rear, adjust the belt, rotate the pouch to the front; open snap fasteners, unfold, place the deflated preserver over your head; inflate by pulling the CO2 lanyard or blowing into the oral valve. The most common inherently buoyant preserver is the vest-type with collar, the **kapok**: collar, chest, and leg straps, and waist drawstrings. **Shoulder bubble:** a bubble of air trapped in the shoulders of your blouse supports you at the surface.

**Field-expedient flotation.** **Waterproofed pack:** supports **one to three Marines**, heads out — hug it to your chest, chin on top; two or three Marines form a wagon wheel around it. **5-gallon plastic water jug:** even partially filled, supports one to three Marines, heads out — grasp opposite sides, chin on top; wagon-wheel for a group.

**Inflated blouse.** **Preferred method** (keeps your head out of the water): sleeves and front fully buttoned; pinch the collar by the top two front buttons and exhale air into the neckline. **Alternate method** (requires submerging your head): sleeves buttoned, top two front buttons unbuttoned; seal the collar tight against the neck, bend forward no more than 45 degrees, forcefully exhale a full lung of air into the blouse, reseal at once, tilt head back, raise elbows; cross ankles and stop moving.

**Inflated trousers.** In **warm water**, trousers are a primary expedient flotation device. **Blow method** (weak swimmers): drop 2 feet below the surface with the waistband open and blow air in, repeating until filled. **Sling method** (strong swimmer): hold the trousers above the water behind your head, sling them overhead to trap air. **Splash method:** flutter your hand in the waistband at the surface to send air bubbles into the legs. Replenish air by **scooping** bubbles into the waistband.

## 4. Cold Water, Drownproofing, and Survival Strokes

**Cold water kills fast.** Heat exchange in water is about **25 times greater** than in air; wet clothing loses insulating quality. About **50 percent of body heat is lost through the head**.

**HELP position (individual).** With a life preserver, assume the **heat escape lessening posture (HELP)**: tuck your chin down to cover your throat; draw your legs up to protect the groin; place your arms across your chest, hands in your armpits. 

**Huddle position (group).** Three or more Marines with life preservers wedge tightly together and lock arms to form a circle. If more than five Marines, make clusters of huddles; rotate everyone inside the huddle to re-warm each person's core temperature.

**Drownproofing.** Positive buoyancy floats at the surface; neutral buoyancy floats a few feet beneath; negative buoyancy sinks. **Do not hold your breath below the surface for more than 10 seconds.** **The sweep** (slight to excellent positive buoyancy): float face down, bent 45 degrees at the waist, arms and legs dangling, relaxed; spread feet slowly for a single kick; cross arms in front of chest; exhale before raising your head for a breath; bring legs together and sweep arms down and out until fully extended — this raises your face above the water; with air replenished, return your face to the water and relax. Full step order for both methods → `references/flotation-and-cold-water.md`. Drownproofing methods are the **T-method** (best with negative buoyancy) and the **sweep**; remember **SAFE — slow easy movements**.

**Survival strokes.** **Crawl stroke** (front crawl/freestyle): fast but **not a preferred survival stroke** — exhausting for beginners; **bilateral breathing is preferred**. **Breast stroke:** use to swim underwater, through oil or debris, and in rough seas; the **best stroke for long-range swimming**; glide 1–3 seconds. **Side stroke:** both arms give buoyancy with slight propulsion; most propulsion comes from the kick; lead arm extended beyond the head, palm down; face out of the water for free breathing. **Elementary backstroke:** on your back, face up, arms pressed to sides, legs extended and joined; trace hands up your sides, extend arms out to a "T", elbows locked — **do not raise arms above the head**; sweep palms toward thighs; legs bend both knees 90 degrees, heels drawn downward, circle in a whipping action ending in glide. ## 5.

## 5. Water Rescues

**Rescue from a distance first.** A panicking victim can seize and inadvertently drown a rescuer. Prefer reaching, wading, throwing; swim only if they cannot reach. Before every rescue: **STOP**, **LOOK**, **LISTEN**, **REACH** (reach with an object, do not make direct contact if possible), **THROW** (throw a rescue/flotation device if you cannot reach).

**Reaching techniques.** **Reach** — water's edge, talk constantly, keep contact with land/support; extend a stick, pack, or rifle (magazine removed, chamber empty), pull slowly to safety. **Throw** — a weighted floating item (canteen one-fourth full of water), bowline around its neck, lid refastened; rope end under the ball of your forward foot; coil **20–30 yards** in your nonthrowing hand; underhand-throw just over the victim's head so the rope trails across the outstretched arms.

**Approaches** — the victim's state decides: distressed swimmer, active and drowning, or passive and drowning. **Rear** — active victim: reassure until within **2–6 yards**, then approach from behind. **Crawl approach** — fastest; passive and/or unconscious; head above the water for free breathing and eye contact; **54 yards or less**, keep your head raised the whole way; more than 54 yards, face in the water for four or five strokes, then raise your head to regain eye contact. More than 54 yards away: crawl to close distance, then stop **6–8 feet** out.

**Level-offs** — victim face up, parallel to the surface. **Front surface** (passive victim): stop 6–8 feet out, reassess and reassure, grasp the wrist, rotate the victim face-up. **Single armpit:** stop 6–8 feet out, grab the armpit, sideways with your elbow in the center of the victim's back; pull with the armpit hand while pushing with the elbow to bring the victim horizontal face-up; then extend the towing arm fully locked out and begin a single armpit tow.

**Tows and carries.** The **lifesaving stroke** is a modified side stroke: the top arm tows or carries the victim while a scissor kick provides propulsion. **Wrist tow:** front approach with a modified breast stroke, stop 6–8 feet out, grasp the underside of the wrist; lean back, pull and kick strongly to bring the victim horizontal, twist the wrist to rotate them face-up; lifesaving stroke to safety. **Cross-chest carry:** breast approach, stop 6–8 feet out, level off face-up, keep a one-hand grip, reach over with the free hand to encircle the chest; release the first grip; lifesaving stroke, firm chest grip, your hip on the victim's back — if they struggle, tighten your grip or defend yourself. **Tired swimmer's assist** — keep the 6–8 foot margin while getting behind the victim; one arm extended under the victim's opposite armpit; maintain a 45-degree angle away from the victim; tow with the lifesaving stroke. **Single armpit tow** and **collar tow** — full steps → `references/water-rescues.md`.

**Defense against a drowning victim.** **Do NOT sacrifice your life to save the victim.** A panicking victim can grab you with great strength trying to climb out of the water. **Blocks:** one or both open hands on the victim's upper chest, lean backwards and submerge rapidly, swim underwater away, surface quickly; stop 6–8 feet out and reassess. **Front head-hold escape:** victim gripping your head and neck from the front — quick breath, tuck your chin into your shoulder; clap your hands above your head three times to submerge instantly; if they do not release, apply pressure to the brachial pressure points; thrust the victim's arms up and away, swim underwater away, surface at the ready position, stop 6–8 feet out and reassess. **Rear head-hold escape:** victim gripping your head and neck from the rear — quick breath, tuck chin down, turn your head to either side, raise shoulders; strong stroke, clap hands above your head three times and submerge instantly; if no release, brachial pressure points; thrust the victim's arms up and away, twist until free, swim underwater away and surface, stop 6–8 feet out and assess. **Wrist-grip escape:** if the victim grabs your wrist, submerge them by pushing down on their shoulder with your free hand, then give three hard jerking pulls with your trapped hand → `references/water-rescues.md`.

**First aid and rescue breathing.** Remove the unconscious victim from the water when possible and stop any bleeding. Open the airway, check breathing and pulse — rescue breathing is in §6; no pulse: start CPR (see MCRP 3-40A.9). **Warning: if the victim has no pulse and is not breathing, administer CPR immediately. If the victim has a pulse but is not breathing, give rescue breathing only. If the victim has a pulse and is breathing, do not give CPR — CPR could prove fatal.**

## 6. Treatment of Casualties and Dangerous Marine Life

**Drowning.** Drowning is suffocation by a liquid — the victim inhales water, or the throat closes by reflex. If the victim stops or grows calmer, they have likely been overcome by fatigue, hypothermia, or lack of air — at this stage the victim usually has **20 to 60 seconds** before going under. The same CPR warning as §5 applies. Rescue breathing: victim on back, head back, pinch nose, two full breaths; no inhale → reposition head, two more breaths; check pulse. **A victim who is not breathing and has no pulse may appear dead. Do NOT decide that death has occurred** — continue treatment; a corpsman or medical officer decides.

**Hypothermia.** Abnormal lowering of core temperature to **95 degrees or below**; the body loses heat faster than it produces it. Water transfers heat 25 times faster than air; depending on water temperature, a victim can succumb within minutes. Symptoms once core temperature drops below 95 degrees: violent shivering; slow or slurred speech; disorientation; loss of skin color; blue and pinched lips; shivering slowing or stopping. **Treatment:** begin as soon as possible (see MCRP 3-40A.9 for specifics). **Caution: do NOT apply heat to extremities.** Check for a pulse for **at least 45 seconds** before concluding there is none. Conscious victim: remove wet clothing, wrap in dry blankets, warm fluids. Unconscious victim: place on back, tilt head, check breathing. **Do NOT massage the victim. Do NOT give alcohol.**

**Heat-related injuries.** **Heat cramps:** muscles cramp in arms, legs, and/or stomach — shade and water. **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating; pale, moist, cool skin; headache, weakness, dizziness, loss of appetite; may also have heat cramps, nausea, vomiting, chills, rapid breathing, confusion, tingling in hands or feet — pour water over the victim and fan them. **Heat stroke:** the victim **stops sweating** — red, hot, dry skin; headache, dizziness, nausea, fast pulse, seizures, mental confusion; can become unconscious and die if not treated quickly — shade, water, elevate both legs if possible.

**Burns.** Apply a field dressing or the cleanest material available. **Do NOT remove clothing stuck to the burns. Do NOT break blisters. Do NOT apply grease, ointment, or powder.** Electrical burns: check for both entry and exit burns. Chemical burns: flush with large amounts of water, cover with a dry dressing. **White phosphorus:** flush with water, cover with a wet material or mud to exclude air. Laser burns: apply a field dressing.

**Common medical problems of sea survival.** **Seasickness** — nausea and vomiting from the bobbing motion of a flotation device; can cause dehydration and exhaustion. If flames, smoke, or irritants get in the eyes, flush with saltwater, then freshwater if available; apply ointment if available; bandage both eyes for 18 to 24 hours. **Seasickness treatment:** if the victim cannot take pills orally, give them rectally. **Saltwater sores:** flush with freshwater, allow to dry, apply antiseptic; do not open or drain. **Sunburn prevention:** erect an improvised canopy for shade, cover exposed skin with sunscreen or lip balm. **Never drink saltwater, drink urine, drink alcohol, smoke, or eat when water is not available.**

**Dangerous marine life.** Most marine life will not deliberately attack a human; the most common injuries are bites, stings, or punctures. Only about **20 percent** of shark species attack people. Protection: stay with other swimmers — a group can maintain a 360-degree watch; keep all clothing on, including footwear;  For more detail, see MCRP 8-10B.7 and MCRP 3-40A.9. **Shark attack:** sharks are attracted by smell and vibration; if bitten, stop the bleeding and treat for shock. **Sea snakes** are venomous — avoid them; if bitten, keep the victim still and evacuate. **Poisonous fish:** poisons concentrate in the liver, intestines, and eggs — do not eat them. **Jellyfish:** gently remove clinging tentacles with a towel; use diluted ammonia or alcohol if available. **Spiny fish, urchins, stingrays, and cone shells:** soak wounds in hot water to deactivate heat-sensitive toxins. **Do NOT use iodine to disinfect coral cuts** — coral polyps feed on iodine and may grow inside the flesh. ## 7. **Neap tides** show the least change between high and low — they occur at the half moon, when the Sun and Moon are aligned at a 90-degree angle with the Earth.

## 7. Negotiating Water Obstacles

**Tides.** Periodic changes in surface levels of oceans, bays, gulfs, inlets, and rivers, caused by the Moon's and Sun's gravity. **Neap tides** show the least change between high and low — they occur at the half moon, when the Sun and Moon are aligned at a 90-degree angle with the Earth and their gravitational pulls offset each other. **Spring tides** show the greatest change — they occur at the new and full moon. **Flood tide** flows in; **ebb tide** flows out.

**Surf.** Waves break on entering shallow water, creating the surf zone. When a new wave is within about **3 yards**, start swimming toward shore; continue until the wave lifts you toward the beach; once it loses momentum, relax and repeat the cycle. Escape: tuck into a ball with your head against your knees and forearms locked around your legs; relax until the turbulence subsides and you float to the surface — **this can take 30 seconds or more** — then swim toward shore.

**Currents.** **Offshore currents** occur outside the surf zone, flowing parallel to or away from shore; if tide-created, strength and direction vary. **Rip currents** occur when waves pile water against the shore faster than it can drain; water flows rapidly along the beach until deflected seaward by a bottom obstruction, then flows through the surf zone at up to **2 knots**.

**Back bays.** Behind beach dunes, low-lying ground thickly covered with scrub gives way to wetlands of muddy islands separated by channels of brackish water.

**Rivers and canals.** A river is a large natural stream; the slope of the riverbed and the volume of water determine its current.

## 8. Fording Waterways

**Fords are dangerous — cross as quickly as possible.** A ford is any site in a river, stream, or canal where the water is shallow enough for troops or vehicles to cross without flotation devices. **Ford site criteria:** accessibility (low banks, gentle gradients), slow current (≤ **1.5 meters per second**), firm footing, shallow depth, narrow width, concealment. Measure width by the A–B–C–D method → `references/fording-and-knots.md`.

**Selection of a ford site.** **Concealment** — the ford hides personnel and vehicle movement from enemy observation. **Slow current** — the current should not exceed **1.5 meters per second** if possible.

**Determine the slope.** Units move into and out of water faster and more quietly if entry and exit points are not steep or muddy. **Clinometer** — measures slope percentage; a component of the M9 armored combat excavator and organic to many Marine engineer units (engineers are also taught field-expedient methods). **Line of sight and pace** — stand at the bottom of the slope, keep your eyes level, pick a spot on the slope, then pace the distance; paces multiplied by a standard measure of **0.75 meter** give the run; eye-level height (usually **1.5 to 1.7 meters**) gives the rise; repeat until the whole distance is measured.

**Determine the current speed.** Current speed increases as channels narrow — it may be necessary to locate a wider ford for a slower current. **Do not attempt to swim across currents moving faster than 1.5 meters per second** — equivalents: quick-time march rate of **120 counts per minute with one 30-inch step at each count**; **5 feet per second**; **3.5 miles per hour**; **5.5 kilometers per hour**.

**Measure river width.** Estimate from the width of the river's symbol on a scaled topographic map, or use the compass technique: stand at the water line (A) and shoot an azimuth to a point on the opposite bank (B); move upstream or downstream to a point (C) where you can shoot an azimuth **45 degrees** larger or smaller than the original; measure the distance between A and C — **that distance equals the river's width**.

**Calculate downstream drift.** A river's current causes personnel and equipment to drift downstream; aimed straight across, they sideslip downstream. The manual gives a formula using river width, current speed, and crossing speed; the crossing speed for a swimmer is generally limited to **1 meter per second**, and all measurements must be in the same unit of measure.

**The buddy system.** Whenever a Marine unit must enter or operate on the water, every Marine is paired with a swimming partner — an experienced swimmer with a weak swimmer.

**Water crossings.** **Care of weapons:** Marine infantry weapons and munitions are designed to operate after immersion, but protect weapons from moisture whenever possible. **Pack raft:** two waterproofed packs and two M16A2 service rifles — packs side by side, shoulder straps on the deck, tops opposite each other; loosen the main compartment straps; insert one rifle on each end between the straps and the packs with muzzles opposite each other, front sight post under the top flap; tighten the straps; use excess straps as safety lashing. **Gas-tube weapon care:** close the bolt before entering the water — water down the barrel can enter the gas tube.

**Single-rope bridge.** A temporary, quick way to cross small rivers; extra security in swift water. If the river has swift currents or water depths above **4 feet** and the unit is carrying sufficient rope, secure the rope on both banks as a handhold. **Rope:** nylon rope is normally coiled in **120-foot lengths**, **0.6 inches** in diameter, breaking strength about **3,840 pounds**. **Transport tightening system:** double butterfly knot + two carabiners; butterfly pulled about one-third of the distance across, then to the far shore. **Crossings:** High and dry (hang below on sling + carabiner, ankles crossed above rope), swift (face upstream, carabiner, walk sideways), slow (no carabiner, on back, pack as support). **Removal:** last Marine pulls the standing end, bowline around waist, far-shore pull-through.

**Knots (Appendix A).** **Square knot** — joins two ropes of equal diameter so they form one continuous rope. **Bowline** — loop that will not tighten or slip, easily untied. **Half hitch** — rope to tree or larger rope; not secure alone. **Two half hitches** — secure the running end. **Round turn and two half hitches** — fasten rope to a tree; does not jam. **Butterfly knot** — anchor lines; pulls a bridge taut. → `references/fording-and-knots.md`

## Operating Rules


1. **Mindset first.** Know the craft, its flotation, and its egress before you board; survival is training applied under control.
2. **Never inflate a life preserver inside the craft.** Inflate only once clear.
3. **Keep your clothes on.** Trousers and blouse are flotation and insulation; the pack is flotation too.
4. **In cold water, stay still.** Do not swim to stay warm; HELP or huddle, and swim only with a flotation device toward a visible shoreline.
5. **Rescue from a distance.** Reach, wade, or throw before you swim to a victim; never let the victim grab you; never sacrifice your life for the rescue.
6. **Fords are dangerous — cross fast, and never swim a current faster than 1.5 meters per second.**
