# FM 20-32 Mine/Countermine Operations

## Purpose

FM 20-32, *Mine/Countermine Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 29 May 1998, with Changes 1 through 5, the last dated 1 April 2005, 520 pages) — the Army's mine-warfare manual. Part One covers mine operations: mechanics, principles and minefield types, scatterable mines and delivery systems, special-purpose munitions, conventional mines, row mining, standard-pattern minefields, and reporting and recording. Part Two covers countermine operations: reduction, route and area clearance, mining in special environments, and booby traps and expedient devices. Appendices cover installation/removal (A), special-purpose munition controls (B), threat operations (C), Air Volcano (D), safety and training (E), mine awareness (F), countermine data (G), and metric conversion (H). The manual supersedes FM 20-32, 30 September 1992.

**Status and legal framing — read this first.** This manual is dated: it is a 1998 manual as amended through 2005, and current Army mine/countermine doctrine has moved on (including changes to US antipersonnel-mine policy). Per the preface, the era's US policy: employment of non-self-destructing antipersonnel mines (APLs) is restricted to (1) defending the US and its allies from armed aggression across the Korean demilitarized zone and (2) training personnel engaged in demining and countermine operations, and use of the M18A1 claymore in the command-detonation mode is not restricted under international law or Executive Order. All references in the manual to US employment of non-self-destructing APLs (such as row mining) are intended to provide doctrine for use in Korea only, and are printed in bold lettering throughout the manual. Detailed APL doctrine is also provided so US forces recognize how the enemy can employ these weapons. Commanders are directed to consider increased use of other systems — the M18A1 claymore, nonlethal barriers, sensors, and direct and indirect fires — as the US military seeks to end reliance on APLs. Treat the manual as the doctrinal record of its era: its mechanics, marking, reporting, reduction, and clearance procedures remain the reference for understanding mines, but its employment authorizations must be checked against current policy and law.

**The first rule of this subject.** Mines and booby traps do not distinguish friend from foe. Everything in this manual — marking, reporting, recording, turnover, inspection, and maintenance — exists because an unrecorded or unmarked minefield kills friendly soldiers and civilians long after the battle. An unreported, unrecorded minefield is a hazard, not an obstacle.

## 1. Mechanics of Mines

**1.1. What a mine is.** A land mine is an explosive device designed to destroy or damage equipment or personnel — equipment targets include ground vehicles, boats, and aircraft. A mine is detonated by its target, the passage of time, or controlled means. There are two types of land-based mines: antitank (AT) and antipersonnel (AP). Mines generally consist of a firing mechanism, a detonator or igniter, a booster charge, a main charge (usually forming the body of the mine), and a casing. Mines are hand-emplaced, remote-, ground-, or air-delivered. Soldiers can surface-lay or bury mines and place AHDs on hand-emplaced AT mines. **US forces are not authorized to employ AHDs on any type of AP mine** (some countries do).

**1.2. Initiating actions.** Mines are actuated by applying pressure (including tilt rod), pulling a trip wire, releasing tension or breaking a trip wire, releasing pressure, magnetic influence, electromagnetic frequency, seismic/vibration sensing, acoustic sensing, infrared sensing, or command detonation. Arming requires disengaging the safety device, usually by removing a safety pin.

**1.3. Antitank mines.** An AT mine produces a **mobility kill (M-Kill)** — destroying one or more vital drive components (for example, breaking a track) — or a **catastrophic kill (K-Kill)** — destroying the weapon system and/or the crew. Sensing types: **track-width** (usually pressure-actuated, requiring contact with the track) and **full-width** (activated by acoustics, magnetic influence, tilt rod, radio frequency, infrared, command, or vibration; tilt-rod or magnetic-influence fuses are the most common). Warhead types: **blast** (usually an M-Kill, though a K-Kill is also possible) and **shaped charge / self-forging fragment** (a Misznay-Schardin plate formed into a slug or long rod; a K-Kill is probable if the crew or ammunition compartment is hit).

**1.4. Antipersonnel mines.** AP mines can kill or incapacitate. Fusing includes pressure, seismic, wire, and command detonation. Effects: **blast** (cripples the foot or leg of a soldier who steps on it; can also kill); **bounding fragmentation** (throws a canister into the air, which bursts and scatters fragments); **direct fragmentation** (propels fragments toward the target); **stake fragmentation** (bursts and scatters shrapnel in all directions).

**1.5. Antihandling devices.** AHDs function in one or more modes — pressure, pressure-release, tension, and/or tilt — and detonate the mine when someone attempts to move, lift, or disarm it. US hand-emplaced AHDs include the M5 pressure-release firing device. AHDs do not prevent an enemy from reducing a minefield, but they make reduction slower and more dangerous. → `references/mine-types-and-scatterable.md`

## 2. Mine-Warfare Principles and Minefield Types

**2.1. Four types of minefields.** Volcano, Modular Pack Mine System (MOPMS), standard-pattern, and row mining are not types of minefields — they are means of emplacement used for tactical, nuisance, and protective minefields, and may be replicated by a phony minefield.

- **Protective minefields** protect soldiers, equipment, supplies, and facilities from enemy attack or other threats. They are usually employed at small-unit level (platoon or company/team); authority to emplace is normally delegated to the company/team commander. They provide close-in protection, deny mechanized penetration and dismounted infiltration, impose delay, and break up the assault — much like final protective fires. Composition follows the greatest close-combat threat: predominantly AP mines against dismounted infantry, predominantly AT mines against a tank threat. **Protective minefields must be recovered before the unit leaves the area**, or turned over to a relieving unit — often overlooked and difficult to control because emplacement is decentralized. Hasty protective minefields are temporary, laid from basic load; mines should be buried if time permits but may be surface-laid; AHDs and low-metallic mines are not used so the field can be easily recovered; mines are employed outside hand-grenade range but within small-caliber weapons range; all mines are picked up by the emplacing unit on leaving unless enemy pressure prevents retrieval or the field is transferred to a relieving commander.
- **Tactical minefields** directly affect the enemy's maneuver in a way that gives the defending force a positional advantage. They are integrated with intense fires and obstacle groups to produce disrupt, fix, turn, and block effects; they do not stop an attacker alone. Design variables include density, depth, front, and minefield variables; siting, emplacement, and resourcing are coordinated with the maneuver commander. Obstacle emplacement authority rests with the higher commander, and obstacles must not interfere with future operations.
- **Nuisance minefields** impose caution on enemy forces and disrupt, delay, and sometimes weaken or destroy follow-on echelons. Once emplaced they do not require cover by observation or direct fire — and the manual notes that marking a nuisance field is seldom worthwhile, since the field is meant to be found by the enemy.
- **Phony minefields** deceive the enemy about the exact location of real minefields, cause the attacker to question the decision to breach, and may cause him to expend reduction assets wastefully. They should be used only after the enemy has become mine-sensitive, and the deception story must never be compromised. **Live mines are never laid in a phony minefield** — a minefield designated as phony implies there are no live mines in it.

**2.2. Minefield marking.** Minefields are marked with fencing (barbwire or concertina) and mine markers, with signs on pickets, across the front and both sides and as far out as practical. Marking and covering fire should be the same for tactical and protective minefields. **Minefield marking discipline is the difference between an obstacle and a fratricide hazard** — a field that is not marked, or whose marking is not maintained, endangers friendly forces and civilians. → `references/minefield-design-and-emplacement.md`

**2.3. Turnover, inspection, and maintenance.** When a unit is relieved in place, protective minefields are turned over to the relieving unit on an obstacle-turnover worksheet; the relieving commander accepts responsibility for the field, its records, and its marking. Minefields are inspected and maintained for as long as the field remains in place.

## 3. Scatterable Mines and Delivery Systems

**3.1. General characteristics.** Scatterable mines (SCATMINEs) are remotely emplaced by artillery, aircraft, helicopter, ground vehicle, or manpack. They can be emplaced more rapidly than conventional mines, provide faster response and greater tactical flexibility, allow remote placement, and use less manpower and tonnage. Their self-destruct (SD) time clears the minefield on expiration, so the commander can later move through a previously denied area. Limitations: extensive coordination is required; mines are visible on the surface; accuracy and orientation are limited — a mine landing on its side or at an angle may not function as designed.

**3.2. Antipersonnel scatterable mines.** The M67 and M72 AP SCATMINEs are wedge-shaped, dispensed from the ADAM projectile (a special 155-mm artillery munition); each weighs 540 grams and is 7 centimeters high. The M74, BLU 92/B, M77, and Volcano AP SCATMINEs are cylindrical, 6 centimeters high and 12 centimeters in diameter, each containing 540 grams of Composition B4; they kill through combined blast and fragmentation to a fatal distance of 15 meters. Each has eight trip wires (four on top, four on bottom) that deploy after ground impact up to 12 meters from the mine; a tension of 405 grams on one wire breaks the electrical circuit and detonates the mine.

**3.3. Antitank scatterable mines.** All AT SCATMINEs are cylindrical, weigh approximately 1.8 kilograms, contain 585 grams of cyclonite (RDX), and have a magnetically induced fuse. They produce a K-Kill using a self-forging fragment warhead formed from a Misznay-Schardin plate, which penetrates the vehicle's belly armor; spalling metal kills the occupants. The warhead is bidirectional; the magnetic fuse detonates as the magnetic field changes over the mine. AHDs are built into 20 percent of M70, M73, and M75 mines; Volcano, M76, and BLU 91/B mines have no AHDs but may detonate when moved, sensing a significant change from their original orientation. SD times: 4 hours, 48 hours, or 15 days depending on type (M73/RAAM 48 hours, M70/RAAM 4 hours, BLU 91/B Gator 4/48 hours/15 days, M76/MOPMS 4 hours recyclable up to 3 times, Volcano 4/48 hours/15 days). Arming time is within 1 minute after ground impact for RAAM mines and 2 minutes for Gator, MOPMS, and Volcano. Five centimeters of water prevents formation of the Misznay-Schardin slug — mine the banks and approaches instead.

**3.4. Delivery systems.** **ADAM/RAAM** — 155-mm artillery-delivered AP/AT mines (9 mines per projectile). **Gator** — USAF-delivered (CBU-89/B dispenser, 72 mines per dispenser). **Volcano** — ground- and air-delivered (5 mines per M87 canister, 6 per M87A1). **MOPMS** — man-portable Modular Pack Mine System (17 mines per M131 dispenser). Command and control, authority, and coordination for scatterable-mine employment are specified in Chapter 3; scatterable minefields are reported on the scatterable minefield/munition field report and record worksheet (Chapter 8). → `references/mine-types-and-scatterable.md`

## 4. Special-Purpose Munitions

**4.1. M18A1 Claymore.** A command-detonated directional fragmentation munition; its use in the command-detonation mode is not restricted under international law or Executive Order. Installation and removal procedures are in Appendix A; controls and components in Appendix B.

**4.2. Selectable Lightweight Attack Munition (SLAM).** A multipurpose munition with four operating modes, selected by a selector switch: **bottom attack** (magnetic sensor, for vehicles passing over); **side attack** (passive infrared sensor — PIRS — active when the selector is set to 4, 10, or 24 HOURS and the PIRS cover is removed; the SLAM self-destructs (M4) or self-neutralizes (M2) if the selected time expires before detonation); **timed demolition** (selector set to 15, 30, 45, or 60 MINUTES; magnetic sensor and PIRS inoperable; detonates after the selected time); and **command detonation** (manual initiation using standard military blasting caps and a priming adapter, bypassing the fuse and safing-and-arming assembly). **Antitamper feature:** active only in bottom- and side-attack modes — the SLAM detonates if an attempt is made to change the selector switch position after arming.

**4.3. M93 Hornet.** An AT/antivehicular off-route munition of lightweight material (35 pounds) that one person can carry and employ. A nonrecoverable munition that automatically searches, detects, recognizes, and engages moving targets by top attack at a standoff distance of up to 100 meters. Employed by combat engineers, rangers, and SOF. The remote control unit (RCU) is a hand-held encoding unit; after encoding it can arm the Hornet, reset its SD times, or destroy it, at a maximum operating distance of 2 kilometers. The active battery pack is inserted during prearming and has an estimated life of 4 hours; do not prearm too early. SD times: 4 hours, 48 hours, 5 days, 15 days, or 30 days, determined by mission and commander's intent. Employed life is 60 days prearmed and 30 days armed; above 100°F, 15 days prearmed and 30 days armed. High winds, heavy rain, snow, ice, and extreme temperatures reduce detection range; radio-frequency jamming limits communications but not the Hornet's ability to engage targets. Employment scenarios: reinforcing conventional and Volcano minefields, gauntlet obstacles, and area-disruption obstacles. → `references/mine-types-and-scatterable.md`

## 5. Conventional Mines and Emplacement

**5.1. US conventional mines.** AT mines include the **M15** (pressure), **M19** (plastic, pressure), and **M21** (tilt-rod, shaped charge). AP mines include the **M14** (blast, pressure; 11.5–13.5 kilograms of force depresses the pressure plate; designed to incapacitate rather than kill) and the **M16** (bounding fragmentation; 103 mm diameter, 199 mm high including fuse, 3.5 kg; casualty radius 27 meters for M16/M16A1 and 30 meters for M16A2; activated by 3.6–9 kg of pressure on the fuse or a pull of 1.4–4.5 kg on the trip wire). (US employment of non-self-destructing AP mines is Korea-only per the status note.)

**5.2. Laying and concealment.** The method depends on the mine type, the ground, and the operation. Prong-activated AP mines are buried with prongs exposed and held firmly upright; buried more deeply they become unreliable. Trip-wire-activated AP mines are buried so the trip wire can function. Mines with pressure plates function when completely buried as long as they are buried with the top about 5 centimeters below ground; a hole dug much wider than the mine avoids bridging action that can prevent activation. Tilt-rod fuses require the mine body buried with the tilt-rod assembly clear of the ground; extension rods are camouflaged before the mine is armed. High pressure is required to activate AT mines; in soil with low bearing pressure (soft sand or clayey soil) the mine may be forced down without detonating unless the ground is prepared. After laying, remove all traces of digging; cut turf in an X, I, or U shape, lay the mine, and replace the turf. AT mines in standard-pattern minefields should be buried; single-impulse-fuse mines may be surface-laid if conditions dictate, but double-impulse-fuse mines should always be buried.

**5.3. Row mining (Chapter 6).** Row mining lays mines in rows rather than in the standard pattern; the minefield requirements computation worksheet determines the number of mines, regular strips, AHDs, strip cluster composition, man-hours, fencing and marking material, truckloads, and engineer tape. The manual's sample problem (400-meter field, density 0.5-0-0, 6-meter spacing) works out to 222 mines to request after the 10 percent resource factor. Standardized disrupt, fix, turn, and block row minefields are given in the chapter.

**5.4. Standard-pattern minefields (Chapter 7).** A cluster is a 2-meter-radius semicircle 3 meters off the strip centerline; live clusters contain one AT mine (Korea only: up to five mines, only one AT); omitted clusters contain no mines but are numbered and recorded on DA Form 1355. Clusters are omitted within lanes and gaps, within 2 meters of boundaries, lanes, or another cluster, and where terrain prohibits emplacement. The base mine in a live cluster is the first mine laid, 3 meters from the strip centerline; when a live cluster contains an AT mine, the AT mine is always the base mine. A regular strip has a centerline and two rows of clusters (Row 1 enemy side, Row 2 friendly side); strips are lettered, Strip A closest to the enemy; a standard-pattern minefield contains at least three regular strips; strip centerlines are at least 15 meters apart. Safety tapes are used 8 meters from the strip centerline so personnel installing trip wires do not move forward into armed clusters. The irregular outer edge (IOE) is on the enemy side, a baseline from which short strips extend at irregular angles; IOE clusters number about one-third those in a regular strip; no trip wires are used in the IOE, but AHDs may be employed. → `references/minefield-design-and-emplacement.md`

## 6. Reporting and Recording

**6.1. The record is the mission.** Every minefield and munition field is reported and recorded on **DA Form 1355 (Minefield Record)** and **DA Form 1355-1-R (Hasty Protective Minefield Record)**. Reports run from the emplacing unit through the chain of command; the scatterable minefield/munition field report and record worksheet covers scatterable fields. The obstacle numbering system is 11 characters plus one obstacle-status symbol: characters 1–4 identify the headquarters type and designation that established the zone (A = armor division/brigade, I = infantry division/brigade, C = cavalry division, R = cavalry regiment, Z = corps); character 5 is the obstacle zone letter; 6 is the belt number; 7 is the group letter; 8–9 are the obstacle type (for example, MB block, MD disrupt, MF fix, MT turn, MH hasty protective, MN nonstandard, MP protective, MQ nuisance, MS standard-pattern, MU dummy/decoy, SB Gator, SF ADAM/RAAM, SM MOPMS, SV Volcano, HC claymore, HH Hornet/WAM, HS SLAM); 10–11 are the obstacle number in the group; the status symbol is / planned, — being prepared, + prepared, X completed.

**6.2. What gets recorded.** Minefield/munition field records include the type and number of mines, cluster composition, strip layout, lanes and gaps, marking, and the location data needed to find, reduce, or recover the field. Booby traps are recorded the same way as nuisance mines (Chapter 8 procedures); when traps are set in areas occupied by the enemy they are not recorded — it is sufficient to note their location and type in after-action reports.

## 7. Countermine Operations

**7.1. Breaching.** A breach is a combined-arms operation by support, breach, and assault forces; failure to synchronize suppression and obscuration with reduction and assault can cause rapid, devastating losses. Breach lanes turned over to follow-on engineers are normally 4.5 meters wide; two to four assault lanes are normal for a brigade-size lead unit. Follow-on engineers widen lanes to 10 meters for two-way traffic and keep lanes a minimum of 100 meters apart.

**7.2. Clearing.** Clearing is the total elimination or neutralization of mines from an area, not usually conducted under enemy fire. A limited clearing operation follows a breaching force: engineers improve existing breach lanes, widen and mark them, and clear new ones. Area clearance begins with thorough reconnaissance to identify minefield limits and mine types; identified limits are marked with an expedient system of single-strand barbwire or concertina. Magnetic- or seismic-fused mines are attacked with mechanical assets; pressure mines can be destroyed with hand-emplaced explosives; trip wires on AP mines are eliminated with grapnel hooks before moving forward to detect mines. After destruction, engineers proof used lanes and routes with a mine roller or another blast-resistant device to ensure all mines were eliminated. **Demining** — complete removal of all mines and unexploded ordnance to safeguard the civilian population after hostilities cease — is extremely manpower- and time-intensive, sometimes contracted; it is not a formal Army mission, though SOF may support it.

**7.3. Minefield reduction (Chapter 10).** Detection systems include the Airborne Standoff Minefield Detection System (ASTAMIDS), mounted on a UH-60 Blackhawk, UAV, or fixed-wing aircraft, which detects and classifies thermal and other anomalies as suspected minefields (about 300 feet altitude, 70 knots airspeed, about 215 feet swath, 2-hour capability). The Interim Vehicle-Mounted Mine Detector (IVMMD) is mounted on a blast- and fragmentation-survivable vehicle to detect and mark buried and surface-laid metallic AT mines during route clearance, using a 3-meter-wide detection array and towing three mine-detonation trailers that apply heavy ground pressure to initiate pressure-activated mines (maximum operating speed 20 kph while towing). Reduction methods include explosive (mine-clearing line charges — MICLICs), mechanical (clearing blades, rollers, armored dozers with mine rakes), and manual (detectors, probes, grapnels, hand-emplaced explosives). → `references/countermine-reduction-clearance.md`

## 8. Route and Area Clearance

**8.1. Organization.** A brigade or battalion task force normally conducts clearance-in-zone operations; to clear a route, the battalion task force focuses a company team as the main effort. The task organization has a support force, an assault force, and a breach force that sweeps the route and reduces mine and explosive threats, task-organized into sweep teams.

**8.2. Sweep teams.** A sweep team is a trained detection team that searches for mines and explosive devices. A platoon-size element can normally clear a 4.5-meter-wide path; a squad-size element a 1.5-meter-wide path. The normal platoon-size sweep team is twelve soldiers in modified column. Equipment includes six mine detectors (three backups), six probes, two grappling hooks with 60 meters of rope each, demolition kits, mine- and lane-marking material, ten stakes, a panel marker, a minimum of four smoke grenades, and an operational map. When 100 percent coverage is required, the team establishes a clear lane with the grapple hook and cord, and the first mine-detector operator sweeps a 1.5-meter lane left or right of the cord up to the grapple hook. Routes are tracked by status (red, amber, green) based on time since clearance and the intelligence and enemy situations; a mine risk assessment is prepared before the operation order is issued.

**8.3. Mining in special environments (Chapter 12).** Urban mining uses buildings, roofs, dead spaces, and rubble: the M14 (Korea only) is small enough for stairs and cellars; the M16 (Korea only) with trip-wire actuation covers rooftops, backyards, and cellars; the M18A1 claymore can be sited on sides of buildings, in abandoned vehicles, or in sturdy structures — but claymores are hazardous in confined built-up areas, with backblast danger close to friendly forces.

## 9. Booby Traps and Expedient Devices

**9.1. Setting booby traps.** Setting booby traps is a dangerous task and must be carefully controlled. The setting-party commander establishes a control point on arrival at the trap area — a headquarters and material holding area. Each setting party consists of one or two men, working in a clearly defined area, positioned so accidental detonation will not cause friendly casualties; entry to areas where traps are being set is strictly controlled. Traps are marked until the area is evacuated, using standard booby-trap signs when available (a 28 × 20 cm sign, white with red markings) or temporary markings, removed after evacuation. Setting parties do not arm firing devices or traps until ordered; in a one-man party the man remains with the trap until it is armed; in a two-man party one man returns to the control point and reports while the other remains with the trap. Parties enter and leave by the same route. The commander inspects traps before ordering arming, and devises an arming plan letting parties withdraw safely — in open areas, arm from the furthest mine from the control point; in buildings, arm from the top down. **DANGER: do not attempt to further camouflage the area after the trap is armed.** Rehearse laying procedures whenever possible, laying practice traps and exercising control and safety measures.

**9.2. Clearing booby traps.** Procedures include pulling with a grapnel (select the pull site carefully — it might itself be mined or trapped; if a pulled trap does not explode, wait at least 30 seconds before approaching), trip-wire checks (place a grapnel hook as close as possible to the trip wire; do not touch it until the pulling party is in a covered area), pull-and-release and pressure mechanisms (one-half pound of explosive suspended above a pressure plate actuates it), destroying in place (only if damage from the explosion is acceptable; do not assume the main charge is safe to handle just because the mechanism has been destroyed), clearing by hand (extremely hazardous; only by EOD personnel or experienced engineers), explosive line charges, armor, and fire (unexploded traps after a fire are considered extremely sensitive and must not be cleared by hand). Improvised traps in low-intensity conflicts are variations on electric firing devices (current passed between two contacts, made from household items), mechanical firing devices, and delays — the simplest and most accurate delay is a clock or timer. → `references/booby-traps-safety-awareness.md`

## 10. Safety and Mine Awareness

**10.1. Safety (Appendix E).** Safety and training procedures govern mine handling, emplacement, and reduction, including MOPMS safety and fragment-hazard zones and fencing, Hornet safe separation and arming, and the booby-trap clearance precautions in §9.2.

**10.2. Mine awareness (Appendix F).** Mine awareness covers recognition of mines and minefields, the mine risk assessment prepared before operations, and reporting suspected mines through the chain of command. Any soldier who encounters a suspected mine or minefield marks it, reports it, and does not attempt to clear it without training and orders. Threat operations (Appendix C) describe how enemy forces employ mines, including AHDs on AP mines (which US forces do not do) and their breaching techniques.

## Output Contract

When answering a mine/countermine question from FM 20-32, give the mine or munition type and its functioning first, then the numbers, then the marking, reporting, and safety discipline that governs it. Always state whether the procedure is US-authorized or Korea-only/foreign-threat doctrine, and whether the item is self-destructing. Never present an unrecorded minefield as acceptable. Where current policy or doctrine differs from the 1998/2005 manual, say so.

## Operating Rules

1. **Report and record everything.** DA Form 1355 is not paperwork — it is what keeps friendly forces and civilians alive after the battle.
2. **Mark it, fence it, maintain it.** An unmarked minefield is a fratricide hazard, not an obstacle.
3. **Recover protective minefields or turn them over.** Never walk away from a protective field without turnover.
4. **Never lay live mines in a phony minefield.** The deception depends on it.
5. **No AHDs on AP mines for US forces.** Know the rule and know that threat forces do not follow it.
6. **Assume every suspected mine is live and possibly booby-trapped.** Pull with a grapnel from cover; wait 30 seconds after a failed pull; hand-clear only as EOD or an experienced engineer.
7. **Check current policy.** The manual's APL employment authorizations are Korea-only and dated; current law and policy govern.
