# Mine Types and Scatterable Mines — FM 20-32

## Mechanics of Mines

**Definition:** a land mine is an explosive device designed to destroy or damage equipment or personnel (equipment targets include ground vehicles, boats, and aircraft). It is detonated by the action of its target, the passage of time, or controlled means. Two types: antitank (AT) and antipersonnel (AP).

**Components:** firing mechanism or other device (sets off the detonator or igniter charge); detonator or igniter (sets off the booster charge); booster charge (may be attached to the fuse or igniter or be part of the main charge); main charge (in a container, usually forming the body of the mine); casing (contains all parts).

**Delivery:** hand-emplaced (manual arming; labor-, resource-, and transport-intensive), remote-delivered, ground-delivered (less resource-intensive than hand-emplaced; minefield boundaries precise though mines are not precisely placed), air-delivered (less time and labor; not as precisely placed). Soldiers can surface-lay or bury mines and can place antihandling devices (AHDs) on hand-emplaced AT mines. US forces are not authorized to employ AHDs on any type of AP mine.

**Initiating actions:** applying pressure (including tilt rod); pulling a trip wire; releasing tension or breaking a trip wire; releasing pressure; magnetic influence; electromagnetic frequency; seismic/vibration; acoustic; infrared; command detonation. Arming requires disengaging the safety device, usually by removing a safety pin.

## Antitank Mines

**Types of kills:** mobility kill (M-Kill) — destroys one or more vital drive components (for example, breaks a track) and immobilizes the target; catastrophic kill (K-Kill) — the weapon system and/or the crew is destroyed.

**Types of sensing:** track-width (usually pressure-actuated, requiring contact with the track) and full-width (acoustics, magnetic influence, tilt rod, radio frequency, infrared, command, or vibration; tilt-rod or magnetic-influence fuses are the most common).

**Types of warheads:** blast (high-explosive detonation; usually an M-Kill, though a K-Kill is also possible) and shaped charge / self-forging fragment (directed-energy warhead — a Misznay-Schardin plate formed into a slug or long rod; a K-Kill is probable if the crew or ammunition compartment is hit).

## Antipersonnel Mines

**Types of kills:** AP mines can kill or incapacitate.

**Types of sensing:** pressure (load placed on the mine), seismic (sensor detects vibrations), trip wire or break wire (something pulls or breaks the wire), command detonation (a soldier fires the mine on detecting the enemy in the blast area).

**Types of effects:** blast (cripples the foot or leg; can also kill); bounding fragmentation (throws a canister into the air, which bursts and scatters fragments); direct fragmentation (propels fragments in the general direction of the target); stake fragmentation (bursts and scatters shrapnel in all directions).

## Antihandling Devices

AHDs function in one or more modes — pressure, pressure-release, tension, and/or tilt — and detonate the mine when someone attempts to move, lift, or disarm it. US hand-emplaced AHDs include the M5 pressure-release firing device. AHDs do not prevent an enemy from reducing a minefield, but they make reduction slower and more dangerous.

## Scatterable Mines — General

**Capabilities:** faster response than conventional mines; remote placement; increased tactical flexibility (on expiration of the self-destruct time the minefield is cleared and the commander can move through an area previously denied); efficiency (delivered by fixed-wing aircraft, helicopters, artillery, manpack, or ground vehicles; reduced manpower, equipment, and tonnage); increased lethality (AT scatterable mines use a self-forging fragment to produce a full-width kill).

**Limitations:** extensive coordination with higher, adjacent, and supporting units; proliferation of targets; visibility on the surface; accuracy and orientation limits (a mine landing on its side or at an angle may not function as designed).

**Life cycle:** mines arm after ground impact (within 1 minute for RAAM, 2 minutes for Gator, MOPMS, and Volcano) and self-destruct at the end of the selected SD time (4 hours, 48 hours, 15 days, depending on type).

## Antipersonnel Scatterable Mines

- **M67 and M72 (ADAM):** wedge-shaped, dispensed from the ADAM projectile (special 155-mm artillery munition); each weighs 540 grams and is 7 centimeters high.
- **M74, BLU 92/B, M77, Volcano AP:** cylindrical, 6 cm high, 12 cm diameter; each contains 540 grams of Composition B4; kill through combined blast and fragmentation, shrapnel propelled upward and outward to a fatal distance of 15 meters. Each has eight trip wires (four top, four bottom) deploying after ground impact up to 12 meters from the mine; wires look like very fine olive-drab-green thread, weighted at the free end; 405 grams of tension on one wire breaks the electrical circuit and detonates the mine.

## Antitank Scatterable Mines

All AT SCATMINEs are cylindrical, weigh approximately 1.8 kilograms, contain 585 grams of cyclonite (RDX), and have a magnetically induced fuse. They are designed to produce a K-Kill using a self-forging fragment warhead formed from a Misznay-Schardin plate that penetrates the vehicle's belly armor; spalling metal kills the occupants. The warhead is bidirectional (fires from top or bottom). The magnetic fuse detonates as the magnetic field changes over the mine.

| Mine | Delivery | Fuse | SD time | Mines per delivery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M73 | 155-mm artillery (RAAM) | Magnetic | 48 hr | 9 per M718 projectile |
| M70 | 155-mm artillery (RAAM) | Magnetic | 4 hr | 9 per M741 projectile |
| BLU 91/B | USAF (Gator, CBU-89/B) | Magnetic | 4 hr / 48 hr / 15 days | 72 per dispenser |
| M76 | MOPMS (M131 dispenser) | Magnetic | 4 hr (recyclable up to 3 times) | 17 per dispenser |
| Volcano | Ground/air (M87/M87A1 canister) | Magnetic | 4 hr / 48 hr / 15 days | 5 per M87; 6 per M87A1 |

AHDs are built into 20 percent of M70, M73, and M75 mines. Volcano, M76, and BLU 91/B mines have no AHDs but may detonate when moved, because the mine senses a significant change from its original orientation. Due to small size, reduced explosive, and possible improper orientation, AT SCATMINEs have less chance of destroying a vehicle than a conventional full-width AT mine; an armored vehicle will not always be destroyed after an encounter. Five centimeters of water prevents formation of the Misznay-Schardin slug, so effectiveness in water obstacles is reduced — mine the banks and approaches instead.

**Delivery systems:** ADAM/RAAM (155-mm artillery), Gator (USAF CBU-89/B), Volcano (ground and air; components and disrupt/fix/turn/block minefields in Chapter 3), MOPMS (man-portable; emplacement and safety zone in Figure 3-11; safety zones, fragment hazard zones, and fencing in Chapter 3). Command and control, authority, and coordination for scatterable-mine employment are specified in Chapter 3.

## Special-Purpose Munitions

**M18A1 Claymore:** command-detonated directional fragmentation munition. Use in the command-detonation mode is not restricted under international law or Executive Order. Installation and removal in Appendix A; controls and components in Appendix B.

**SLAM (Selectable Lightweight Attack Munition):** four modes by selector switch —
- **Bottom attack:** magnetic sensor; vehicle passing over.
- **Side attack:** passive infrared sensor (PIRS) detects trucks and light armored vehicles by sensing the change in background temperature when vehicles cross in front of the PIRS port; directional, aligned with the explosively formed penetrator (EFP); active when the selector is set to 4, 10, or 24 HOURS and the PIRS cover is removed. Self-destructs (M4) or self-neutralizes (M2) if the selected time expires before detonation.
- **Timed demolition:** selector set to 15, 30, 45, or 60 MINUTES; magnetic sensor and PIRS inoperable; detonates after the selected time.
- **Command detonation:** manual initiation using standard military blasting caps and a priming adapter; bypasses the fuse and safing-and-arming assembly.
- **Antitamper:** active only in bottom- and side-attack modes — the SLAM detonates if an attempt is made to change the selector switch position after arming.

**M93 Hornet:** AT/antivehicular off-route munition, lightweight (35 pounds), one person can carry and employ. Nonrecoverable; automatically searches, detects, recognizes, and engages moving targets by top attack at a standoff of up to 100 meters, using sound and motion detection. Employed by combat engineers, rangers, and SOF. The remote control unit (RCU) is a hand-held encoding unit that interfaces with the Hornet in remote mode; after encoding it can arm the Hornet, reset its SD times, or destroy it, at up to 2 kilometers. Active battery pack inserted during prearming has an estimated life of 4 hours — do not prearm too early, or munitions become duds. SD times: 4 hours, 48 hours, 5 days, 15 days, or 30 days (mission and commander's intent); the munition self-detonates after SD time expires. Employed life: 60 days prearmed (remote arming), 30 days armed; above 100°F, 15 days prearmed and 30 days armed. High winds, heavy rain, snow, ice, extreme cold, and extreme heat reduce detection range. RF jamming (for example, the hand-emplaced expandable jammer, HEXJAM) limits communications but not the Hornet's ability to engage targets. Employment scenarios: conventional minefield reinforcement (two staggered rows of Hornets spaced 100 meters apart, 50–100 meters from the front edge of the minefield, plus a row 50 meters behind it to reduce breaching capability), Hornet reinforcing a Volcano minefield, area-disruption obstacle, gauntlet obstacle (one cluster and platoon), and Hornet-enhanced turn-and-fix obstacle groups. In close operations Hornets fix and weaken the enemy along its avenue of approach, serve as an offensive-support weapon, and act as a situational obstacle on exposed flanks; in deep operations they are emplaced along key routes in gauntlet obstacles and at C2 and logistics sites; in rear operations they are emplaced (unarmed) along key routes in preparation for retrograde; in early-entry operations they supplement light forces along high-speed avenues of approach.
