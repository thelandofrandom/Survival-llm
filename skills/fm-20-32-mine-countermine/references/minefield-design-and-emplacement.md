# Minefield Design and Emplacement — FM 20-32

## Mine-Warfare Concepts

Mines are explosive devices emplaced to kill, destroy, or incapacitate enemy forces and to interfere with enemy command and control. They are employed differently and target the enemy in unique ways that support the overall concept of the operation.

## Types of Minefields

- **Protective:** protect soldiers, equipment, supplies, and facilities from enemy attack or other threats (in operations other than war, preventing unauthorized access rather than destroying an enemy force). Usually employed at platoon or company/team level; authority normally delegated to the company/team commander. Hasty protective minefields are temporary, laid from basic load, mines buried if time permits but may be surface-laid; AHDs and low-metallic mines are not used so the field can be easily recovered; mines employed outside hand-grenade range but within small-caliber weapons range; all mines picked up by the emplacing unit on leaving unless enemy pressure prevents retrieval or the field is transferred to a relieving commander. Deliberate protective minefields are part of a unit's deliberate defense. Composition follows the greatest close-combat threat: predominantly AP mines against dismounted infantry threatening armor; predominantly AT mines against a tank threat to infantry. Neither AP nor AT mines are used in isolation. A protective minefield may be a few mines in front of a platoon or a standard-pattern minefield around an airfield. **Protective minefields must be recovered before the unit leaves the area, or turned over to the relieving unit.**
- **Tactical:** directly affect the enemy's maneuver to give the defending force a positional advantage. Integrated with intense fires and obstacle groups to produce disrupt, fix, turn, and block effects; they do not stop an attacker by themselves. Design variables include density, depth, and front; vehicle mine-encounter probability rises with minefield density (Figure 2-4). Siting and emplacement are coordinated with the maneuver commander; obstacle emplacement authority, obstacle control measures, and fratricide prevention are governed by the higher commander. Obstacle zones, belts, and groups structure the obstacle plan; the scheme-of-obstacle overlay and obstacle-execution matrix synchronize emplacement with maneuver.
- **Nuisance:** impose caution on enemy forces and disrupt, delay, and sometimes weaken or destroy follow-on echelons. Once emplaced they do not require cover by observation or direct fire. Marking and covering fire should be the same as for a tactical minefield, but marking a nuisance field is seldom worthwhile, since the field is meant to be found by the enemy.
- **Phony:** deceive the enemy about the exact location of real minefields, cause the attacker to question the decision to breach, and may cause him to expend reduction assets wastefully. Used only after the enemy has become mine-sensitive. The deception story must never be compromised. **Live mines are never laid in a phony minefield.**

Volcano, MOPMS, standard-pattern, and row mining are not types of minefields; they are means of emplacement.

## Minefield Marking

Criteria, perimeter, and techniques are specified in Chapter 2 (Figures 2-24, 2-25). Minefields are marked with fencing (barbwire or concertina) and mine markers, with signs on pickets, across the front and on both sides and as far out as practical. Marking and covering fire should be the same for tactical and protective minefields. **Minefield marking discipline is the difference between an obstacle and a fratricide hazard.**

## Turnover, Inspection, and Maintenance

When a unit is relieved in place, protective minefields are turned over to the relieving unit using an obstacle-turnover worksheet (Figure 2-26); the relieving commander accepts responsibility for the field, its records, and its marking. Minefields are inspected and maintained — marking repaired, mines checked, records updated — for as long as the field remains in place. Decentralized emplacement of protective obstacles makes consolidating reports and records difficult and requires command involvement.

## Minefield Supply Operations

Resupply nodes, resupply rules, supply location, and resupply methods (supply-point, service-station, and tailgate methods) are specified in Chapter 2. Resource requirements are determined from the minefield design: number of mines, strips, AHDs, man-hours, fencing and marking material, truckloads, and engineer tape.

## Conventional Mines

**AT mines:** M15 (pressure), M19 (plastic, pressure), M21 (tilt-rod, shaped charge). **AP mines:** M14 (blast, pressure; 11.5–13.5 kg of force depresses the pressure plate; designed to incapacitate rather than kill; modified by gluing a metal washer to the pressure plate to aid detection) and M16 (bounding fragmentation; 103 mm diameter, 199 mm high including fuse, 3.5 kg; casualty radius 27 meters for M16/M16A1 and 30 meters for M16A2; activated by 3.6–9 kg of pressure on the fuse or a pull of 1.4–4.5 kg on the trip wire). US employment of non-self-destructing AP mines is Korea-only (see the status note in SKILL.md).

**Laying and concealment:** the method depends on mine type, ground, and operation. Prong-activated AP mines should be buried with prongs exposed and held firmly upright so the target exerts a direct downward pressure rather than a sideways thrust; buried more deeply they become unreliable because the layer of spoil may prevent the mechanism from operating. Trip-wire-activated AP mines are buried so the trip wire can function. Mines with pressure plates function when completely buried as long as the top is about 5 centimeters below ground; digging the hole much wider than the mine avoids bridging action that can prevent activation. Tilt-rod fuses require the mine body buried with the tilt-rod assembly clear of the ground; extension rods are camouflaged before the mine is armed. High pressure is required to activate AT mines; in soil with low bearing pressure (soft sand or clayey soil) the mine may be forced down without detonating unless the ground is prepared. After laying, remove all traces of digging; cut turf in an X, I, or U shape, lay the mine, and replace the turf; make a final check after concealing each mine. AT mines in standard-pattern minefields should be buried; single-impulse-fuse mines may be surface-laid if conditions dictate, but double-impulse-fuse mines should always be buried.

## Row Mining (Chapter 6)

Row mining lays mines in rows rather than in the standard pattern. The minefield requirements computation worksheet (Figure 6-1) determines: number of mines; number of regular strips; number of AHDs; strip cluster composition; estimated man-hours for installation; amount of fencing and marking material; number of truckloads; and number of rolls of engineer tape. Calculation steps (round up at each step): (1) density × front = number of mines; (2) front ÷ mine spacing = mines per row; (3) mines ÷ mines per row = number of rows; (4) mines per row × rows = actual number of mines; (5) mines × 1.1 = mines to request (10 percent resource factor); (6) vehicle loads from Table 2-8; (7) fencing and marking material. Sample problem from the manual: a 400-meter row minefield at density 0.5-0-0 (AT-AP fragmentation-AP blast), mines spaced 6 meters apart — 200 mines; 67 mines per row; 3 rows; 201 mines; 222 to request; 2 five-ton dump trucks (222 ÷ 204); concertina ([400 × 2] + [200 × 2] + 160) × 1.4 = 1,904 meters; 127 pickets; 127 signs. Standardized disrupt, fix, turn, and block row minefields are given in the chapter, with site layout and step-by-step laying procedures (Figures 6-3 through 6-12) and the strip feeder report.

## Standard-Pattern Minefields (Chapter 7)

**Clusters:** a cluster is a 2-meter-radius semicircle located 3 meters off the strip centerline. Live clusters contain one AT mine (Korea only: up to five mines, only one AT). Omitted clusters contain no mines but are numbered and recorded on DA Form 1355. Clusters are omitted within lanes and gaps, within 2 meters of boundaries, lanes, or another cluster (including the IOE), and where terrain (trees, rocks) prohibits emplacement. The base mine in a live cluster is the first mine laid, 3 meters from the strip centerline; when a live cluster contains an AT mine, the AT mine is always the base mine (Korea only: if no AT mine is present, the largest metallic AP mine is the base mine). The first cluster in a strip is on the enemy side, 6 meters from the beginning-of-strip marker. Minimum distance between a cluster and a lane, gap, boundary, or another cluster is 2 meters (measured from the edge of the cluster). Cluster composition (number of mines by type in any cluster in a specific group) remains the same through the entire strip and is recorded on DA Form 1355. The cluster boundary must be at least 15 meters from the minefield perimeter fence. Clusters are numbered beginning with the first cluster on the enemy side; odd-numbered clusters are always on the enemy side of the strip, even-numbered on the friendly side.

**Regular strips:** a regular strip (lettered strip) consists of a strip centerline and two rows of clusters (Row 1 enemy side, Row 2 friendly side). Strips are designated by letters (A, B, and so forth), Strip A closest to the enemy. A standard-pattern minefield contains at least three regular strips. Minimum distance between strip centerlines is 15 meters; there is no maximum. Safety tapes are used behind each regular strip, 8 meters from the strip centerline (3 meters from the outer edge of the cluster), so personnel installing trip wires do not move forward into armed clusters. Marking of end points indicates the direction of laying (for example, A1 to A2).

**Irregular outer edge (IOE):** the IOE is on the enemy side of the minefield — a baseline from which short strips extend at irregular angles, identified by turning-point markers. Turning points should be no more than 45 degrees from the last azimuth; the length of short strips is not standard; a marker is emplaced at the end of each short strip, numbered consecutively beginning with I1E. The number of clusters in an IOE is approximately one-third the number in a regular strip. The first cluster along a short strip is placed on the enemy side and must be at least 6 meters from the IOE baseline; the cluster boundary must be at least 2 meters from the IOE baseline. No trip wires are used in the IOE, but AHDs may be employed. (Korea only: AP mines actuated by trip wires are placed on the enemy side of each regular strip; no more than one mine per cluster uses trip wires; no more than two trip wires extend from the mine; trip wires are angled toward the enemy and at least 2 meters from the cluster, lane border, and minefield boundary; trip wires are used only with AP fragmentation mines; they are not considered AHDs.)

**Lanes and gaps:** minefield lanes and gaps are marked and recorded; lane closure procedures are specified in Chapter 7 (Figure 7-9). Mine-emplacement procedures and laying and fusing sequences are given in Figures 7-7 and 7-8.

## Reporting and Recording (Chapter 8)

Every minefield and munition field is reported and recorded on **DA Form 1355 (Minefield Record)** and **DA Form 1355-1-R (Hasty Protective Minefield Record)**; scatterable fields use the scatterable minefield/munition field report and record worksheet. The reporting chain runs from the emplacing unit through the chain of command (Figure 8-1).

**Obstacle numbering system (Table 8-1):** 11 characters plus one obstacle-status symbol. Characters 1–4: headquarters type and numerical designation that established the zone (A = armor division/brigade, I = infantry division/brigade, C = cavalry division, R = cavalry regiment, Z = corps). Character 5: obstacle zone letter. Character 6: belt number. Character 7: group letter. Characters 8–9: obstacle type (Table 8-2). Characters 10–11: obstacle number in the group. Status symbol: / planned, — being prepared, + prepared, X completed. Example: I005-A2A-SM21/ — the 5th Infantry Division planned the obstacle in Zone A; 21st obstacle in Group A, Belt 2; not yet executed; MOPMS field.

**Obstacle type abbreviations (Table 8-2, selected):** M minefield/munition field — MB block, MC chemical, MD disrupt, MF fix, MH hasty protective, MN nonstandard, MO point, MP protective, MQ nuisance, MS standard-pattern, MT turn, MU dummy/decoy; S scatterable — SB Gator, SF ADAM and RAAM, SM MOPMS, SV Volcano, SW scatterable mines (generic); H hand-emplaced munitions — HC claymore, HH Hornet/WAM, HO other, HS SLAM; I improvised explosive devices — ID directional, IO omnidirectional; T booby traps — TA booby-trapped area, TB bodies, TE equipment, TM materiel, TP passage; W wire obstacles; A miscellaneous; B bridge demolition; R road crater; U unexploded ordnance.
