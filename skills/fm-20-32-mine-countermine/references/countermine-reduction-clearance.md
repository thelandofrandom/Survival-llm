# Countermine, Reduction, and Clearance — FM 20-32

## Countermine Operations (Chapter 9)

**Breaching fundamentals:** a breach is a combined-arms operation by support, breach, and assault forces. Failure to synchronize effective suppression and obscuration with reduction and assault can result in rapid, devastating losses of friendly troops in the obstacle or the enemy's fire sack. The commander cannot adequately synchronize combat power in the short time available when he encounters an obstacle under fire; the number of decisions required will rapidly overwhelm him. Synchronization is ensured through detailed reverse planning, clear subunit instructions, effective command and control, and a well-rehearsed force.

**Clearing operations:** clearing is the total elimination or neutralization of mines from an area. It is not usually conducted under enemy fire, but can be conducted by engineers during war or after hostilities as part of nation assistance. A limited clearing operation can be conducted by follow-on engineers after the breaching force has reduced the minefield and secured the area: engineers initially improve existing breach lanes by widening and marking them, and clear and mark new lanes. A clearing operation is also conducted to eliminate all mines in a minefield (previously identified, reported, and marked in a friendly area of operations) that hinders mobility or is a hazard to friendly forces.

**Upgrade of breach lanes:** lane clearance is more deliberate than lane breaching and normally takes longer. The breach force turns over lanes to follow-on engineers; lanes are normally 4.5 meters wide. Two to four assault lanes are normal if the lead unit was brigade-size. If forces continue to pass through existing lanes while further reduction is conducted, follow-on engineers first reduce new lanes. At a minimum, two lanes are required for an assaulting task force and one lane for an assaulting company/team. Follow-on engineers: ensure lanes are a minimum of 100 meters apart; reduce additional lanes using the equipment and techniques in Chapter 10; widen lanes to 10 meters to allow two-way traffic; mark breach lanes using the original marking system or the division SOP; emplace entrance, exit, and left and right lane markers to provide day and night capability. Traffic control is critical during lane reduction and when shifting lanes; engineers may provide guides at the lanes. A limited amount of mechanical breaching assets is available for clearing; follow-on engineers will probably not have tank-mounted mine-clearing blades or rollers — the main mechanical clearing asset is an armored dozer with a mine rake. Mine-clearing line charges (MICLICs) are used if available. To eliminate the danger of forces entering the minefield adjacent to lanes, the minefield is marked with fencing (barbwire or concertina) and mine markers across the front, on both sides, between lanes, and to the left and right of the crossing site as far out as practical. Engineers may help remove damaged vehicles from minefield lanes; recovery vehicles should be available near lanes.

**Area clearance:** actions begin with a thorough reconnaissance to identify the minefield limits and the types of mines — a time-consuming process that is hazardous to shortcut. Identified limits are marked with an expedient system of single-strand barbwire or concertina. In the worst case the minefield was reported but not marked and its limits are unknown. If mines are magnetic- or seismic-fused, mechanical assets are used. Pressure mines can be destroyed with hand-emplaced explosives. When a manual procedure is used, eliminate trip wires on AP mines with grapnel hooks before moving forward to detect mines. Engineers visually detect mines or detect them with mine detectors and probes, and mark mines for destruction by explosives. After the mines are destroyed, engineers proof used lanes and routes with a mine roller or another blast-resistant device to ensure all mines were eliminated.

**Demining:** complete removal of all mines and unexploded ordnance to safeguard the civilian population within a geopolitical boundary after hostilities cease. Extremely manpower- and time-intensive, sometimes contracted. Not a formal Army mission or function, though SOF may support it.

## Minefield Reduction (Chapter 10)

**Detection systems:**

- **ASTAMIDS (Airborne Standoff Minefield Detection System):** provides rapid detection of minefields; mounted on a UH-60 Blackhawk helicopter, an unmanned aerial vehicle, or a fixed-wing aircraft. It detects and classifies thermal and other anomalies as suspected minefields along routes or in areas of interest. Components: a sensor with associated electronics, the minefield-detection algorithm and processor (MIDAP), an air-data package (GPS, radar altimeter, inertial measurement unit), a power supply, workstations, a digital data recorder, mounting racks, and a modified floor. The aircrew must maintain an altitude of 300 feet and an airspeed of approximately 70 knots for accurate detection within a sensor ground swath of approximately 215 feet wide; 2-hour operational capability. On initial detection the operator communicates it to the pilot, who flies a verification pass; if detection is indicated again, the pilot resumes the mission (route reconnaissance) or continues the survey pattern to determine the minefield borders. ASTAMIDS is not as precise as ground detection systems but is accurate enough to help mitigate the dangers of minefields. A Blackhawk flying the steady altitude, speed, and path required is extremely vulnerable and must be planned and coordinated carefully.
- **IVMMD (Interim Vehicle-Mounted Mine Detector):** mounted on a blast- and fragmentation-survivable vehicle; designed to detect and mark buried and surface-laid metallic AT mines, primarily during route clearance. Should not be used where the enemy employs mines that are not pressure-fused. A complete IVMMD consists of one mine-detection vehicle (MDV), one towing/mine-detection vehicle (T/MDV), three mine detonation trailers, spare-wheel modules, and a container of spare parts. The MDV (4.8 tons, 4-cylinder engine, manual transmission) has a 3-meter-wide detection array of two induction coils (left and right) suspended between the axles; on encountering a metallic object the operator is notified by an audible signal and a visual signal denoting which side detected it; the operator stops, backs up, reencounters the object (locate mode to identify, pinpoint mode to find the center), and activates a marking system that deploys water-based ink onto the roadway. The MDV can negotiate vertical slopes up to a 20 percent grade. The T/MDV (6-cylinder engine) tows three detonation trailers; maximum operating speed while towing is 20 kph; it can negotiate 20 percent grades, but going down requires first gear with trailer brakes deployed. Each detonation trailer has two axles of different lengths so the three trailers together cover the full width; they are very heavy and apply heavy ground pressure to initiate pressure-activated mines.

**Reduction methods:** explosive (mine-clearing line charges — MICLICs), mechanical (mine-clearing blades, mine-clearing rollers, armored dozers with mine rakes), and manual (mine detectors, probes, grapnel hooks, hand-emplaced explosives). Countermine data (Appendix G) gives the technical data used in planning reduction.

## Route and Area Clearance (Chapter 11)

**Task organization:** a brigade or battalion task force normally conducts clearance-in-zone operations. To clear a route, the battalion task force focuses a company team as the main effort on the proposed main supply route (MSR). The organization has three forces:

- **Support force:** two maneuver platoons and the maneuver company team XO; flank security, rear security, and protection to the breach force; neutralizes hostile forces encountered. In rugged terrain or highly mined areas, moving the assault force on the flanks is too risky — aviation assets can provide flank security while ground forces provide rear security. The assault force also searches for suspected off-route mines.
- **Assault force:** a maneuver platoon, an engineer squad, a mortar section, a medical team, a PSYOP team, an EOD team (or one on call), and a forward observer; mission as in a breaching operation (Chapter 9).
- **Breach force:** a maneuver platoon (including the commander) and an engineer platoon (minus); sweeps the route and reduces mine and explosive threats, task-organized into sweep teams.

**Sweep teams:** a trained detection team that searches for mines and explosive devices. Organization depends on the sweep mission and the length, width, and surface composition (pavement, gravel, dirt) of the area. A platoon-size element can normally clear a 4.5-meter-wide path; a squad-size element a 1.5-meter-wide path. The normal platoon-size sweep team is twelve soldiers in a modified column, supervised by the platoon leader; best suited for sweeping routes in friendly territory not under constant surveillance. Personnel and equipment (Table 11-2): NCOIC, mine-detector operators, probers/markers, radio operator, demolition teams, medics, vehicle operator; six mine detectors (three backups) with extra batteries, six probes, two grappling hooks with 60 meters of rope each, one demolition kit or bag per demolition man, mine-marking material, lane-marking tape, ten stakes, one panel marker, four smoke grenades minimum, and an operational map with maneuver graphics.

**Sweep technique:** when 100 percent coverage is required, the team establishes a clear lane from which to operate: the grapple hook is thrown one additional time into the suspected minefield, the cord is pulled taut with the free end secured to a stake, establishing a left or right physical boundary marker; the first mine-detector operator sweeps a 1.5-meter lane left or right of the cord, up to the grapple hook.

**Control:** the tasked unit must have a clear understanding of the mission, intent, and end state (for example, clear the road width including the shoulders and secure the route). The maneuver commander/task force S3 controls movement of all personnel and equipment along the route (travel authorization coordinated through the S4); prepares a mine risk assessment before issuing the operation order (example in Appendix F); tracks route status (red, amber, green) based on time since clearance and the intelligence and enemy situations; tracks clearance progress and integrates it into maneuver and combat service support plans; determines route length using clearly definable start and end points; sets priorities for the route-clearance element; and coordinates with adjacent units, the host nation, nongovernmental and private voluntary organizations, and SOF. PSYOP/civil affairs support the counterintelligence effort through civilian interviews, direct civilians to displaced-personnel holding areas, and disperse civilians and provide traffic management to isolate the route during clearance.

## Mining Operations in Special Environments (Chapter 12)

**Urban terrain:** building obstacles include areas within and adjacent to buildings; forces lay mines with wire obstacles to deny infantry access to covered routes and weapon positions. Roof obstacles combine wire with booby traps and directional fragmentation mines, with antihelicopter obstacles. Dead spaces — areas that cannot be observed and are protected from direct fire — are restricted with obstacles and mines. Effective AP mines in urban terrain: the M14 (Korea only), whose small size suits stairs and cellars and which can be used with metallic AP and AT mines to confuse and hinder breaching; the M16 (Korea only), whose trip-wire actuation covers rooftops, backyards, and cellars (twine or wire attached to the release-pin ring can rig it for command detonation); and the M18A1 claymore, which can be sited on sides of buildings, in abandoned vehicles, or in any sturdy structure — a series along a street establishes a highly effective ambush zone, and claymores fill the dead space in the final protective fires of automatic weapons. **Caution:** claymores present a hazard in confined, built-up areas; exercise caution close to friendly forces because of backblast danger. Conventional AT mines are also employed in urban terrain. Chapter 12 also covers mining in other special environments (desert, jungle, cold, mountainous, and riverine terrain) and the effects of environment on mine functioning and detection.
