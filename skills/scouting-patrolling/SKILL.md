# Scouting and Patrolling

## Purpose

MCTP 3-01A *Scouting and Patrolling* (July 2020, 314 pages) is the Marine Corps' combined manual for the two skills that let a small unit move through hostile ground, find the enemy and report back without being found. Part I (Chapters 1–5) is patrolling: planning, organization, types of patrols, movement, and the patrol base. Part II (Chapters 6–11) is scouting: the hunter's mindset, observation, tracking, profiling in a population, and fieldcraft. It replaces and consolidates older scouting/patrolling publications and reflects the hard lessons of Iraq and Afghanistan — IEDs, drones, operating among civilians, and that every Marine is an intelligence collector.

Who it's for: patrol leaders, scouts, and anyone who must move a small team through terrain where the enemy may be present, gather information, or conduct a raid or ambush — and, for the survival-LLM reader, anyone who needs to move unseen, observe without being observed, read sign, or operate where the population itself is the terrain. The core idea: a patrol succeeds or fails in planning and rehearsal, long before it leaves friendly lines; and scouting is hunting — the scout thinks like a hunter, reads the ground like a tracker, and reports what he sees without embellishment.

## 1. Planning

**Definition of a patrol.** A patrol is a detachment sent out by a larger unit to conduct a specific mission — to gather information (reconnaissance) or to conduct a combat task (raid, ambush, security, contact). Patrols are temporary, mission-organized, and self-sufficient.

**Purpose of patrolling.** Reconnaissance patrols answer the commander's information requirements; combat patrols accomplish a combat task. Both extend the commander's eyes, ears, and reach beyond friendly lines, and deny the enemy the same.

**Intelligence.** Patrolling exists inside the intelligence cycle: direction (what the commander needs to know) → collection (the patrol goes and looks) → processing (analysis) → dissemination (the answer reaches the decision-maker). The patrol leader must understand the intelligence requirements before planning anything else. A patrol that returns with interesting information but not what the commander needed has failed.

**Types of patrols (overview).** Combat patrols: raid, ambush, security, contact. Reconnaissance patrols: zone, area, route. (Detail in section 3 below.)

**Patrol movement techniques.** Foot (stealthy, slow, terrain-independent — the default), mounted (fast, noisy, road-dependent), waterborne (where water dominates the terrain). The choice is driven by mission, terrain, enemy, and time — and foot movement is the baseline every patrol must execute.

**Tenets of patrolling.** Six, non-negotiable:
1. **Detailed planning** — no patrol leaves without a complete plan, briefed and rehearsed.
2. **Productive rehearsals** — rehearse actions on the objective, actions on contact, and passage of lines until they are automatic. A rehearsal that only walks through the easy parts is worse than none.
3. **Thorough reconnaissance** — of the route, the objective, and the enemy, from maps, imagery, and prior patrols, before departure.
4. **Positive control** — the patrol leader maintains control of every element at all times; no one moves without the leader knowing.
5. **All-around security** — 360-degree security at all times, including during halts, in the patrol base, and during actions on the objective.
6. **Every Marine a collector** — every member observes, remembers, and reports. Collection is not a specialist task; it is everyone's job.

**Planning considerations.** Route planning (primary and alternate routes, checkpoints, danger areas, rally points); fire support planning (available fires, how to call them, no-fire areas); explosive hazards (IED/mine threat, route clearance, actions on discovery); environment (weather, terrain, vegetation, water, temperature, disease). The patrol leader plans for the worst case on each and briefs it.

**Unit commander's duties.** The commander who orders the patrol determines the requirements, assigns the unit, provides adequate planning time and all available information to the patrol leader, provides special skills and equipment (corpsman, interpreter, engineer, air support), provides miscellaneous support (transport, resupply, communications), supervises the plan and preparations, and debriefs the patrol on return. The debrief is part of the mission, not an afterthought: intelligence dies if not extracted promptly and completely.

**Patrol leader duties — the troop-leading steps.**
1. **Begin planning** — receive the mission, issue a warning order, make a tentative plan, start the movement of personnel and equipment.
2. **Arrange reconnaissance** — coordinate map, imagery, and human reconnaissance of the route and objective.
3. **Make reconnaissance** — personally reconnoiter when possible, or use the best available substitute.
4. **Complete the plan** — finalize, including PACE contingencies (Primary/Alternate/Contingency/Emergency) for communications and movement.
5. **Issue the order** — brief the patrol in full, five-paragraph order format (situation, mission, execution, administration/logistics, command/signal). Every member must understand the mission, route, objective, actions on contact, and rally points.
6. **Supervise** — inspect personnel and equipment, conduct rehearsals, verify readiness. Supervision continues throughout the patrol.

**Combat operations center (COC) coordination.** The COC is the patrol's lifeline: the watch officer tracks the patrol, the COC provides radio checks, position reports, and fire support coordination, and information flows both ways — the patrol reports position, status, and findings; the COC updates the patrol on enemy, friendly forces, and support. The patrol and the COC agree in advance on reporting intervals, check-in procedures, and what happens if the patrol misses a check-in (the overdue-patrol procedure). A patrol that cannot communicate is on its own — plan for that.

## 2. Patrol Organization

**Organization.** A patrol is task-organized for its mission: the patrol leader builds it from available personnel and attachments. The standard structure is a patrol leader, an assistant patrol leader, and elements (headquarters, security, assault/support, reconnaissance) composed of teams. The size varies with the mission — a reconnaissance patrol may be four to six Marines; a raid patrol may be a reinforced squad or platoon.

**Elements and teams.** Typical elements: headquarters (patrol leader, assistant, radio operator, corpsman), security element (protects the patrol and the objective), assault element (conducts the action on the objective), support element (fire support from a support-by-fire position), and reconnaissance element (scouts the route and objective). Teams are the smallest building blocks (two to four Marines) with a designated team leader. Every element has a leader reporting to the patrol leader; every team has a leader reporting to the element leader. The chain is short and explicit.

**Patrol enablers.** Organic enablers come from the parent unit (corpsman, radio operator, machine gunner). Supporting enablers are attached for the mission (engineer for breaching, interpreter, dog team, air controller, fire support observer). The patrol leader requests enablers early — attachments need time to plan and rehearse with the patrol, and an enabler arriving at departure time is a liability.

**Key leader responsibilities.** *Patrol leader* — responsible for everything: planning, preparation, execution, and the patrol's welfare. *Assistant patrol leader* — second in command; takes over if the leader becomes a casualty; supervises the rear during movement; manages the patrol base, resupply, and casualty evacuation. *Element leaders* — control their elements and report to the patrol leader. *Team leaders* — control their teams and report to the element leader. *Radio operator* — maintains communications and keeps the log. *Unit corpsman* — medical care, casualty collection point, medical advice.

## 3. Types of Patrols

**Combat patrols.** Conducted to accomplish a combat task.
- *Raid patrols* — a surprise attack on a fixed objective to destroy or capture it or its personnel, then withdraw before the enemy can react. Keys: surprise, speed, violence of action, planned withdrawal. Organized into security, assault, and support elements, with a clear plan for entry, action on the objective, and exfiltration.
- *Ambush patrols* — a surprise attack from a concealed position on a moving or temporarily halted enemy: destroy or capture personnel and equipment, harass, deny freedom of movement, collect prisoners and material. Types: point ambush (single killing zone), area ambush (multiple related ambushes), anti-armor ambush. Success needs a good killing zone (the enemy is in it, you can see and shoot into it, he cannot easily escape), surprise, coordinated fires, and a rehearsed withdrawal. Plan the site (fields of fire, cover, concealment, routes in and out), the trigger (a point, a signal, a command), the assault element's movement into the killing zone, and security for the flanks and withdrawal route. Execute: occupy quietly, all-around security, wait, initiate on the trigger with maximum violence, assault through the killing zone to confirm kills and collect intelligence, then withdraw on a planned route to a rally point. Never linger.
- *Security patrols* — protect a unit or installation: detect and destroy enemy reconnaissance, provide early warning, deny the enemy observation of friendly positions (perimeter patrols, observation posts, sweeps of dead space).
- *Contact patrols* — establish contact with a friendly unit or element to exchange information or coordinate operations (recognition signals, link-up procedures, communications).

**Reconnaissance patrols.** Conducted to gather information without being detected and without engaging the enemy — "reconnaissance by stealth." Fundamentals: gain the information, avoid detection, avoid decisive engagement, and report. If a reconnaissance patrol is forced to fight, the mission has usually already failed — the enemy knows you are there, and the information you sought may now be worthless.
- *Zone reconnaissance* — reconnaissance of a defined area (a zone) to determine the enemy's disposition, terrain, and routes within it.
- *Area reconnaissance* — reconnaissance of a specific location (a hill, a village, a crossing) to answer specific questions about it.
- *Route reconnaissance* — reconnaissance of a route to determine its suitability for movement: its condition, obstacles, danger areas, and enemy presence.
- Equipment for reconnaissance patrols is light: observation gear (binoculars, night vision, cameras), communications, and enough firepower for self-defense — not enough for a pitched battle.

**Tracking.** A specialized form of reconnaissance: following the trail (spoor) of a person, animal, or vehicle to find them. Covered in detail in Chapter 9 and in the tracking reference.

## 4. Movement to and from the Objective Area

**Passage of lines.** Moving through friendly lines — departing and re-entering — is one of the most dangerous moments of a patrol, because friendly forces may mistake the patrol for the enemy. Procedure: coordinate in advance with the unit holding the line (time, location, number of personnel, recognition signals, password and countersign); depart through a designated passage point with a friendly-unit guide; on re-entry, approach slowly, make contact by the agreed signal, and wait to be recognized before moving. Never surprise a friendly position. The same discipline applies to re-entry after an ambush or raid, when the patrol is stressed and moving fast — that is exactly when fratricide happens.

**Organization for movement.** Formations are chosen for control, security, and reaction: *column* (fast, easy to control, vulnerable from the flanks); *wedge* (V-shape, good all-around security, the default for open terrain); *line* (maximum firepower forward, for the assault); *echelon* (protects an exposed flank); *vee* (inverted wedge, firepower forward and to the flanks). Control is through hand-and-arm signals, radio (sparingly — radio silence is the default), and the patrol leader's position. Under limited visibility: slower, closer spacing, more frequent halts, compass and pace count.

**Control measures.** *Checkpoints* are prominent terrain features along the route for position confirmation. *Rally points* are where the patrol reassembles if dispersed: initial (near departure), en route, objective, and re-entry (near friendly lines). Every member must know every rally point and the actions at each. If scattered, members move to the nearest rally point and wait the prescribed time; if the patrol leader does not arrive, the senior member present takes charge and follows the contingency plan.

**Danger areas.** Any place where the patrol is exposed to enemy observation or fire: open ground, roads, trails, streams, ridgelines. Actions: halt in cover, observe, send a reconnaissance element across, establish security on the far side, then cross the rest of the patrol in small groups or on line, quickly, at the widest point. With an IED threat: scan for indicators (disturbed earth, wires, debris, dead animals, absence of people), use a cleared lane, keep spacing, never step where you cannot see the ground, never bunch up. Cross water at the shallowest, narrowest point with the least current, security on the far bank first.

**Patrol hide sites.** A concealed location where the patrol halts to observe, rest, or wait: cover and concealment, good observation, multiple routes in and out, away from trails and population, not on an obvious feature. Occupy quietly, all-around security, observation post, noise and light discipline.

**Patrol leader's actions in developing the situation.** When the patrol makes contact or finds something unexpected: halt and establish security; observe and report; determine what the contact is (size, composition, activity, direction); decide whether to engage, bypass, or withdraw — and act. Never rush into a fight without understanding it. The withdrawal is planned with the same care as the approach: a different route if possible, rally points, a re-entry plan, and a contingency for pursuit. The most dangerous moment of a raid or ambush is the withdrawal, when the enemy is alerted and the patrol is loaded with casualties and prisoners.

**Actions on contact.** The manual's immediate action drills are the heart of patrol survival:
- *Immediate action drills* — pre-rehearsed responses to contact: return fire, seek cover, report, and maneuver. The first seconds decide the outcome; drills exist so the patrol acts without waiting for orders.
- *Reaction to an ambush* — if caught in the killing zone, the response is immediate, violent, and aggressive: return fire, throw smoke, and assault through the ambush (attacking into the ambush is usually safer than running out of it — the killing zone is designed to kill people who run). If caught outside the killing zone, maneuver to support the engaged element and attack the ambushers from the flank. Never freeze, never bunch, never retreat along the route you came in on (the most likely place for a second ambush).
- *Reaction to an IED discovery* — halt, establish security, mark the device, report, and bypass by a safe distance. Do not approach, do not touch, do not attempt to disarm it unless trained and tasked to do so. If the device is command-detonated, the triggerman is nearby — security must cover 360 degrees, including high ground and buildings.
- *Reaction to an IED attack* — after the blast, expect a follow-on ambush. Take cover, return fire, establish security, treat casualties, and report. Do not rush to the blast site — it may be a secondary device.
- *Reaction to an air attack* — disperse, take cover, and go still. Do not fire at aircraft unless ordered and you have a realistic chance of hitting them; firing reveals your position.
- *Reaction to an enemy unmanned aircraft (drone)* — take cover and concealment, go still, minimize electronic emissions, and report. Drones are the enemy's eyes; if one sees you, artillery or an ambush follows. Movement under drone observation is a deliberate problem: move at night, in bad weather, under cover, in small groups, and never in a predictable pattern.

## 5. Establishing and Operating a Patrol Base

A patrol base is a secure location where the patrol halts for an extended period — to rest, plan, resupply, or prepare for an operation. Selection: away from trails, population, and obvious features; with cover and concealment, water nearby if possible, and good observation. Occupy in a planned sequence (security first), establish a perimeter with all-around security, with priorities of work: security, communications, hygiene, water, food, rest, equipment maintenance, in that order. Exit in reverse order, quietly, leaving no sign of occupation — a patrol base found after the patrol leaves tells the enemy where the patrol was and where it may be going.

## 6. The Scout as a Hunter

**Scouting.** The scout is the commander's eyes. Basic scouting skills: observation, reporting, movement, fieldcraft, and communications. Advanced scouting adds enhanced observation, tracking, and profiling.

**The hunter's mindset.** The manual's central metaphor: the scout thinks like a hunter. A hunter studies his prey — its habits, its routes, its signs — and he studies the ground. He is patient, moves quietly, watches before he acts, and acts only when the moment is right. The methodology: understand the environment, find the sign, read the sign, predict the quarry's behavior, and position to observe or intercept. The incident timeline reconstructs events — what happened, when, in what order — from sign, observation, and the population.

**Traits of an effective scout.** Tactical patience (wait hours for minutes of observation); tactical cunning (deception, misdirection, using the enemy's expectations against him); deception (making the enemy see what you want him to see); and the Good Shepherd philosophy — the scout protects the population and the patrol, and the population protects the scout. Interlocking fields of observation and reporting: scouts position so observation overlaps, with no seams or gaps, and reporting overlaps too — two scouts reporting the same thing from different angles is confirmation, not redundancy.

## 7. Scouting in Support of Intelligence Collection

Scouting feeds the intelligence cycle. Collection activities include tactical questioning (asking the right questions of detainees and civilians, within legal and ethical bounds), site exploitation (searching a location systematically for documents, weapons, and intelligence — documenting everything before it is disturbed), and document and media exploitation (reading, photographing, and translating what is found). Identity activities — collecting identity attributes (biometrics, documents, photographs) and using that data — help identify who the enemy is and who he associates with. The scout's rule: report what you saw, not what you think it means; the analyst decides the meaning.

## 8. Scout Observation and Employment

**Understanding observation and perception.** Observation is the act of seeing; perception is the act of interpreting what is seen. The human eye has limits, and perception has biases — the scout must know both.

**The human eye.** Daylight vision is sharp and color-capable; night vision is coarse, monochrome, and slow to adapt (30 minutes to fully adapt to darkness; a single flash of white light destroys it). Off-center vision (looking slightly to the side of an object) works better at night, because the rod cells that detect low light are concentrated away from the center of the retina. The eye is drawn to movement, contrast, and things that break a pattern.

**Pattern perception.** The brain matches what it sees to templates and prototypes: it recognizes a human shape, a vehicle, a weapon by matching fragments to a stored pattern. This is why camouflage works — it breaks the pattern, so the brain does not complete the match — and why the scout must learn patterns, so recognition is fast. The eye and brain are built to detect movement, especially in peripheral vision, and biological motion (a walking human) is recognized almost instantly. Conversely, the eye adapts to a static scene and stops noticing it — which is why a scout must keep scanning rather than staring, and why an observer must be relieved regularly.

**Human information processing.** Visual and cognitive illusions are common: heat shimmer, mirages, seeing what you expect, missing what you are not looking for (inattentional blindness). Mitigation: use a systematic search pattern, two observers, optics, and verification — never report a single observation as fact without a second look or a second observer. Attention is limited; memory is fallible; learning is slow. The scout records observations as they happen (observation log), uses a standard reporting format, and sketches or photographs what he sees. Memory alone is not evidence.

**Observation methodology.** Principles: search systematically (hasty search for a quick scan of a wide area; detailed search for a slow, overlapping scan of a specific area), maintain observation (relieve observers, keep the log current, never leave a sector unwatched), and record and report. Why objects are seen: shape, shadow, silhouette, surface (shine), spacing, movement — the six factors that break camouflage. The scout looks for these in the enemy and eliminates them in himself. In limited visibility (night, fog, rain, smoke), use night vision and thermal optics when available, sound as a cue, off-center vision, and scan slowly, and accept slower, less certain observation.

**Recording and reporting.** The observation log records time, location, what was seen, and the observer. The reporting format is standard (SALUTE: Size, Activity, Location, Unit/Uniform, Time, Equipment). Sketches and cameras support the report. Report promptly: intelligence that arrives late is history, not intelligence.

## 9. Tracking

Tracking is following the trail — spoor — of a person, animal, or vehicle to find them. The tracking team is a team leader, a tracker, flank security, rear security, and cover (positioned to protect the tracker while he works). The ten common rules: know your quarry; move slowly; never lose the spoor; confirm every sign; work as a team; maintain security; record what you find; anticipate the quarry's actions; never track alone; and know when to stop (trail cold, or the tactical situation demands it).

Spoor is any sign of passage: footprints, broken vegetation, displaced earth, discarded material, scent. Reading spoor determines what made it, how many, how fast, how long ago, and in what direction. Age is assessed by weathering: sharpness of footprint edges, moisture of displaced earth, wilting of broken vegetation. Footprints reveal gait — stride length (speed), stride width (load), foot angle (direction, fatigue), depth (weight) — and numbers are assessed by direct count at a chokepoint, average pace over a measured distance, or comparison against a known track.

Processing the initial commencement point: secure the site, locate the spoor, record information (photograph, sketch, measure). Follow-up: draw conclusions and recommendations as you go, interpreting continuously, and move tactically — the tracker works low and slow, security covers him, the team never outruns its own security. Techniques: micro-tracking (individual footprints, slow and precise) and macro-tracking (general line from larger signs, faster but less certain). Formations: the "Y" (tracker at the point, flankers out, rear security behind), echelon "half-Y," column, and on-line; hand-and-arm signals keep the team silent. Lost spoor: the tracker alone searches in expanding spirals or boxes around the last known sign; if that fails, the whole team searches in a coordinated pattern. Anti-tracking is the reverse: increase the time-distance gap, use hard ground, water, and vegetation to leave less sign, brush out tracks, backtrack, and counter-track (follow your pursuers to ambush them). Urban tracking uses different sign (dust, scuffs, displaced objects); in limited visibility, use light sparingly — light reveals the tracker.

## 10. Scouting Within a Population

Operating among civilians is a different problem from operating in empty terrain. Profiling is the systematic observation of people to identify anomalies — individuals or behaviors that do not fit the baseline. Human responses to fear (fight, flight, freeze, or compliance) are predictable and observable. Heuristics — mental shortcuts — let a scout make fast decisions, but they must be disciplined: identify the anomaly, assess context and relevance, then decide.

The six domains of profiling: biometric cues (physical characteristics); kinesics (body movement and posture); proxemics (use of space and distance); geographics (where people are and go); iconography (symbols, clothing, markings); atmospherics (the mood of a place — tension, normalcy, fear). Threat indicators include smuggling behavior, unusually high situational awareness, mimicry and mirroring (copying others to blend in), and illustrators and emblems (gestures and symbols signaling affiliation). Suicide attack threats demand distance, barriers, observation, and control of access — plus awareness of insider threats and green-on-blue triggers.

Rapport is the scout's most important tool in a population: the components of rapport (trust, respect, shared interest), universal similarities (family, food, work, children), cultural considerations, and rapport-building behaviors (patience, listening, honesty, consistency). A scout who has rapport gets information; one who does not gets silence — or a warning to the enemy.

## 11. Fieldcraft

Fieldcraft is the individual skills that keep a scout alive and unseen: camouflage (breaking the outline, matching the background, eliminating shine and shadow), cover (protection from fire — solid objects that stop bullets) versus concealment (protection from observation — anything that hides you, even if it does not stop bullets), and individual movement (low crawl, high crawl, rush). Under limited visibility, sound discipline matters more than visual camouflage: move slowly, avoid noise, use sounds to navigate and detect the enemy, and plot threat positions by sound and touch. Range estimation: mental estimation (known sizes, appearance of objects at known distances), from a terrain study (map and imagery), and in good visibility (finger width, size of a man, bracketing). The manual's appendices carry the working tools: the patrol warning order, patrol order formats, debrief format, observation log, tracking forms, and fieldcraft checklists.

## Output Contract

When answering from this skill, give the patrol leader's answer: mission, route, objective, actions on contact, rally points, and the contingency plan — in that order. For any movement question, name the formation, control measures, and danger areas. For any observation question, give the reporting format (SALUTE) and the observation log requirement. For tracking, give the spoor-reading procedure and the lost-spoor drill. Never recommend a patrol without a warning order, rehearsal, rally points, and a COC coordination plan. Cite chapter and appendix (e.g., "MCTP 3-01A, Ch. 4 — actions on contact").

## Operating Rules

1. **The patrol is won or lost in planning and rehearsal.** Never skip the troop-leading steps for speed.
2. **Every Marine a collector.** Observe, remember, report — and write it down as it happens.
3. **All-around security, always.** Halts, patrol bases, and actions on the objective are when the enemy strikes.
4. **Avoid detection on reconnaissance; violence on contact.** A reconnaissance patrol that fights has usually failed; a combat patrol that hesitates has usually died.
5. **Rally points save patrols.** Every member must know every rally point and the actions at each.
6. **Passage of lines kills the careless.** Coordinate, use guides, never surprise a friendly position.
7. **If caught in an ambush, attack through it.** Freezing and running along the entry route are what the killing zone is for.
8. **Report what you saw, not what you think it means.** The analyst decides the meaning; the scout's job is accurate observation.
9. **In a population, rapport is the weapon.** The scout who has rapport gets information; the one who does not gets silence.
10. **Anti-tracking is tracking in reverse.** If you can read sign, you can also erase it — and you must know when the enemy is reading yours.
