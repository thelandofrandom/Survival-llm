## The tracking team

| Role | Job |
|---|---|
| Team leader | Controls the team, makes decisions, reports |
| Tracker | Reads the spoor; works low and slow at the point |
| Flank security | Covers the tracker's flanks |
| Rear security | Covers the rear |
| Cover | Positioned to protect the tracker while he works |

## Ten common rules for tracking

1. Know your quarry.
2. Move slowly.
3. Never lose the spoor.
4. Confirm every sign.
5. Work as a team.
6. Maintain security.
7. Record what you find.
8. Anticipate the quarry's actions.
9. Never track alone.
10. Know when to stop (trail cold, or tactical situation demands it).

## Spoor and sign

Spoor = any sign of passage: footprints, broken vegetation, displaced earth, discarded material, scent. Reading spoor determines: what made it, how many, how fast, how long ago, which direction.

**Aging spoor (weathering):** sharpness of footprint edges; moisture of displaced earth; wilting of broken vegetation; presence of insects or animals in the sign. Fresh sign is sharp, moist, and undisturbed; old sign is softened, dry, and disturbed by weather and animals.

**How tracks are seen:** look for disturbance, not for the track itself — displaced earth, crushed vegetation, shadow in a depression. Light matters: low-angle light (early morning, late afternoon) throws shadows that reveal tracks; overhead midday light hides them. Look from a low angle, and look ahead of the track, not down at your feet.

## Footprints and gait

| Indicator | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Stride length | Speed — longer stride = faster movement |
| Stride width | Load and balance — wider = heavier load or unstable ground |
| Foot angle | Direction and fatigue — splayed feet suggest fatigue or heavy load |
| Depth | Weight — deeper = heavier (load, large person, soft ground) |
| Disturbance at heel/toe | Acceleration (deep toe) or braking (deep heel) |

## Assessing the number of quarry

- **Direct count:** at a chokepoint where tracks are distinct, count individual sets.
- **Average pace:** count footprints in a measured distance and divide by the average pace of one person.
- **Comparison:** match a known individual's track against the group to identify a specific person in it.

## Processing the initial commencement point

1. Secure the site.
2. Locate the spoor.
3. Record information: photograph, sketch, measure.

## Conducting the follow-up

Make conclusions and recommendations as you go; interpret information continuously; move tactically — the tracker works low and slow, security covers him, the team never outruns its own security.

## Techniques and formations

- **Micro-tracking:** following individual footprints; slow, precise.
- **Macro-tracking:** following the general line using larger signs; faster, less certain.
- **"Y" formation:** tracker at the point, flankers out to the sides, rear security behind.
- **Echelon "half-Y":** one-sided variant for a known threat direction.
- **Column:** fast movement along a clear trail.
- **On line:** sweeping a wide area.
- Hand-and-arm signals keep the team silent.

## Lost spoor procedures

1. Tracker alone searches in expanding spirals or boxes around the last known sign.
2. If that fails, the whole team searches in a coordinated pattern.
3. If the trail is lost, mark the last known point, report, and do not wander — a lost tracking team becomes a lost patrol.

## Anti-tracking

- Increase the time-distance gap: move faster and farther than the pursuer can follow.
- Spoor reduction: use hard ground, rock, and water; avoid soft earth and vegetation; brush out tracks; walk in streams.
- Backtracking: walk backward over your own trail, then step off and move in a new direction.
- Counter-tracking: follow your own pursuers to ambush them.
- Urban tracking uses different sign: dust, scuffs, displaced objects, disturbed surfaces.
- Limited visibility: use light sparingly — a light reveals the tracker. Use sound and touch; move slowly.
