# Mountain Planning, Sustainment, and Survival

Distilled from MCRP 12-10A.3, Chapters 1–3, 9–10, and 12–16 (U.S. Marine Corps, Apr 2018).

## Intelligence in Mountainous Terrain (Chapter 1)

- The IPB process and cycle do not change in the mountains; the **data** that goes into it changes. Planning and the commander's direction adjust accordingly.
- Analyze terrain in three dimensions: elevation, slope, aspect, and the compartments ridges and valleys create. A valley is a corridor; a ridge is a wall; a saddle is a door.
- Build a **Modified Combined Obstacle Overlay (MCOO)** for mountain terrain — a standard overlay understates how much ground is impassable.
- Enemy mobility is constrained to the same corridors yours is — the corridors are the decisive terrain.
- **Geology:** rock type decides whether an anchor holds, whether a slope sheds rock, whether a position can be dug. Caves (soluble rock, lava flows) are shelter, storage, and ambush positions. Rockfall is a daily hazard.
- **Glaciology:** formation of glacial ice, glacier classifications and types, features impeding travel — crevasses, seracs, icefalls, moulins, bergschrunds.
- **Personnel:** altitude degrades judgment before it degrades the body. Acclimatization is measured in days. Cold, wind, and solar radiation combine at altitude in ways temperate experience does not predict.
- **Equipment and weapons:** cold thickens lubricants, drains batteries, makes metal brittle; thin air changes ballistics and engine performance.
- **Cliff reconnaissance:** procedures, operational security, cliff sketch, cliff report. A cliff that cannot be sketched accurately cannot be climbed on a plan.

## Command, Control, and Communications (Chapter 2)

- Plan each C2 node's role, required functionality, information exchange requirements, and displacement requirements. In mountains C2 displaces constantly — the plan says who moves, when, and to where, in advance.
- **Radios** are degraded by terrain masking; plan relay sites on high ground and accept periods out of contact.
- **Satellite comms** work where line of sight fails but need a clear sky view and are vulnerable to weather and jamming.
- **Field wire** is slow to lay in mountains and easy to cut.
- Build redundancy: no single system, no single path, no single node.

## Maneuver Planning (Chapter 3)

- **Small units:** a squad in a compartment is often on its own — resupply by rope or helicopter, fires limited by masking and rockfall danger, squad leader's decisions final.
- **Mounted operations:** vehicles are road-bound; mountain roads are predictable, narrow, easily blocked or ambushed.
- **Battalion maneuver:** a fight for corridors, passes, and high ground that observes the corridors. Massing is slow; time and distance are hours per kilometer.
- **Cold weather:** cold slows movement, increases sustainment demand, turns a delay into a casualty event. Plan cold into the timeline.

## Security (Chapter 9)

- High ground observes; compartments hide. Secure approaches that are few but dead ground extensive.
- 360-degree problem with a vertical component: the enemy above you is more dangerous than the enemy in front of you.
- Classic error: holding the high ground while blind to the valley that feeds it.

## Alpine Equipment and Movement (Chapter 10)

- **Rest step:** lock the knee, rest on the skeleton, breathe, step. Small technique, outsized payoff over a long climb.
- **Pressure breathing** keeps a climber moving at altitude.
- Load carriage is a discipline: every ounce is carried uphill; a unit that overloads its Marines has already lost the first engagement — with its own packs.

## Fires (Chapter 12)

- Masking blocks observation and line of sight; high-angle fire becomes the norm; near misses risk rockfall and avalanche.
- A fire mission that cannot be observed cannot be adjusted — and in the mountains, observation is the scarce resource.

## Logistics and Sustainment (Chapter 13)

- Resupply by foot, pack animal where they exist, rope, and helicopter — and the helicopter is weather-limited at altitude.
- Caches on the route; water at altitude is heavy and scarce.
- A mountain unit consumes more and carries less than a flat-ground unit. Sustainment planning starts with the route and the altitude, not the supply tables.

## Force Protection (Chapter 14)

- Protection against the environment as much as the enemy: rockfall, avalanche, lightning on exposed ridges, altitude, cold, fatigue.
- Route selection, weather discipline, rest discipline. A unit not protected from its environment cannot be protected from its enemy.

## Survival (Chapter 15)

- Priorities when separated from the unit: **shelter first, then water, then food, then signaling** — and the will to keep going.
- The mountain survival problem is usually a cold problem, a water problem, or an injury problem — usually all three at once.

## Training (Chapter 16)

- **Phase I** — individual skills.
- **Phase II** — environmental training: the unit in the mountains, learning the environment by living in it.
- **Phase III** — MEB/MEU mission rehearsal exercise: the full mission, in mountain terrain, under realistic conditions.
- Training that skips a phase produces a unit that knows the techniques and cannot apply them. Rope systems, glacier travel, and crevasse rescue are perishable skills.
