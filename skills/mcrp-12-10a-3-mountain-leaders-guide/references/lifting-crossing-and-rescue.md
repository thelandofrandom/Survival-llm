# Lifting, Crossing, Rappelling, and Rescue

Distilled from MCRP 12-10A.3, Chapters 5–8 and 11 (U.S. Marine Corps, Apr 2018).

## Mechanical Advantage Systems (Chapter 5)

- Ratios taught: **1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 9:1**. The ratio trades distance for force — a 3:1 lets one person lift three times the load by pulling three times the rope.
- Build from the anchor, through pulleys (or carabiners, which cost efficiency through friction), to the load.
- Plan for the **real** ratio, not the theoretical one: a 3:1 built with carabiners instead of pulleys is closer to 2:1 in practice.

## One-Rope Bridge

1. **Site selection:** narrow gap, solid anchors both sides, approach that does not expose the builders.
2. **Distance estimation** before construction.
3. **Organization:** bridge team with named roles — anchor team, far-side team, crossing team.
4. **Construction:** tension the rope — tight enough to cross, not so tight the anchor fails.
5. **Crossing:** one at a time, clipped in, moving steadily.
6. **Rescue:** techniques for a person stuck mid-span.
7. **Retrieval:** recover the bridge when the unit moves on.

## Gaining Height in Rope Installations

- **Natural** height (tree, rock), **quad-pod**, or **A-frame** — raise the rope so a load or casualty is hauled over an edge instead of dragged against it.
- A hauling system that drags its load over a rock edge will cut its own rope.

## Vertical Hauling System

- Site selection, construction, the **endless loop** for continuous hauling, the **vertical hauling line** for a single load.
- Hauling is slow, mechanical work — how heavy loads (weapons, ammunition, casualties) get up a cliff a unit has climbed light.

## Suspension Traverse

- Two-rope system for gaps a single rope cannot bridge. Site selection, construction, gaining height with an A-frame, tree, or quad-pod, construction of the belay line, operation, load considerations.
- The suspension traverse is the heavy answer; the one-rope bridge is the light one.

## Stream Crossing

- Site selection, safety precautions, individual methods, team methods, tactical crossing.
- A knee-deep mountain stream can knock a loaded Marine off his feet. Cross as a team, rope up, and never cross above a hazard (waterfall, strainer, deep pool) that turns a slip into a fatality.

## Rappelling (Chapter 6)

- Site selection; duties of the **rappel point NCO** (owns the anchor, rope, commands, and the go/no-go decision); types of rappels.
- **First person down** is the most exposed: anchor untested under load, route unknown, alone at the bottom.
- Rappelling commands, rescue techniques, retrievable rappels, equipment.

## Balance Climbing

- Individual preparations; duties of the **spotter** (the safety system on low-angle terrain); spotting and climbing commands; actions if falling.
- Holds: types, combination holds, general use; movement on slab; body position — weight over the feet, hips close to the rock, three points of contact.

## Top Roping

- Belay stance from the top and from the bottom; securing the climber; climber responsibilities; commands and signals. The rope comes from above, so a fall is short — the training and practice system.

## Placing Protection

- **Natural:** threads, horns, chockstones. **Artificial:** nuts, cams, pitons.
- Assess fixed protection left by others — never trust it blindly. Use the nut tool; rack equipment in the order it will be needed.
- Place protection often and well; never climb far above your last piece.

## Belaying for Party Climbing

- Types of belay anchors; elements of a belay chain; belayer's responsibilities; establishing the belay; methods of belaying a fall; disassembly; hanging belay stance; alternate belays.
- The belayer is the climber's life: attentive, anchored, never distracted.

## Party Climbing

- Climbing classification; route selection; lead climber responsibilities; rules for placing protection; attaching rope to protection.
- **Prevent rope drag** and **prevent the zipper effect** — a redirect that turns one failed piece into a cascade.
- Lead climber's actions on top; retrieving protection; fall forces; night climbing and silencing gear; three-person team techniques; Class 1–4 climbing.

## Rescue Climbing (Chapter 7)

- **Assess first** — a rushed rescue makes two casualties.
- Rescuing an injured lead climber and rescuing an injured number-two climber are different problems with different systems; know both.
- **Military aid climbing:** when rock is too hard, loose, or exposed to free climb — use equipment to make upward progress. Slow and deliberate.

## Steep Climbing (Chapter 8)

- **Steep earth climbing:** procedures on steep soil and scree; using the **grapnel** — a thrown hook giving a handhold or anchor where the ground offers none.
- Steep earth looks climbable and fails under load: test every hold, move one limb at a time, keep the rope on.

## Glacier Travel and Rescue (Chapter 11)

- Travel as a roped party with spacing, probing, and discipline.
- **Crevasse rescue:** fall arrested by the rope team → victim secured → anchor built → victim hauled out with a mechanical advantage system.
- Practice cold, tired, and in the dark — crevasse falls happen that way.
- The snow bridge hiding a crevasse is the glacier's ambush: probe, rope up, never travel a glacier unroped.
