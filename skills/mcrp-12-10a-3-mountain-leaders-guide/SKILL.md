# Mountain Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare Operations

## Purpose

MCRP 12-10A.3, *Mountain Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare Operations* (U.S. Marine Corps, 4 April 2018, 420 pages; formerly MCRP 3-35.1C), is the mountain leader's working reference for fighting in steep, high, compartmented terrain. It is the companion to the winter volume (MCRP 12-10A.2) and to MCTP 12-10A (*Mountain Warfare*): where the winter guide covers snow and ice, this volume covers the mountain itself — the rock, the rope, the glacier, and the planning that lets a unit move, shoot, communicate, and survive where the ground is the enemy's best ally and yours only if you understand it.

The manual's central claim, carried from the World War II *Gebirgsjäger* through Afghanistan, is that nothing in the intelligence process changes in the mountains — what changes is the data that goes into it. Commanders who do a thorough intelligence preparation of the battlespace (IPB) in mountainous terrain win; commanders who treat mountains as flat ground with extra elevation lose. Mountains are found on every continent and mostly near the coast; the principal ranges run along broad belts called cordilleras that encircle the Pacific and lead westward across Eurasia into Africa.

## 1. Intelligence in Mountainous Terrain

**The mountain IPB.** Analyze the area of operations to learn the challenges that are distinct from less rugged environments and how those characteristics affect personnel, equipment, and the enemy. The IPB process and cycle do not change; the planning based on the data, and the commander's direction, adjust accordingly. The results let the commander selectively apply and maximize combat power at critical points in time and space by determining the enemy's course of action and describing the environment and its effects.

**Physical terrain and mountain terrain.** Study the terrain in three dimensions, not two: elevation, slope, aspect, and the compartments the ridges and valleys create. A valley is a corridor; a ridge is a wall; a saddle is a door. The Modified Combined Obstacle Overlay (MCOO) is built for mountain terrain specifically, because a standard overlay understates how much ground is actually impassable. Enemy mobility is constrained to the same corridors yours is — which makes the corridors the decisive terrain.

**Geology.** Rock type decides whether an anchor will hold, whether a slope will shed rock, and whether a position can be dug. Learn the local rock: its hardness, its fracture pattern, its weathering. Formation mechanics explain why ranges look the way they do; plains and plateaus inside mountain regions are the exceptions that become airfields, assembly areas, and killing grounds. Caves form in soluble rock and in lava flows — they are shelter, storage, and ambush positions. Weathering and erosion are continuous: rockfall is a daily hazard, not a rare event, and the scree at the base of a slope tells you what the slope above is shedding.

**Glaciology.** Glaciers are moving rivers of ice. Learn how glacial ice forms, the classifications and types of glaciers, and the features that impede travel — crevasses, seracs, icefalls, moulins, and bergschrunds. A glacier that looks flat and walkable can hide a crevasse under a snow bridge a few inches thick. Glacier knowledge is a prerequisite for Chapter 11, not an ornament.

**Environmental effects on personnel.** Altitude is the silent casualty producer: reduced oxygen degrades judgment before it degrades the body, and a leader who does not recognize altitude effects in himself will not recognize them in his Marines. Cold, wind, and solar radiation combine at altitude in ways temperate experience does not predict. Acclimatization is measured in days, not hours, and the unit that rushes to altitude pays for it in casualties and bad decisions.

**Environmental effects on equipment and weapons.** Cold thickens lubricants, drains batteries, and makes metal brittle. Thin air changes ballistics and engine performance. Dust and rock abrade everything. Test equipment at altitude before depending on it.

**Cliff reconnaissance.** Before any cliff operation, recon the cliff: procedures, operational security (a cliff recon that is observed gives away the operation), the cliff sketch, and the cliff report. The sketch and report are the products the commander decides from — a cliff that cannot be sketched accurately cannot be climbed on a plan.

## 2. Command, Control, and Communications Planning

Mountains break communications the way they break movement: line of sight is short, dead ground is everywhere, and a radio that works in the valley is silent on the ridge.

**C2 nodes.** Plan the roles of each command and control node, what functionality each must have, the information exchange requirements between them, and their displacement requirements — because a node that cannot move when the fight moves is a node that gets bypassed or destroyed. In mountains, C2 displaces constantly; the plan must say who moves, when, and to where, in advance.

**Communications systems.** Radio systems are degraded by terrain masking; plan relay sites on high ground and accept that some positions will be out of contact for periods. Satellite communications work where line of sight fails but need a clear view of the sky and are vulnerable to weather and jamming. Telephone systems (field wire) are slow to lay in mountains and easy to cut. External connectivity — the link back to higher headquarters — is a planned capability with a named owner, not an assumption. Build the communications plan around redundancy: no single system, no single path, no single node.

## 3. Maneuver Planning Considerations

**Small-unit considerations.** Sustainment, force protection, fires, and command and control all change at small-unit scale in the mountains, because a squad in a compartment is often on its own: resupply is a rope or a helicopter, fires are limited by masking and by the danger of rockfall from near misses, and the squad leader's decisions are final in a way they are not on flat ground.

**Mounted operations.** Vehicles in mountains are road-bound, and mountain roads are predictable, narrow, and easily blocked or ambushed. Plan mounted movement as a vulnerability to be managed, not a capability to be assumed.

**Battalion-level maneuver.** At battalion scale the mountain fight is a fight for corridors, passes, and high ground that observes the corridors. Massing is slow; the battalion that tries to mass quickly in the mountains arrives strung out and exhausted. Time and distance are measured in hours per kilometer, not minutes.

**Cold-weather considerations.** Cold is a maneuver factor: it slows movement, increases sustainment demand, and turns a delay into a casualty event. Plan cold into the maneuver timeline.

## 4. Rope Management, Knots, and Anchors

This is the technical heart of the manual, and the skill that everything else stands on. A rope system is only as strong as its weakest element, and the weakest element is usually the anchor or the knot.

**Rope care and inspection.** Inspect rope before every use: cuts, abrasion, glazing from heat, soft spots, contamination with chemicals or grit. Keep a rope log — serial number, history of use, falls taken, inspections — and retire rope on condition, not on a calendar. A rope that has held a factor-2 fall is a rope that has done its job once and may not do it again.

**Coiling.** The mountain coil and the butterfly coil are the two standard methods; each has its place — the mountain coil for carrying, the butterfly coil for rapid deployment. A badly coiled rope is a tangled rope, and a tangled rope at the top of a cliff is a mission failure.

**Mountaineering knots, in four classes:**
- *Class I — end-of-the-rope knots:* stopper knots that keep the rope from running through a device.
- *Class II — anchor knots:* the knots that tie the rope to the anchor — figure-eight on a bight, bowline, and their variants. These are the knots a life hangs from; tie them correctly, dress them, and back them up.
- *Class III — middle-of-the-rope knots:* butterfly, alpine butterfly, and other mid-rope knots for clipping in the middle of a party or building an anchor.
- *Class IV — special knots:* Prusik, Klemheist, and other friction hitches for ascending, descending, and hauling, plus the knots used in rescue systems.
- *Knot strength and testing:* every knot weakens a rope; know the approximate strength loss for the knots you use, and test knots to the manual's standards. A knot that is not dressed and set is not a finished knot.

**Anchors.** Natural anchors (trees, rock horns, boulders, chockstones) are preferred where they exist — they are strong, simple, and fast. Artificial anchors (pitons, nuts, cams, bolts) are placed where nature gives nothing. Anchors are unidirectional (loaded in one direction only) or multidirectional (loaded from several directions), and the system must match the load: a belay anchor that will be loaded downward and outward is not the same as a rappel anchor loaded in one line.

**Anchor systems.** Single-point construction is fast and simple and has a single point of failure; multi-point construction distributes the load and provides redundancy. The manual teaches the standard systems: the in-line two-point anchor, the picket hold fast (for snow and soft ground), the deadman (a buried anchor for snow), the equalized rope anchor, the equalized cordelette anchor, the web-o-lette method, the American triangle, and the self-equalizing cordelette anchor. Each is a different answer to the same question: how do I make this anchor hold, and what happens if one piece fails?

**Principles for evaluating an anchor system.** The acronym-taught principles boil down to: strong (each piece solid), redundant (no single point of failure), equalized (load shared), and no extension (if one piece fails, the system does not shock-load the rest). Evaluate every anchor against all four before anyone commits weight to it.

**Carabiners, slings, and connecting the rope.** Use locking carabiners at the anchor and anywhere a failure would be catastrophic; keep gates closed and locked; keep the rope off sharp edges with padding or by rerouting. Protecting the equipment is protecting the climber.

## 5. Systems for Lifting and Crossing

**Mechanical advantage systems.** The manual teaches the 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, and 9:1 hauling systems — the ratio is the trade of distance for force: a 3:1 system lets one person lift three times the load by pulling three times the rope. Build them from the anchor, through the pulleys (or carabiners, which cost efficiency through friction), to the load. Know the efficiency losses: a 3:1 built with carabiners instead of pulleys is closer to 2:1 in practice, and the leader plans for the real number, not the theoretical one.

**One-rope bridge.** Site selection (a narrow gap, solid anchors both sides, an approach that does not expose the builders), distance estimation, organization (a bridge team with named roles: anchor team, far-side team, crossing team), construction (tensioning the rope — tight enough to cross, not so tight the anchor fails), crossing (one at a time, clipped in, moving steadily), rescue techniques for a person stuck mid-span, and retrieval of the bridge when the unit moves on. A one-rope bridge is the mountain unit's way of turning an obstacle into a route.

**Gaining height in rope installations.** Natural height (a tree, a rock), a quad-pod, or an A-frame — each is a way to raise the rope so a load or a casualty can be hauled over an edge instead of dragged against it. The A-frame and quad-pod are built from poles and lashings; know them cold, because a hauling system that drags its load over a rock edge will cut its own rope.

**Vertical hauling system.** Site selection, construction, the endless loop for continuous hauling, and the vertical hauling line for a single load. Hauling is slow, mechanical work — the opposite of climbing — and it is how heavy loads (weapons, ammunition, casualties) get up a cliff a unit has climbed light.

**Suspension traverse.** A two-rope system for moving people and loads across a gap that cannot be bridged by a single rope: site selection, construction, using an A-frame, a tree, or a quad-pod to gain height, construction of the belay line, operation, and general load considerations. The suspension traverse is the heavy answer to the crossing problem; the one-rope bridge is the light one.

**Stream crossing.** Site selection, safety precautions, individual crossing preparations and methods, team crossing methods, and tactical stream crossing. A mountain stream is cold, fast, and deceptive — a knee-deep crossing can knock a loaded Marine off his feet. Cross as a team, rope up, and never cross above a hazard (a waterfall, a strainer, a deep pool) that turns a slip into a fatality.

## 6. Rappelling and Climbing

**Rappelling.** Site selection, the duties of the rappel point NCO, types of rappels, safety of the first person down, duties of the first person down, rappelling commands, rescue techniques, retrievable rappels, and equipment. The first person down is the most exposed: the anchor is untested under load, the route is unknown, and the person at the bottom is alone. The rappel point NCO owns the system — anchor, rope, commands, and the decision to go or not go.

**Balance climbing.** Individual preparations, the duties of the spotter, spotting and climbing commands, actions if falling, types of holds and combination holds, general use of holds, movement on slab, and body position. Balance climbing is climbing without a rope on low-angle terrain, where the spotter is the safety system. The climber's body position — weight over the feet, hips close to the rock, three points of contact — is the whole technique.

**Top roping.** Establishing a belay stance from the top and from the bottom, securing the climber to the rope, climber responsibilities, and climbing commands and signals. Top roping is the training and practice system: the rope comes from above, so a fall is short and the system is forgiving.

**Placing protection.** Natural protection (threads, horns, chockstones) and artificial protection (nuts, cams, pitons), assessing fixed protection left by others (never trust it blindly), using the nut tool, and racking climbing equipment so each piece is reachable in the order it will be needed. Protection is placed to catch a fall, not to hold a climb — place it often, place it well, and never climb far above your last piece.

**Belaying for party climbing.** Types of belay anchors, elements of a belay chain, the belayer's responsibilities, establishing the belay, methods of belaying a fall, disassembly of the belay stance, the hanging belay stance, special considerations, and alternate belays. The belayer is the climber's life: attentive, anchored, and never distracted.

**Party climbing.** Climbing classification, route site selection, responsibilities of the lead climber, rules for placing protection, attaching the rope to the protection, preventing rope drag, preventing the zipper effect (a redirect that turns one failed piece into a cascade), climbing commands, the lead climber's actions on top, retrieving the protection, fall forces, night climbing and silencing gear, three-person climbing team techniques, and Class 1 through Class 4 climbing. The lead climber's job is to climb, place protection, and build the next belay — and to make the decisions that keep the second climber alive.

## 7. Rescue Climbing

**Rescues for party climbing.** Assess the situation first — a rushed rescue makes two casualties. Rescuing an injured lead climber and rescuing an injured number-two climber are different problems with different systems; know both before you need either.

**Military aid climbing.** Reasons to use military aid (the rock is too hard, too loose, or too exposed to free climb), the gear, and the aid techniques — using equipment to make upward progress rather than relying on holds. Aid climbing is slow and deliberate; it is the answer when the cliff cannot be climbed any other way and the mission requires going up.

## 8. Steep Climbing

**Steep earth climbing.** Climbing procedures on steep soil and scree, and using the grapnel — a thrown hook that gives a handhold or an anchor where the ground offers none. Steep earth is treacherous because it looks climbable and fails under load: test every hold, move one limb at a time, and keep the rope on.

## 9. Security in a Mountainous Environment

Mountains give security and take it away. High ground observes; compartments hide. The chapter covers the security problem in full: how to secure a position whose approaches are few but whose dead ground is extensive, how to use the terrain's natural chokepoints, and how to avoid the classic mountain error — holding the high ground and being blind to the valley that feeds it. Security in the mountains is a 360-degree problem with a vertical component: the enemy above you is more dangerous than the enemy in front of you.

## 10. Alpine Equipment and Movement Techniques

The equipment chapter: what to carry, how to carry it, and how to move in it. Alpine movement techniques — the walk, the rest step, the pressure breathing that keeps a climber moving at altitude — are the difference between a unit that arrives fit to fight and a unit that arrives exhausted. The rest step (lock the knee, rest on the skeleton, breathe, step) is a small technique with an outsized payoff over a long climb. Load carriage in the mountains is a discipline: every ounce is carried uphill, and the unit that overloads its Marines has already lost the first engagement, with its own packs.

## 11. Glacier Travel and Rescue

Travel on a glacier as a roped party, with the spacing, the probing, and the discipline the terrain demands. Crevasse rescue is a system: the fall is arrested by the rope team, the victim is secured, an anchor is built, and the victim is hauled out with a mechanical advantage system. Practice it until the team can do it cold, tired, and in the dark — because crevasse falls happen cold, tired, and in the dark. The snow bridge that hides a crevasse is the glacier's ambush; probe, rope up, and never travel a glacier unroped.

## 12. Fires in a Mountainous Environment

Fires are degraded and complicated by mountains: masking blocks observation and line of sight, high-angle fire becomes the norm, and the danger of rockfall and avalanche from near misses is real. The chapter covers how to plan fires that can actually reach the target, how to observe them from positions that can see, and how to account for the altitude and temperature effects on ballistics. A fire mission that cannot be observed is a fire mission that cannot be adjusted — and in the mountains, observation is the scarce resource.

## 13. Logistics and Sustainment

Mountains are a logistics problem before they are a tactics problem. Resupply is by foot, by pack animal where they exist, by rope, and by helicopter — and the helicopter is weather-limited at altitude. The chapter covers what to stock, where to stock it, and how to move it: caches on the route, water at altitude (which is heavy and scarce), and the hard truth that a mountain unit consumes more and carries less than a flat-ground unit. Sustainment planning starts with the route and the altitude, not with the supply tables.

## 14. Force Protection

Force protection in the mountains is protection against the environment as much as against the enemy: rockfall, avalanche, lightning on exposed ridges, altitude, cold, and the fatigue that makes all of them more likely. The chapter covers the protective measures that keep a unit alive when the mountain itself is the threat — route selection, weather discipline, rest discipline, and the recognition that a unit that is not protected from its environment cannot be protected from its enemy either.

## 15. Survival

Mountain survival is the distillation of the whole manual into the individual: shelter, water, food, fire, signaling, and the will to keep going. The chapter covers what to do when separated from the unit, how to prioritize (shelter first, then water, then food, then signaling), and how to make the decisions that keep a person alive long enough to be found. The mountain survival problem is usually a cold problem, a water problem, or an injury problem — and usually all three at once.

## 16. Training

Training is phased: Phase I is individual skills, Phase II is environmental training (the unit in the mountains, learning the environment by living in it), and Phase III is the MEB/MEU mission rehearsal exercise — the full mission, in mountain terrain, under realistic conditions. Training that skips a phase produces a unit that knows the techniques and cannot apply them. The manual's training chapter is the argument for doing the hard training in the mountains rather than reading about it: rope systems, glacier travel, and crevasse rescue are perishable skills that degrade without practice.

## Output Contract

When asked about mountain warfare operations, answer from this doctrine: lead with the terrain analysis (what the ground allows and forbids), then the system (anchor, rope, hauling, crossing), then the numbers (ratios, spacing, load limits). Rope work is exact: a wrong knot class, a mis-evaluated anchor, or a misunderstood mechanical advantage ratio is a fatal error. Distinguish doctrine (the anchor evaluation principles, the knot classes, the rescue sequences) from judgment (which route, which anchor system for this rock, whether to climb or go around).

## Operating Rules

1. **The IPB is the operation.** In the mountains, the commander who understands the terrain wins; the one who does not is ambushed by it.
2. **No single point of failure.** Every anchor system is strong, redundant, equalized, and non-extending — or it is not finished.
3. **A knot that is not dressed and set is not a finished knot.** Tie it, dress it, set it, back it up.
4. **Plan for the real mechanical advantage, not the theoretical one.** Carabiners cost efficiency; friction is real.
5. **Never travel a glacier unroped.** The crevasse you cannot see is the one that kills you.
6. **The first person down is the most exposed.** The rappel point NCO owns the system and the decision.
7. **Train in the mountains.** Rope systems and crevasse rescue are perishable skills.
