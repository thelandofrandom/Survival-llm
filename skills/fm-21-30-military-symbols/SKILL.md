# FM 21-30 Conventional Signs, Military Symbols, and Abbreviations

## Purpose

FM 21-30, *Conventional Signs, Military Symbols, and Abbreviations* (War Department, 26 November 1941, 70 pages) — the Basic Field Manual that standardized how the U.S. Army of 1941 drew its maps, marked its units, and abbreviated its orders. It was prepared under the direction of the Chief of Engineers and published for the information and guidance of all concerned. It supersedes FM 21-30 of 1 August 1939 and Training Circular No. 48, War Department, 1941.

The manual's purpose is stated in paragraph 1: to supply the information required by the map reader. Persons preparing maps were to be guided by AR 300-15, TM 5-230, and Bulletin 788, *Topographical Instructions of the United States Geological Survey*. The manual is organized in six sections plus an appendix of references: general rules (Section I), general topographic symbols (Section II), symbols for air navigation maps (Section III), military symbols (Section IV), general abbreviations (Section V), and military abbreviations (Section VI).

**Historic — read this first.** This is a 1941 symbol system. Modern U.S. military symbology is fundamentally different: current doctrine (FM 1-02.2 / ATP 1-02.2 and the NATO APP-6 standard it aligns with) uses the framed rectangle/diamond unit symbol with a completely different set of echelon ticks, branch insignia, and color conventions (friendly blue, hostile red, neutral green, unknown yellow — the 1941 blue/red split survives in spirit, but the symbols themselves do not). Do not use FM 21-30 symbols on a modern map, overlay, or order and expect them to be read correctly. Its value today is threefold: it lets you read WWII-era American maps, overlays, and operations orders as their authors intended; it explains the logic — color, placement, and combination — from which modern symbology descends; and its abbreviation lists decode the shorthand of wartime documents. Where a 1941 symbol and a modern one conflict, the modern standard governs.

**A note on the extracted text.** The symbols in this manual are drawn graphics — lines, rectangles, flags, and pictographs. Plain-text extraction (pdftotext) cannot reproduce them: in `references/full-text.md` most symbol entries appear as a label followed by a garbled fragment of the drawing, or by nothing at all. This skill therefore distills the *rules* (color, placement, combination) and the *conventions* (what each class of symbol denotes, in words) and the abbreviation lists, which do survive in text. It does not attempt to redraw the symbols. To see a symbol exactly as drawn, consult the PDF itself via the link at the foot of this file.

## 1. Scope and Authority (Section I)

**1.1. What is authorized.** The manual shows in convenient form the abbreviations and general symbols authorized for military use. Three groups carry Federal Board of Maps and Surveys approval and are prescribed for use on all United States maps: the general topographic symbols of Section II, the air-navigation symbols of Section III, and the general abbreviations of Section V. The purely military symbols of Section IV and the special military abbreviations of Section VI are prescribed by the War Department.

**1.2. Unauthorized symbols.** Symbols other than those authorized may be used, provided they do not conflict with any authorized symbol and are explained in a suitable legend on the map. This is the manual's standing escape clause: a mapmaker may invent, but must not collide, and must explain.

## 2. General Topographic Symbols (Section II)

**2.1. Size.** In general, symbols resemble the objects they represent. They vary in size with the scale of the map. On small-scale maps they are reduced to their most elementary form and do not conform to scale; as scale increases, symbols assume the shape and scaled size of the object represented.

**2.2. Color.** The symbols are shown in appropriate color for a four-color map. When fewer than four colors are used, symbols shown in the colors not used are executed in black ink.

**2.3. Lettering.** Lettering type carries meaning on 1941 maps: vertical Roman type indicates civil divisions; slant Roman (italic) indicates natural water features; vertical Gothic indicates natural land features; slant Gothic indicates works of man. The manual concedes that lettering on hastily prepared military maps and sketches may vary considerably from these types, and that marginal data will not be nearly as complete on military sketches as on finished maps.

**2.4. What the list covers.** Paragraph 4 lists, in order: works and structures (canals, ditches, aqueducts, locks); roads, classified by surface — good motor, poor motor or private, hard impervious surfaces, other surface improvements, good and poor pack trails and footpaths — with U.S. and state route markers; railroads (any kind on small-scale maps; single track, double track, narrow gauge, abandoned, electric passenger-only on large-scale maps); boundaries and survey marks; hydrography (streams, lakes, springs, wells); vegetation (woods, brush, cultivated land, orchards); and relief (contours, depressions, cliffs). The governing idea is resemblance: a symbol should look like the thing it stands for, simplified as scale demands. → `references/topographic-symbols.md`

## 3. Symbols for Air Navigation Maps (Section III)

**3.1. Purpose.** Section III gives the symbols intended for air navigation maps only — a separate list from the general topographic symbols, because a pilot reading a chart at speed needs a different selection and emphasis than a ground map reader.

**3.2. Contents.** The section covers airfields and landing grounds (including advanced landing fields), airdromes, airship hangars and mooring masts, radio and navigational aids, and the obstruction and hazard markings a pilot needs. As with Section II, the symbols are pictographic and scale-dependent; the text list names each feature and the drawing shows it. Consult the PDF for the drawn forms. → `references/topographic-symbols.md`

## 4. Military Symbols — General Rules (Section IV, ¶7)

These are the rules that make the whole system work. Learn them before any individual symbol, because they govern how symbols combine.

**4.1. Color — friend blue, enemy red.** When colors are used, military symbols representing objects in territory occupied or controlled by our forces are shown in **blue**, and those in territory occupied or controlled by the enemy are shown in **red**. Two exceptions: (1) symbols for areas to be covered by friendly fire or gas, although generally located in enemy territory, are executed in blue; (2) symbols for gassed areas, wherever located, are always executed in red.

**4.2. Unit designations — position is meaning.** The position of letters, numbers, or abbreviations relative to the symbol is fixed:

- Serial numbers of **armies and corps** go *within* the symbol for troop units when considered collectively. Army is shown by number spelled out; corps by Roman numerals.
- Serial numbers of **divisions, brigades, regiments, separate battalions, separate companies**, or similar separate units go on the *right*, in Arabic numerals, followed by an abbreviated explanation of the size of the unit.
- Numbers designating **battalions in a regiment** go on the *left*, in Arabic numerals.
- Letters designating **companies, troops, or batteries** are capitals, shown on the *left*. Companies, troops, batteries, or platoons (not separate) designated by name instead of by letter — for example, "Antitank Company" or "Headquarters and Service Troop" — are designated by the proper abbreviation on the left.
- Abbreviations for explanatory purposes go on the *right* (see Section VI). Abbreviations are used instead of symbols to show information that cannot be indicated by a single symbol.
- **Calibers** are shown by numbers and unit of measure, as 37-mm, 155-mm, 12-in., .50-in., usually placed *under* the symbol; often the unit of measure is omitted.

**4.3. Echelon marks.** The size of a unit is shown by marks placed above the basic symbol (the familiar tick system that survives, much changed, in modern symbology):

| Echelon | Mark above symbol |
|---|---|
| Squad | (single short tick) |
| Section | (two ticks) |
| Platoon | (three ticks) |
| Company, troop, battery, or Air Corps flight | I |
| Battalion, cavalry squadron, or Air Corps squadron | II |
| Regiment or group | III |
| Brigade or Air Corps wing | X |
| Division or air force | XX |
| Corps | XXX |
| Army | XXXX |
| Corps area, department, or section of communications zone | OOO |
| Communications zone | OOOO |
| General Headquarters | GHQ |

The same marks are used on boundary and line symbols (front line, main line of resistance, regimental reserve line, outpost line, line of departure, line of communication, straggler line), where the echelon ticks identify the echelon the line belongs to. A soldier is shown by a small circle; an automatic rifleman, assistant leader, and leader by variations on it; a squad leader by a filled dot.

**4.4. The basic symbol list (¶8).** Paragraph 8 lists the basic symbols in six groups:

- **(a) Purpose or character of activity:** military post or station, command post or headquarters (the staff terminates at the location of the establishment), troop unit, airdrome, airship hangar and mooring mast, airport and advanced landing field, autogiro, ammunition point, arsenal (including gas-generating), balloon ascension point and balloon bed, barrage balloon ascension point, barrage, demolitions, depot/supply point (including temporary depot in the combat zone), debarkation or embarkation point, dugout (isolated, in trench system, gas-proof), entanglement (wire, concealed), gas areas (area to be avoided, area blanketed by smoke with time effective, area probably affected by gas cylinder cloud, area to be gassed nonpersistent), general hospital, laboratory/experimental station/proving ground, leader gear (energized cable aiding pilotage through mine fields), message center, mines (individual, chemical land mine, contact mines with the number of mines in each line, controlled mines in 19-mine groups).
- **(b) Arms and services:** the branch symbols placed inside the unit rectangle — infantry, cavalry, field artillery, coast artillery, engineers, signal, medical, quartermaster, ordnance, chemical warfare, air corps, armored force, military police, and others. The branch symbol inside the frame is what tells you *what* the unit is; the echelon ticks above tell you *how big* it is.
- **(c) Size indicators:** the echelon marks of §4.3.
- **(d) Boundaries and lines:** bombardment aviation limits of radius of action, reconnaissance aviation limits of zone of reconnaissance (army, corps, air force, observation), pursuit aviation limits, front line, main line of resistance, regimental reserve line, outpost line, line of departure, line of communication, straggler line, prisoner-of-war enclosure, limit of wheeled traffic by day and by night, line beyond which lights on vehicles are prohibited, limiting point.
- **(e) Weapons:** automatic rifle (dotted when the emplacement is not occupied), machine gun (arrow points in the principal direction of fire; used alone it indicates a water-cooled .30-caliber machine gun; antiaircraft and antitank variants, single gun, section of two guns with sectors of fire and final protective line shading), gun, gun battery, howitzer or mortar (open when the emplacement is unoccupied), 4.2-inch chemical mortar, Livens projector, chemical land mine.
- **(f) Special symbols for hasty sketches and operations maps:** simplified area-occupied symbols (e.g., 2d Battalion, 3d Field Artillery), corps troops areas, brush, cultivated land, stream, woods — plus a suggested road classification (hard surface; graded and improved; graded, not improved; poor road; trail) to be used when additional types need improvised notation or a legend.

**4.5. Application — combining symbols (¶9).** Paragraph 9 gives worked examples showing how basic symbols and abbreviations combine into complete unit designations: Air Corps examples (3d Air Force; 701st Air Base Group; 901st Transport Squadron; 7th Observation Squadron; 88th Reconnaissance Squadron; 101st Balloon Squadron; 301st Balloon Group; 701st Bombardment Wing, Light; 532d Pursuit Group, Interceptor; Second Army Aviation; 203d School Squadron), Armored Force examples (Headquarters Company, 1st Armored Division; Military Police Platoon, Headquarters Company, 1st Armored Division; Transportation Platoon; 1st Reconnaissance Battalion; Rifle Company and Weapons Platoon of the 1st Reconnaissance Battalion), and corresponding examples for infantry, artillery, engineers, and services. The manual stresses that these are only a few of the possible combinations, many more complete than necessary — often the number of a unit, to those familiar with it, will indicate its activity. → `references/military-symbols.md`

## 5. General Abbreviations (Section V, ¶¶10–11)

**5.1. General use (¶10).** Section V lists general abbreviations approved for use on maps — the shorthand of place names, geographic features, and map marginalia (e.g., Sec. for Section, and the hydrographic abbreviations of ¶11 for use with water symbols). These are the abbreviations a map reader meets in the terrain itself, as distinct from the military abbreviations of Section VI, which belong in orders and reports.

**5.2. Hydrographic use (¶11).** Paragraph 11 gives the abbreviations used with hydrographic symbols — the compact letter codes for water features and soundings. → `references/abbreviations.md`

## 6. Military Abbreviations (Section VI, ¶¶12–13)

**6.1. The rules (¶12).** The abbreviations in Section VI are authorized for use in combat orders, messages, records, and reports in the field. With few exceptions all are written without spacing and periods. Dates, when abbreviated, are written thus: **1 Jan 24**. Usually a single word is represented by a single capital letter, or by a capital and a lower-case letter. Staff sections follow the system still recognizable today: **G-1** (personnel), **G-2** (military intelligence), **G-3** (operations and training), **G-4** (supply) at general-staff level, and **S-1** through **S-4** at brigade level and below (battalion/brigade adjutant, intelligence officer, plans and training officer, supply officer).

**6.2. Representative abbreviations.** The list runs from Acting (Actg) to the end of the alphabet, several hundred entries. A working sample — enough to decode most 1941 orders:

- Command and staff: AG (Adjutant General), AGD (Adjutant General's Department), ADC (aide-de-camp), AC of S (assistant chief of staff), AHQ (army headquarters), BHQ (brigade headquarters), Comdr (commander), Atchd (attached), Asst (assistant).
- Arms and branches: AC (Air Corps), AAF (Army Air Forces), AF (Air Force), AFCC (Air Force Combat Command), ArmdF (Armored Force), Arty (artillery), Cav (cavalry), Cml (chemical), AAA (antiaircraft artillery), AAIS/AAAIS (antiaircraft intelligence service), AWS (aircraft warning service), BAR (Browning automatic rifle).
- Units and formations: Bn (battalion), Btry (battery), Brig (brigade), Co (company), Sq (squadron), Gp (group), Wg (wing), Regt (regiment), Div (division), Sec (section), Plt/Plat (platoon, in context), Det (detachment), Tn (train).
- Movement and combat: Adv (advance), AdvGd (advance guard), Atk (attack), Bd (boundary), BrTn (bridge train), Bkry (bakery), Amb (ambulance), ALP (ambulance loading post), Am (ammunition), DP (distribution point), APO (Army Post Office), AEF (American Expeditionary Forces).
- Time and routine: PM (afternoon, 12:00 noon to 12:00 midnight), Apr/Aug (months), App (appendix), Bsc (basic), BM (bench mark), Cem (cemetery), Cen (center), cm (centimeter), cal (caliber).
- Weapons and equipment: AT (antitank), AMecz (antimechanized), Auto (automatic), Avn (aviation), Ax Sig Com (axis of signal communication), Bag (baggage), Bin (balloon), Bomb (bombardment), BSTrk (bomb service truck), BrTn (Hv/L) (bridge train, heavy/light), Cam (camouflage), Capt (captain), Carr (carrier), Cptr (carpenter), Cfr (chauffeur), Ch (chaplain).

**6.3. Worked examples (¶13).** Paragraph 13 gives examples of the abbreviations in use in orders and messages, showing how they combine with the symbols of Section IV — e.g., a unit designation built from number + branch abbreviation + echelon mark, and dates in the 1 Jan 24 form. When decoding a wartime order, read symbol and abbreviation together: the symbol says what and where, the abbreviation says which unit and what it is doing. → `references/abbreviations.md`

## Output Contract

When asked to decode a WWII-era map, overlay, or order: (1) identify whether the item is a topographic symbol, a military symbol, or an abbreviation, and which section governs it; (2) apply the color rule (blue friendly, red enemy, with the two fire/gas exceptions) and the position rules for numbers and letters before guessing at meaning; (3) give the 1941 reading, then state plainly whether the modern equivalent differs — never present a 1941 symbol as current doctrine. When asked for a symbol whose drawn form does not survive in the extracted text, say so and point to the PDF; do not invent a drawing from the label. Cite paragraph numbers (¶7, ¶8, ¶9, ¶12) for rules and readings.

## Operating Rules

1. **Color first.** Blue is friendly, red is enemy — except friendly fire/gas areas (blue) and gassed areas (always red).
2. **Position is meaning.** Right = division/regiment number and explanations; left = battalion number and company letter; within = army/corps number; under = caliber.
3. **Echelon ticks above, branch inside.** Size and type are read together, never alone.
4. **No conflict, and explain.** Any non-authorized symbol must not conflict with an authorized one and must be explained in a legend.
5. **Historic, not current.** FM 21-30 (1941) is a reading key for WWII documents, not a standard for modern maps.
