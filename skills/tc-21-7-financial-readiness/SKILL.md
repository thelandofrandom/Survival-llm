# TC 21-7 Personal Financial Readiness and Deployability Handbook

## Purpose

TC 21-7, *Personal Financial Readiness and Deployability Handbook* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 14 August 2003, 101 pages; supersedes TC 21-7, 17 November 1997; proponent: U.S. Army Finance School) — the Army's plain-language guide to military pay and personal finance, written in question-and-answer form for soldiers in the Active Component (AC) and the Reserve Component (RC: Army National Guard and U.S. Army Reserve). It explains the pay system, the Leave and Earnings Statement (LES), travel claims, deployment preparation, banking and credit, and budgeting and investing, with practical exercises at the end of each chapter (answers in Chapter 8) and a commander's appendix (Appendix A) comparing the AC and RC pay systems.

**Dated-figures warning — read this first.** This is a 2003 handbook, and its dollar figures, rates, and program details are from 2003. They are quoted below as the manual states them — **do not use them as current rates**. Examples: SGLI is described as up to $250,000 of coverage for up to $20 a month (today's SGLI maximum is $500,000); hostile fire / imminent danger pay is given as $150 a month; BAS is given as $242.81 a month when authorized to mess separately and $262.50 when rations-in-kind are not available; per diem meals-and-incidental rates are given as $26–$38 a day. Programs have also changed names and rules since 2003 — the manual's "Soldiers' and Sailors' Civil Relief Act (SSCRA)" was renamed the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA) later in 2003, and the Thrift Savings Plan, myPay, and travel-card rules have all evolved. The durable value of this handbook is its **framework**: how military pay works, how to read an LES, how to prepare finances for deployment, how to budget, and how to use (and not abuse) credit. For current rates and rules, check DFAS, myPay, and current Army regulations.

**What this manual does not cover.** Despite its subject, TC 21-7 contains no section on payday lending, predatory lending, or rent-to-own schemes by name. Its credit guidance is general — compare interest rates, understand co-signer liability, pay more than the minimum on cards, and use the bank or credit union first. Do not cite this manual as a source on predatory-lending protections; the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act material in Chapter 3 is the closest it comes.

## 1. Pay, allowances, and deductions (Chapter 1)

**The pay system.** Soldiers are paid by direct deposit (SURE PAY) to a designated account; active-duty soldiers may elect to be paid once a month or twice a month. Pay and allowances covered in the chapter: monthly base pay, Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH), Basic Allowance for Subsistence (BAS), CONUS and OCONUS cost-of-living allowances (COLA), Family Separation Allowance (FSA), Overseas Housing Allowance (OHA), clothing allowance, per diem, and special pays for special duty.

**2003 figures quoted in the chapter:** BAS $242.81 a month when authorized to mess separately; $262.50 a month when rations-in-kind are not available. Clothing allowance: $274.01 for temporary duty of 15 days in a 30-day period; $548.02 for 30 days in a 36-month period. Special pays include enlistment bonuses, imminent danger/hostile fire pay, flight pay, sea pay, and hardship duty pay — imminent danger/hostile fire pay is given as an extra $150 a month, and hardship duty pay as $150 a month for qualifying officers and enlisted soldiers.

**Deductions.** Federal income tax is deducted from all entitlements ending in the word "PAY" (base pay, foreign duty pay, special duty pay, etc.); state income tax is deducted where the soldier's state of legal residence imposes one; BAH and similar allowances are not taxable. Other common deductions: AAFES deferred-payment-plan charges that are overdue; 50 cents a month from Regular Army enlisted soldiers and warrant officers for the Uniformed Soldiers' and Sailors' Home; **SGLI** — up to $20 a month deducted to purchase up to $250,000 of Servicemember's Group Life Insurance (2003 figures; coverage and cost change periodically); State Sponsored Life Insurance (SSLI) for National Guard soldiers; fines and forfeitures (Article 15 or court-martial); **garnishments** — delinquent alimony, child support, IRS, or commercial debts can be garnished from military pay, processed through DFAS Cleveland after court/legal action; Government Property Lost or Damaged (GPLD); bad checks at the PX, commissary, finance office, or other government agencies (plus a $25–$35 return-check fee per check, 2003 figures); and Montgomery G.I. Bill deductions.

**Allotments.** An allotment is a designated portion of pay authorized to a qualified allottee — a convenient way to meet regular obligations, because allotments continue even when a soldier deploys, is on leave, on TDY, or in the hospital. Active-duty soldiers are authorized **no more than six discretionary allotments** at one time; allotments for Army Emergency Relief (AER), Combined Federal Campaign (CFC), American Red Cross, repayment of a debt to the government, and savings bonds do not count toward the six. Allotments are started through the PAC. Types include bank (consumer loans, car note, deposits), voluntary support of a legal dependent, mortgage/rent payment, non-individual allotments (mutual funds, insurance), charitable contributions, loan repayment (AER/Red Cross), and authorized purchases (U.S. Savings Bonds). When a soldier separates, allotments stop — plan ahead so obligations previously paid by allotment are still paid. → `references/pay-allowances-les.md`

**Thrift Savings Plan (TSP).** A federal government-sponsored retirement savings and investment plan, the same type of savings and tax benefits many private corporations offer under 401(k) plans. The National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2001 extended TSP participation to servicemembers. Enroll with the TSP-U-1 election form through the S-1; check the LES monthly and the semiannual TSP participant statements to confirm contributions. The manual's warning is blunt: TSP is for long-term retirement savings, there are penalties for early withdrawals, and for short-term savings U.S. Savings Bonds are the better vehicle. More information at www.tsp.gov (as cited in 2003).

**myPay.** The online system (as described in 2003) lets soldiers print LESs, make direct-deposit, allotment, and address changes, change TSP options, start/change/stop savings bonds, print travel vouchers, and change tax withholdings. → `references/pay-allowances-les.md`

## 2. Active Component entitlements and reading the LES (Chapter 2)

**Who and how.** Chapter 2 covers Active Component and Active Guard/Reserve (AGR) soldiers, paid on the Defense Military Pay Office–Active Component (DMO-AC). All AC soldiers receive monthly base pay and may be eligible for BAH and BAS depending on duty assignment and dependents. The chapter works through the common scenarios: single enlisted SSG and below in barracks with government meals (base pay, BAS, partial BAH); single enlisted SFC and above electing not to occupy government quarters (base pay, BAH at the without-dependents rate, BAS); enlisted soldiers with dependents in government quarters (base pay, BAS) or off-post (base pay, BAH at the with-dependents rate, BAS); single officers in or out of government quarters; officers with families; and dual-military couples — each soldier paid base pay, BAS, and applicable special pays, with one soldier claiming dependents for BAH at the with-dependents rate when living off-post.

**The LES.** The chapter walks a sample LES block by block (fields 1–24 and beyond): entitlements, deductions, allotments, and the summary — and includes exercises asking the reader to find specific figures on the sample LES (for example, SSG Harris's pay). The mid-month Net Pay Advice (NPA) shows the mid-month payment. The practical discipline the chapter teaches is simple: read every LES, check that entitlements, deductions, and allotments are right, and use the chain of command and finance office when they are not.

**PCS.** A Permanent Change of Station entitles soldiers and dependents to travel and related allowances — the chapter covers home allowances, dislocation allowance, and temporary lodging expense, with travel pay detail deferred to Chapter 4. → `references/pay-allowances-les.md`

## 3. Reserve Component entitlements and mobilization (Chapter 3)

**The RC pay system.** The Reserve Component pay system is the **Defense Military Pay Office–Reserve Component (DMO-RC)**, which pays up to eight times per month. RC soldiers are paid on the next scheduled payday following completion of duty. The soldier — not the unit — holds the soldier's most current pay information. A reservist receives an LES for duty performed and whenever an administrative change is made to the Master Military Pay Account (MMPA); reservists should read each LES carefully to confirm proper pay for duty performed and to review MMPA changes.

**Mobilization.** Mobilization is the process by which the Armed Forces assembles personnel, supplies, and materials for active service in war or national emergency; only the President and/or Congress can order federal mobilization, and the Secretary of Defense directs mobilization of Reserve units and individual RC soldiers. At the mobilization site, soldiers review their pay records; RC soldiers should prepare during IDT assemblies, annual training, and mobilization preparation. If a deployed reservist has a pay problem, the answer is the chain of command, resolved at the lowest level.

**Protections.** The chapter names two: the **Soldiers' and Sailors' Civil Relief Act (SSCRA)** — the 1940 Act enacted by Congress at the start of World War II to protect and offer relief to "those who dropped their affairs to answer their country's call to duty" (renamed the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act, SCRA, later in 2003) — and the **National Committee for Employer Support of the Guard and Reserve**, which assists reservists with reemployment problems after mobilization. → `references/pay-allowances-les.md`

## 4. Travel claims (Chapter 4)

**The form.** Travel reimbursement is claimed on **DD Form 1351-2, Travel Voucher**, used by both active-duty and reserve soldiers but processed through different pay channels — check with the local PAC (active duty) or unit administrator (reserves) to route it correctly. Completing the voucher accurately is the soldier's responsibility. The chapter walks the form block by block with a sample voucher.

**The rules.** Soldiers ordered to TDY may be entitled to mileage reimbursement, reimbursement for commercial transportation (not to exceed the cost of a Government Transportation Request), per diem (meals and lodging), and reimbursement for other authorized expenses. File the voucher **within 5 days after completion of travel**; the traveler signs block 21a; attach orders and original lodging receipts; **all reimbursable expenses of $75.00 or more must be accompanied by original receipts**; type or computer-generate the voucher, or print neatly; the supervisor must review and sign it.

**The "Lodging Plus" per diem system.** Per diem is a daily allowance for food, lodging, and incidental expenses while away from home or permanent duty station. Meals and incidental expenses (M&IE) are reimbursed at rates varying by locale — **$26.00 to $38.00 a day in 2003** — and soldiers receive **75% of the M&IE rate for the first and last day of travel**. Lodging is reimbursed at the actual cost of a single room up to the area lodging rate (a $68 room in a $60 area is reimbursed $60; a $48.25 room is reimbursed $48.25). Soldiers traveling to an Army post with dining and billeting facilities are expected to use them and are reimbursed at the government rate. Per diem does not cover parking or car rental, which are separate reimbursable expenses. → `references/pay-allowances-les.md`

## 5. Financial readiness and deployability (Chapter 5) — the core chapter

The preface calls this "probably the most important chapter of this circular." It is written as a dialogue between SPC Johnson, a single soldier, and her platoon sergeant, SFC Mills, and its thesis is stated in the first answers: **set your finances up so you can deploy without making changes, and do it now** — not when the deployment order arrives, when the finance office will be swamped and may take weeks to process changes.

**Paying bills while deployed.** Pay continues through the same system, deposited by SURE PAY to the designated account. The manual's recommended setup: a checking account with an ATM card, so bills can be paid by check and money accessed worldwide. **Allotments** are the recommended way to pay fixed debts (car payments, mortgages, installment loans) because each has a beginning and end date — but only AC soldiers can establish allotments, and they are limited to six discretionary ones. For bills beyond those six: electronic fund transfer (EFT) through the financial institution (a nominal fee, but bills paid on time with no late charges and no credit damage from mail delays), or a **draft payment** arranged directly with the creditor, which normally carries no fee. Decide the payment method, establish it, and keep a list of every bill's mailing address and amount plus the financial institution's address, phone number, and account numbers. Take the checkbook and ATM card on deployment. Start allotments **right away**, not at deployment notification — the finance office will be processing paperwork for everyone who waited, and late payments result.

**Deployment entitlements.** BAS may terminate on deployment (field rations replace it). Additional pay may include: **Foreign Duty Pay (FDP)** — enlisted soldiers, rate by grade; **Imminent Danger Pay (IDP)** — all soldiers within a specified geographic area, flat monthly rate; **Family Separation Allowance (FSA)** — all soldiers with dependents away from family for more than 30 consecutive days, paid on the 31st day retroactive to the first day, continuing until redeployment.

**Taxes.** In a combat zone (CZ) or qualified hazardous duty area (QHDA) established by Presidential Executive Order, all military pay received by enlisted soldiers and warrant officers is exempt from federal and state income-tax withholding; officers are exempt on pay up to the maximum rate of pay for the Sergeant Major of the Army plus $150. State laws vary — check the state.

**Married soldiers and families.** Prepare an up-to-date **family care plan**; the best financial preparation for a spouse is advance planning. The manual recommends **separate checking accounts** for the soldier and spouse (plus a joint account for use when not deployed, funded monthly for the soldier's own deployed expenses) because a single joint account causes problems during separation. If a spouse must evacuate a foreign country in a hurry, the family care plan should cover it, and the spouse should hold a **DD Form 1337 (Authorization/Designation for Emergency Pay and Allowances)**, which makes the spouse eligible for a noncombatant evacuation payment equal to **2 months' advance pay** — repayable, but immediate money for an apartment, a rental car, and basic expenses. The unit **family support group** (a formally chartered volunteer group of unit family members) provides information and support during separations. A spouse can get a copy of the LES through the rear detachment commander, coordinated with finance and the battalion PAC — a power of attorney should state the spouse is authorized to pick it up.

**The will.** Always have a current will, especially with a family: if a soldier is listed missing in action or a casualty, the family can have problems claiming belongings without one. The JAG office prepares wills for free. Prepare it immediately — do not wait for deployment orders. The chapter closes with **family deployability checklists** covering everything soldiers and families should do before a deployment — use them now, not at deployment time. → `references/financial-readiness-deployability.md`

## 6. Banking and credit (Chapter 6)

**Banking services.** Banks, credit unions, savings and loan companies, and other financial institutions all provide basic banking services; shop around and compare services and fees before choosing.

**Credit, in the manual's definition.** Credit is borrowing money now and promising to pay it back later. **Compare interest rates and get the lowest rate possible** — the manual stresses this repeatedly, because the interest rate is the price of the loan. To apply for a loan, fill out a loan application (loan information, personal information, employment information, financial information, and co-applicant or co-signer information) and provide a copy of the purchase agreement. The institution verifies the information and runs a credit check. A bank or credit union should be the **first stop** for a car loan, but compare rates elsewhere too. On a big purchase a down payment is usually needed, and the larger the down payment the less must be borrowed.

**Co-signers.** A co-applicant applies for the loan with the borrower (e.g., a spouse). A co-signer — often a parent for a young soldier — is the person responsible for the loan if the borrower fails to pay; having a co-signer with a good credit history can help get the loan. The manual's framing is blunt: the co-signer pays if you do not.

**Leasing vs. buying.** The manual treats leasing as an option when a soldier does not think he can save enough to buy in a year, but its through-line is that the only other way to buy expensive things without saving in advance is to buy on credit — and credit costs interest. Saving first is the manual's preferred path throughout.

**Credit cards.** Every card has a spending limit set by the company. Exceeding it means the company can refuse the purchase; if it approves it, further purchases may not be approved. The monthly statement shows purchases, balance, and the minimum payment; companies charge different interest rates and some charge a yearly membership fee. The manual's practical advice: know the rate, know the fee, and pay more than the minimum.

**Government Travel Charge Card — not a credit card.** The Army Travel Charge Card (part of the Government Travel Charge Card program, implemented for official travel expenses) differs from a personal card in a decisive way: **it is not a credit card — the balance cannot be carried forward**. The account must be paid in full when the monthly bill from Bank of America is received. Individual Billing Accounts (IBAs) are issued to individual government travelers for their personal travel expenses.

**Checking accounts.** The three basic types are **standard** (full service: unlimited check writing, ATM deposits and withdrawals), **interest-bearing** (pays interest on the average daily balance, with a minimum balance that varies by institution), and **premium** (requires a minimum balance of $10,000 across savings, money-market, and time deposits, and adds benefits such as a no-fee credit card with a reduced interest rate, free checks, and reduced rates on personal loans). Any of them can be opened as a joint account (both people can write checks and access the money — the advantage in an emergency, the disadvantage that two people writing checks makes the account harder to manage and balance) or an individual account (only the opener writes checks — easier to manage). The manual's bottom line: discuss the options with an account representative; the soldier's personal financial situation determines the best account.

**Writing checks and reconciling.** A checking account lets a soldier write checks to pay bills in person or by mail — and "checks are like cash," so no one untrusted should have access to the checkbook. The institution sends a monthly statement; reconcile it against the soldier's own records, re-add if the balances do not match, and visit or call the institution if the discrepancy persists. Errors by either side are corrected through the institution. → `references/banking-credit.md`

## 7. Budgeting and investments (Chapter 7)

**Why budget.** "The best way to track your money is to develop a budget." A budget helps a soldier track money, develop a savings plan, and afford what he wants by planning spending, saving, and investing. Good saving habits now mean money for emergencies (unexpected car and home repairs), for special purchases, and for long-term financial security — but saving requires examining current spending habits and changing some of them.

**How to make one.** Write down the goal, list fixed expenses (the same every month) and variable expenses (they vary month to month — clothing, phone bills, and the like), add savings as a line item, total expenses plus savings, and subtract from monthly income. The manual's worked example for a single soldier: goal, a new stereo; savings of $100 a month for the stereo and $50 a month for other savings; expenses and savings totaling $700. The budget should be reviewed after 3 months to compare actual costs with estimates, and adjusted as income, debts, and priorities change. It should be flexible — "your budget will fail unless you invest the time and effort to make it work."

**The family budget example.** A married soldier with a wife and daughter, saving for a $300 vacation to visit his parents: fixed expenses — rent $450, utilities $70, food $300 (the commissary is the manual's tip for cheaper food), car payment $290, gas/oil/repairs $100, car insurance $60; variable expenses — clothing $75, cable TV $25, babysitting $40, telephone $45, church $40, school $130, recreation $50, incidentals/pocket money $120; savings — vacation $75, other $50. Total expenses and savings: **$1,920**. Income: $1,428 after taxes plus $520 from the spouse's part-time work, **$1,948** combined — a remaining balance of **$28**. The manual's lesson is explicit: go over the budget with the spouse, both have to plan together to make it work, and if it is not working, change it — but stick to some kind of budget. The $300 vacation goal is reached in 4 months at $75 a month.

**PCS moves.** Budget for a PCS: the Army pays for the move, but there are out-of-pocket expenses — hotel/motel bills at the old station, while traveling, and at the new station until housing is found; security deposits for housing and utilities; transportation, meals, laundry, and other incidentals that will not be reimbursed. Talk to the finance office before the move about entitlements and per diem rates for the new area. On a PCS, pay is a flat per diem rate, so no travel records are needed for the voucher. Never close the old SURE PAY account until the first SURE PAY deposit has been made to the new one — and there is no obligation to switch financial institutions on a PCS; if happy with the current one, stay.

**Emergencies.** If an emergency causes money problems, the manual's first answer is: come and see the leader. **Army Emergency Relief (AER)** and the **American Red Cross (ARC)** are the two major organizations that help, with assistance for stolen or lost money, funeral expenses, travel for valid emergency leave, emergency car repairs, fire or other disaster, clothing replacement, and other situations causing money problems. Out of town, any military installation's family assistance office coordinates with AER. Assistance is often an **interest-free loan that must be paid back**, but AER and ARC also offer assistance that does not have to be repaid, plus non-financial help such as food baskets and commissary vouchers. The first sergeant and commander can point to other sources of help.

**Investments — a brief survey.** The chapter closes with short descriptions of the main investment vehicles and their risk, in the manual's own terms: **savings accounts** (low risk); **Certificates of Deposit (CDs)** — money left for a set period, higher interest than savings accounts, longer period means higher yield, penalty for early withdrawal, low risk; **Money Market Deposit Accounts (MMDAs)** — rates vary with market conditions, minimum balance required, usually only six pre-authorized withdrawals a month (three payable to a third party), and the manual calls them risky because the money is invested in the money market; **stocks** — part ownership of a company, value rises or falls with the company, dividends possible, fairly risky; **government bonds** — a loan to a government entity, U.S. Treasury bonds the safest, less risky than stocks because of guaranteed payback at maturity; **corporate bonds** — a loan to a corporation at a fixed interest rate, similar in risk to stocks because the investor is betting the company does well; **mutual funds** — many investors pooling money into a diversified list of stocks, bonds, and bills, managed by knowledgeable people, generally less risky than stocks and bonds because of diversification, with common types Growth, Growth and Income, and International/Global; **money market mutual funds** — pooled investment in short-term items (Treasury bills, CDs, banker's acceptances, commercial paper), income and liquidity, fairly risky because money markets fluctuate; **Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs)** — no taxes on the investment until withdrawal, available through most financial institutions, fairly safe as long as the institution is sound, in Traditional and Roth forms — the manual notes Roth IRAs offer no withdrawal penalties if the account has been open five years and the taxpayer is at least 59½ or is using the withdrawal for college or a first home, with no requirement to start taking money by 70½ and no tax-deductible contributions; and **tangible investments** (things that can be seen and touched). The chapter's investment advice is deliberately thin — it describes the vehicles and their relative risk, and leaves the choice to the soldier. → `references/budgeting-investments.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a soldier's pay, entitlement, or deployment finance question from TC 21-7, answer in the manual's framework — pay first (what is owed), then the mechanism (allotment, SURE PAY, LES), then the deployment or budget consequence. Quote 2003 dollar figures only with the year attached, and say plainly when a rate or program has changed since 2003 (SGLI, SSCRA/SCRA, TSP, travel card). If the question is about payday or predatory lending, say the manual does not address it and answer from the manual's general credit principles instead of pretending it does.

## Operating Rules

1. **Prepare now, not at deployment.** Finances set up so the soldier can deploy without changes; allotments and EFT started months ahead.
2. **Read every LES.** The soldier holds the most current pay information; check entitlements, deductions, and allotments monthly.
3. **Six discretionary allotments, no more.** AER, CFC, Red Cross, government-debt repayment, and savings bonds do not count.
4. **Never close the old account first.** On a PCS, keep the old SURE PAY account until the first deposit lands in the new one.
5. **The Government Travel Card is not a credit card.** Pay it in full when the bill arrives; the balance cannot be carried forward.
6. **Budget with the spouse, review in 3 months.** A budget both partners own, adjusted as income, debts, and priorities change.
7. **2003 figures are history.** Quote them with the year; verify current rates with DFAS and myPay.
