# Desert Operations and Logistics

Distilled from FM 90-3, Chapters 2–4 (U.S. Army, Jan 1993).

## Preparations (Chapter 2)

- **The principle:** a force sent to a desert theater will fight with the equipment it has, in accordance with current doctrine. Equipment and doctrine can be modified, but much depends on how well troops and leaders have mastered individual training.
- Units trained in Germany and the United States have basic technical and tactical skills adaptable to desert warfare — but adaptation is deliberate, not accidental.
- **Preparation sequence:** individual training (acclimatization, water discipline, desert navigation, desert driving), unit training (modified tactics, desert live-fire, desert movement), equipment preparation (desert modifications, spare parts for the parts the desert destroys fastest, filters/covers/cleaning gear).
- Train operators on the equipment they will actually use, in desert conditions, **before** deployment — not after arrival. Surveillance, target acquisition, and night observation devices are all affected by the environment; learn their desert limitations in training, not combat.

## Tactical Operations (Chapter 3, Section I)

- **The key to success is mobility** — proven in Desert Storm: wide and rapid flanking movements like Rommel's and Montgomery's in North Africa.
- **Trafficability:** generally good; cross-country movement a lesser problem — but not always. Salt marshes can be NO-GO in the rainy season. Sand bogs down traffic and makes foot movement slow and exhausting. Steep dune and rocky mountain slopes can be NO-GO for vehicles. Wadis (dry stream beds) create cross-compartmented terrain with steep banks.
- The desert's openness is both opportunity and danger: it permits the wide flanking movement and exposes the force making it.

## Offensive Operations (Chapter 3, Section II)

- A mobile offense: wide envelopments, rapid exploitation, pursuit across open ground.
- Find the enemy, fix him, envelop him, exploit before he can reposition across ground that favors the side that moves first.
- Protect long, exposed flanks and lines of communication while moving.

## Defensive Operations (Chapter 3, Section III)

- Defense of positions dominating movement, depth by dispersion and successive positions.
- Uses the desert's long fields of fire and observation; guards against the envelopment the same open ground permits the attacker.
- A defense tied to a single position in the desert is a defense waiting to be bypassed.

## Combat Service Support (Chapter 4)

- **The logistics problem:** a lack of every kind of resource, especially ports and railways with high capacity for moving combat supplies. Arid environment burdens supply, aviation, communications, and maintenance.
- A unit's tactical effectiveness depends largely on available combat service support; its vulnerability lies in exposed lines of communication and immobile bases of supply and support.
- Water, fuel, and ammunition move long distances on few, exposed roads. Supply bases are immobile and therefore targets.
- The support plan must account for distances, dust, heat's effect on vehicles and supplies, and the enemy's ability to interdict a line of communication that has nowhere to hide.
- **The supply problem is harder than the fighting problem** — the force that cannot be supplied cannot fight.
