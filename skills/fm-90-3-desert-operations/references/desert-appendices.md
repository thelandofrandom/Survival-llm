# FM 90-3 Appendices

Distilled from FM 90-3, Appendices A–H (U.S. Army, Jan 1993).

## A — Desert Countries of the World

- Brief descriptions of notable deserts of the world. Example: the Arabian (Eastern) desert — **70,000 square miles** between the Nile and the Red Sea.
- Detailed information from Defense Intelligence Agency terrain analysis and the DA Pamphlet 550-series. Figure A-1 maps desert locations worldwide.

## B — Employment of Aircraft in Desert Operations

- The desert is probably the most severe of all environments in which aviation units must operate.
- Standard operating procedures differ from areas with abundant contrasting terrain and substantial vegetation.
- Air combat operations that rely on heavy vegetation and varying contour terrain for concealment do not work in the desert — aircraft are exposed. The appendix is the special-considerations set for employing them anyway.

## C — Techniques for Operating Equipment in the Desert

- The daily discipline: driving (drivers and track commanders observe the guidelines for desert areas), maintenance, and the techniques that save both equipment and lives.
- Chapter 1 diagnoses the desert's attack on equipment; Appendix C is the treatment.

## D — Effects of the Environment on NBC Weapons

- NBC weapons and smoke produce casualties, destroy/disable equipment, and disrupt operations.
- Desert weather and terrain affect how NBC and smoke weapons behave and how they are employed — heat, wind, and absence of vegetation change how agents disperse and persist.
- FM 3-3, FM 3-4, FM 3-5, and FM 3-50 cover NBC defense and smoke operations; this appendix addresses the desert's specific effects.

## E — Desert Concealment and Camouflage

- Camouflage in the desert requires imagination, ingenuity, and intelligence.
- No natural overhead cover, increased range of vision, bright terrain tones → emphasis on **siting, dispersion discipline, and decoys** for deception and surprise.
- **Total concealment is rarely achieved** — but proper camouflage reduces the effectiveness of enemy observation.
- Cover from direct fire may come from dunes, hills, and other irregularities.
- Poor camouflage may compromise a high-level plan; impress the concept on fresh units and replacements on arrival.

## F — Operations in Mountains

- Full mountain operations are in FM 90-6; this appendix covers special conditions in arid ranges such as the southern Sinai and the shores of the Red Sea.
- Mountains are high and rugged with very steep slopes. Valleys narrow with steeper sides and are usually the only routes allowing ground movement of men and equipment at speed or in quantity.
- Water is nonexistent on hilltops and unusual in valleys except during flash floods after rains.
- Lateral ground communications are limited unless the force moves across the spines of ranges. Navigation is difficult — maps are likely to be inaccurate.

## G — Water Usage in Desert Operations

- Water is a critical item of supply; forces trying to survive without adequate water have always met with disaster. Finding and keeping water sources may be the most crucial issue in desert conflicts.
- Requirements calculation: drinking water plus almost **2 gallons per man per day** for other functions (cooking, hygiene, etc.).
- Mission factors change requirements: how the mission affects water needs, local availability, and heating rates for water.
- Some sources may provide more than **20,000 gallons of water per day** — but availability varies; plan for the worst case.

## H — Fratricide Reduction

- Fratricide: employment of friendly weapons with intent to kill the enemy that results in unforeseen death or injury to friendly personnel.
- The modern battlefield is more lethal than any in history; pace is rapid; the non-linear battlefield creates command and control challenges.
- **Thermal imagery acquisition outranges friend-or-foe identification.** Accuracy and lethality at long range make misidentification fatal.
- The appendix focuses on actions leaders can take with current resources to reduce the risk.
