# Desert Environment and Personnel

Distilled from FM 90-3, Chapter 1 (U.S. Army, Jan 1993).

## The Environment

- Deserts are arid, barren regions incapable of supporting normal life due to lack of water. Annual rainfall: **0–10 inches**.
- Terrain varies; the sole common denominator is lack of water, with sparse/absent vegetation and eroded topsoil.
- Temperatures: over **136°F** in the deserts of Mexico and Libya; bitter cold of winter in the Gobi. Day-to-night fluctuation exceeds **70°F** in some deserts.
- Highest known ambient temperature: **136°F (58°C)**. Internal tank temperatures in the Sahara in WWII approached **160°F (71°C)**. Winter in Siberian deserts and the Gobi: **−50°F (−45°C)**. Inland Sinai day-to-night swing: up to **72°F**.
- **Winds:** near-hurricane force; dust and sand make life intolerable, maintenance very difficult, visibility a matter of meters. Sahara *khamseen* lasts days (spring/summer). Iran's "wind of 120 days": sand from the north at up to **75 mph**, almost constant. No one is buried alive by a sandstorm, but individuals are separated from units. Dust raised by wheels, tracks, and marching troops gives away movement.

## Acclimatization

- Allow ~**2 weeks**, with progressive heat exposure and physical exertion.
- Significant acclimatization: **4–5 days**. Full acclimatization: **7–14 days**, with **2–3 hours/day** of exercise in the heat.
- **Acclimatization does not reduce, and may increase, water requirements.** No total protection against heat exists.
- If troops cannot fully acclimatize before heavy labor: limit heavy activity to cooler hours, rest frequently. Check the weather daily.

## Climatic Stress

- Caused by air temperature, humidity, air movement, and radiant heat — worsened by lack of acclimatization, overweight, dehydration, alcohol, lack of sleep, old age, poor health.
- Body maintains **98.6°F** by conduction/convection, radiation, and evaporation. In the daytime desert, evaporation is most important — air temperature is already above skin temperature. High humidity defeats evaporation and the cooling mechanism fails.

## Water Discipline — Drinking Rules

- Consider water a tactical weapon; **force** water consumption. Troops in armored vehicles, MOPP gear, and body armor need more.
- Drink cool water when possible (**50–55°F**).
- Drink **1 qt in the morning, at each meal, and before strenuous work**. In hot climates: at least **1 qt/hour**. At higher temperatures: **over 2 qt/hour**.
- Frequent drinks beat the same amount all at once. Larger personnel need more.
- Replace salt loss through eating meals.
- Reduce workloads in the first days of heat exposure; increase gradually with acclimatization.
- Rest frequently in the shade; lower work rate as heat increases; do heavy work in cooler hours (early morning, late evening).

## Water Quantities

- More than **3 gallons of drinking water per man per day** in the heat.
- **9 quarts/day** is the minimum to survive; **5 gallons** is recommended.
- Non-drinking functions (cooking, hygiene, other) need almost **2 gallons per man per day** on top of drinking water (Appendix G).
- A force incapable of long-distance resupply of its water requirements cannot operate.

## Heat Illness

- Ladder: heat cramps → heat exhaustion (most common) → heat stroke (the killer).
- Cool the casualty, replace fluids, evacuate. Heat casualties require medical treatment and supervision; prevention beats treatment.

## Other Personnel Effects

- Sunburn and skin injury, eye injury from glare and dust, respiratory effects of dust, foot problems from heat and sand, and the psychological effects of the desert — isolation, monotony, constant heat and discomfort. The desert attacks morale as surely as the body.

## Effects on Equipment (Chapter 1, Section III)

- Engines ingest dust and lose power; filters clog; lubricants thin in heat and thicken in cold desert nights; tires and tracks wear faster on rock and sand; electronics overheat; batteries fail; optics degraded by dust and heat shimmer.
- Equipment designed for temperate climates must be modified — and maintained far more aggressively — to survive the desert. Appendix C is the technique set.
