# Desert Operations (FM 90-3)

## Purpose

FM 90-3, *Desert Operations* (U.S. Army, January 1993, 221 pages), is the classic Army doctrine for desert warfare — the manual that distilled the Second World War's North Africa campaigns, and then the 1991 Desert Storm experience, into a working guide for commanders and staffs. It was superseded as joint doctrine by ATP 3-90.99/MCTP 12-10D (2021), but it remains the fuller treatment of several subjects the later manual compresses: the desert environment's effects on personnel and equipment in detail, preparations and training for deployment, operations in desert conditions, combat service support, and the appendices on aircraft employment, equipment techniques, NBC weapons, concealment and camouflage, mountain operations in arid ranges, water usage, and fratricide reduction. Read it alongside MCTP 12-10D: the 2021 manual is the current doctrine, and this is the deeper bench behind it.

The manual's governing idea, stated in Chapter 1 and proven in Desert Storm, is that successful desert operations require adaptation to the environment and to the limitations its terrain and climate impose — equipment and tactics must be modified for a dusty, rugged landscape where temperatures vary from extreme highs down to freezing and visibility may change from 30 miles to 30 feet in a matter of minutes. Deserts are arid, barren regions incapable of supporting normal life due to lack of water, and that single fact drives everything else.

## 1. The Environment and Its Effects on Personnel (Chapter 1, Sections I–II)

**The environment.** Desert terrain varies considerably from place to place; the sole common denominator is lack of water, with its consequent effects — sparse or absent vegetation and eroded topsoil. The basic landforms are similar to those elsewhere, but the topsoil has been stripped away. Temperatures vary by latitude and season, from over **136°F** in the deserts of Mexico and Libya to the bitter cold of winter in the Gobi. In some deserts the day-to-night fluctuation exceeds **70°F**. Annual rainfall varies from 0 to 10 inches.

**Temperature.** The highest known ambient temperature recorded in a desert was 136°F (58°C). Lower temperatures than this produced internal tank temperatures approaching **160°F (71°C)** in the Sahara during the Second World War. Winter temperatures in Siberian deserts and the Gobi reach **−50°F (−45°C)**. Low temperatures are aggravated by very strong winds producing high windchill. The cloudless sky permits the earth to heat during sunlit hours, yet cool to near freezing at night — in the inland Sinai, day-to-night fluctuations are as much as **72°F**.

**Winds.** Desert winds achieve velocities of near hurricane force; dust and sand suspended in them make life intolerable, maintenance very difficult, and restrict visibility to a few meters. The Sahara *khamseen* lasts for days at a time in spring and summer. The deserts of Iran are known for the "wind of 120 days," sand blowing almost constantly from the north at up to **75 mph**. No one is buried alive by a sandstorm, but individuals can become separated from their units. Rapid temperature changes invariably follow strong winds. Even without wind, the dust clouds raised by wheels, tracks, and marching troops give away movement — and wind drags the signature downwind.

**Acclimatization.** Acclimatization to heat is necessary to permit the body to reach and maintain efficiency in its cooling process. Allow approximately **2 weeks** for acclimatization, with progressive increases in heat exposure and physical exertion. Significant acclimatization can be attained in **4–5 days**, but full acclimatization takes **7–14 days**, with **2–3 hours per day** of exercise in the heat. Gradually increase physical activity until full acclimatization is achieved. Critical: **acclimatization does not reduce, and may increase, water requirements.** There is no such thing as total protection against the debilitating effects of heat. When troops cannot become fully acclimatized before heavy labor, limit heavy activity to cooler hours and rest frequently. Check the weather daily — day-to-day and region-to-region variations in temperature, wind, and humidity can be substantial.

**Climatic stress.** Climatic stress in hot deserts is caused by any combination of air temperature, humidity, air movement, and radiant heat, worsened by lack of acclimatization, being overweight, dehydration, alcohol, lack of sleep, old age, and poor health. The body maintains its optimum temperature of **98.6°F** by conduction/convection, radiation, and evaporation (sweat). In the daytime desert, evaporation is the most important — air temperature alone is probably already above skin temperature. If relative humidity is high, sweat cannot evaporate, and the cooling mechanism fails.

**Water discipline — the drinking rules.** Consider water a tactical weapon. Reduce heat injury by forcing water consumption. Soldiers and Marines in armored vehicles, MOPP gear, and body armor need to increase their water intake. The manual's rules:
- Drink cool water when possible (**50–55°F**).
- Drink **one quart of water in the morning, at each meal, and before strenuous work**. In hot climates drink at least **one quart of water each hour**. At higher temperatures hourly requirements increase to **over two quarts**.
- Take frequent drinks — they are more effective than drinking the same amount all at once. Larger Soldiers and Marines need more water.
- Replace salt loss through eating meals.
- Reduce workloads and duration of physical activity during the first days of heat exposure, then gradually increase with acclimatization.
- Modify activities when risk factors are present (fatigue, loss of sleep, previous heat exhaustion, medication).
- Take frequent rest periods in the shade if possible; lower the work rate as the heat condition increases.
- Perform heavy work in the cooler hours — early morning or late evening — if possible.

**Water quantities.** The manual's planning figures: a man needs more than **3 gallons of drinking water per day** in the heat; **9 quarts per day** is the minimum to survive, but **5 gallons** is recommended. Appendix G's full accounting puts total daily water requirements (drinking, cooking, hygiene, and other functions) at almost **2 gallons per man per day** for the non-drinking functions alone, on top of drinking water. A force incapable of long-distance resupply of its water requirements is a force that cannot operate — finding and keeping water sources may be the most crucial issue in desert conflicts.

**Heat illnesses.** The manual describes the symptoms and treatment for the heat-illness ladder: heat cramps, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke. Heat exhaustion is the most common; heat stroke is the killer. The treatment principle throughout is the same: cool the casualty, replace fluids, and evacuate — heat casualties require medical treatment and supervision, and preventing casualties is much better than treating them.

**Other personnel effects.** The chapter covers the full range: sunburn and skin injury, eye injury from glare and dust, respiratory effects of dust, foot problems from heat and sand, and the psychological effects of the desert — isolation, monotony, and the strain of constant heat and discomfort. The desert attacks morale as surely as it attacks the body, and the leader's job includes managing both.

## 2. Environmental Effects on Equipment (Chapter 1, Section III)

The desert's effects on equipment are the other half of Chapter 1, and the manual treats them with the same seriousness as the personnel effects: heat, dust, sand, and temperature swings attack vehicles, weapons, communications, and optics. Engines ingest dust and lose power; filters clog; lubricants thin in heat and thicken in cold desert nights; tires and tracks wear faster on rock and sand; electronics overheat; batteries fail; optics are degraded by dust and heat shimmer. The chapter's message is that equipment designed for temperate climates must be modified — and maintained far more aggressively — to survive the desert. Appendix C is the technique set that turns this diagnosis into daily practice.

## 3. Preparations for Desert Operations (Chapter 2)

**The preparation principle.** A force sent to a desert theater will fight with the equipment it has, in accordance with current doctrine. Equipment and doctrine can be modified to suit the new environment, but much will depend on how well Soldiers, Marines, and leaders have mastered their individual training. Units that trained in Germany and the United States have the basic technical and tactical skills that can be adapted for desert warfare — but adaptation is a deliberate act, not an accident.

**Deployment preparation.** The chapter covers the preparation sequence: individual training (acclimatization, water discipline, desert navigation, desert driving), unit training (modified tactics, desert live-fire, desert movement), and equipment preparation (desert modifications, spare parts for the parts the desert destroys fastest, and the filters, covers, and cleaning gear that keep machines running). Training should be modified for the desert environment, and the points to emphasize are the ones described in Chapter 1 and Appendix D: heat, water, navigation, and the equipment's vulnerabilities.

**What the desert changes about training.** The chapter's training guidance is practical: train operators on the equipment they will actually use in the desert, in desert conditions, before deployment — not after arrival. Surveillance, target acquisition, and night observation devices are all affected by the environment, and operators must learn their desert limitations in training, not in combat.

## 4. Operations in Desert Conditions (Chapter 3)

**Section I — How the desert environment affects tactical operations.** The key to success in desert operations is mobility, clearly evident in Desert Storm: wide and rapid flanking movements similar to those Rommel and Montgomery executed in North Africa. Trafficability and cross-country movement become critical when using these tactics. Trafficability is generally good in the desert and cross-country movement is a lesser problem — but not always. Salt marshes can create NO-GO conditions during the rainy season. Sand can bog down traffic and make foot movement slow and exhausting. The steep slopes of dunes and rocky mountains can make vehicular movement a NO-GO. The wadis — dry stream beds — create cross-compartmented terrain, and their banks can be steep and difficult to cross. The desert's openness is both the opportunity and the danger: it permits the wide flanking movement, and it exposes the force making it.

**Section II — Offensive operations.** The desert offense is a mobile offense: wide envelopments, rapid exploitation, and pursuit across open ground. The attacker uses the desert's openness to move fast and strike where the defender is not — and must protect his own long, exposed flanks and lines of communication while doing so. The chapter's offensive treatment is the classic one: find the enemy, fix him, envelop him, and exploit before he can reposition across ground that favors the side that moves first.

**Section III — Defensive operations.** The desert defense is a defense of positions that dominate movement, with depth achieved by dispersion and successive positions. The defender uses the desert's long fields of fire and observation, and must guard against the envelopment the same open ground permits the attacker. The defense that is tied to a single position in the desert is a defense waiting to be bypassed.

## 5. Combat Service Support (Chapter 4)

**The logistics problem.** There is a lack of every kind of resource in the desert, especially the sophisticated infrastructure of ports and railways with their high capacity for moving combat supplies. Logistical support is always a challenge, and an arid environment burdens all types — supply, aviation, communications, and maintenance. Commanders must be sensitive to the constraints, and those providing support must work to overcome them. A unit's tactical effectiveness depends to a large degree on the combat service support available; equally, its vulnerability lies in its exposed lines of communications and the immobility of its bases of supply and support.

**What that means in practice.** Water, fuel, and ammunition must be moved over long distances on roads that are few and exposed. Bases of supply are immobile and therefore targets. The support plan must account for the distances, the dust, the heat's effect on vehicles and supplies, and the enemy's ability to interdict a line of communication that has nowhere to hide. Combat service support for desert operations is described in detail in this chapter because it is the operation's center of gravity: the force that cannot be supplied cannot fight, and in the desert the supply problem is harder than the fighting problem.

## 6. The Appendices

- **A — Desert Countries of the World.** Brief descriptions of notable deserts of the world — the Arabian (Eastern) desert, 70,000 square miles between the Nile and the Red Sea, and the rest — with terrain analysis available from the Defense Intelligence Agency and the DA Pamphlet 550-series. Figure A-1 maps the desert locations of the world.
- **B — Employment of Aircraft in Desert Operations.** The desert is probably the most severe of all environments in which aviation units must operate. Standard operating procedures differ from areas with abundant contrasting terrain and substantial vegetation. Air combat operations that rely on heavy vegetation and varying contour terrain for concealment do not work in the desert — aircraft are exposed, and the appendix is the special-considerations set for employing them anyway.
- **C — Techniques for Operating Equipment in the Desert.** The daily discipline: driving (drivers and track commanders observe the guidelines for desert areas), maintenance, and the techniques that save both equipment and lives. If Chapter 1 diagnoses the desert's attack on equipment, Appendix C is the treatment.
- **D — Effects of the Environment on NBC Weapons.** The primary purpose of NBC weapons and smoke is to produce casualties, destroy or disable equipment, and disrupt operations. Both desert weather and terrain affect the behavior of NBC and smoke weapons and influence their tactical employment — heat, wind, and the absence of vegetation change how agents disperse and persist. FM 3-3, FM 3-4, FM 3-5, and FM 3-50 describe NBC defense and smoke operations; this appendix addresses the desert's specific effects.
- **E — Desert Concealment and Camouflage.** In the desert, camouflage problems require imagination, ingenuity, and intelligence. The lack of natural overhead cover, the increased range of vision, and the bright tones of the desert terrain place emphasis on siting, dispersion discipline, and the skillful employment of decoys to achieve deception and surprise. Total concealment is rarely achieved — but proper camouflage measures reduce the effectiveness of enemy observation. Cover from direct fire may be afforded by dunes, hills, and other irregularities. Poor camouflage may compromise a high-level plan; the importance of the concept must be impressed on fresh units and individual replacements on arrival.
- **F — Operations in Mountains.** Mountain operations are described in detail in FM 90-6; this appendix describes special conditions associated with operating in mountains such as those in the southern Sinai and on the shores of the Red Sea. Mountains are high and rugged with very steep slopes; valleys become narrower with steeper sides and are usually the only routes allowing ground movement of men and equipment at speed or in quantity. Water is nonexistent on hilltops and unusual in valleys except during flash floods after rains. Lateral ground communications are limited unless the force moves across the spines of ranges. Navigation is difficult — maps are likely to be inaccurate.
- **G — Water Usage in Desert Operations.** Combat operations in the desert pose unique problems because there is so little water and troops and equipment cannot survive without it. Water is a critical item of supply; forces trying to survive without adequate water have always met with disaster. Finding and keeping water sources may be the most crucial issue in desert conflicts. The appendix covers proper uses of water, requirements calculation (including the ~2 gallons per man per day for non-drinking functions), and the mission factors that change requirements.
- **H — Fratricide Reduction.** Fratricide is as old as warfare itself — the employment of friendly weapons with intent to kill the enemy that results in unforeseen death or injury to friendly personnel. The modern battlefield is more lethal than any in history, the pace is rapid, and the non-linear battlefield creates command and control challenges. The ability to acquire targets using thermal imagery exceeds the ability to accurately identify them as friend or foe. The appendix focuses on actions leaders can take with current resources to reduce the risk.

## Output Contract

When asked about desert operations from this manual, answer from its doctrine: lead with the environment's numbers (136°F maximum, 72°F day-night swing, 7–14 days to acclimatize, 3+ gallons of drinking water per day, 5 gallons recommended), then the preparation (train in desert conditions before deployment), then the operation (mobility, wide flanking movements, exposed logistics). Where this manual and MCTP 12-10D differ — most notably on acclimatization timelines and water figures — name both and say which is current doctrine. Distinguish doctrine (the drinking rules, the acclimatization schedule, the camouflage principles) from judgment (which route, which defensive method, how to site a position on this ground).

## Operating Rules

1. **Mobility is the key to success.** The desert rewards the wide, rapid flanking movement and punishes the static force.
2. **Water is a tactical weapon.** Force consumption; plan for 3+ gallons of drinking water per man per day, 5 gallons recommended, plus ~2 gallons for other functions.
3. **Acclimatize for 7–14 days.** Significant acclimatization in 4–5 days; full in 7–14, with 2–3 hours of exercise in the heat daily. Acclimatization does not reduce water requirements.
4. **Train in the desert before you fight in it.** A force fights with the equipment it has; adaptation is deliberate or it does not happen.
5. **Total concealment is rarely achieved — so use siting, dispersion, and decoys.** Camouflage discipline must be impressed on every replacement on arrival.
6. **Protect the lines of communication.** The unit's vulnerability lies in its exposed logistics and immobile supply bases.
7. **Identify before you fire.** Thermal acquisition outranges identification; fratricide is a leadership problem with leadership solutions.
