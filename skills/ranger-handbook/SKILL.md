# Ranger Handbook

## Purpose

TC 3-21.76, the U.S. Army Ranger Handbook (September 2025, 380 pages), distilled into field-usable doctrine. It is the pocket reference for the Ranger platoon leader, platoon sergeant, squad leader, and every Ranger who may have to assume command: how to lead a small unit, plan and order a mission, call for fire, move, patrol, fight, and bring casualties home. The handbook's own standard is blunt — every Ranger prepares to take command in an emergency — so this skill is written for the person who may have to do exactly that, with no higher headquarters to ask.

The Ranger lineage the handbook carries matters because it explains the standard: Rogers' Rangers in 1756 ("Have your musket clean as a whistle, hatchet scoured, sixty rounds powder and ball"), Marion's Partisans, Merrill's Marauders, the 1974 Ranger battalions, and the motto earned at Normandy — "Rangers lead the way!" The Ranger Creed's core is volunteered service, intestinal fortitude, and never leaving a fallen comrade. The doctrine below is how that creed is executed in the field.

## 1. Leadership — the standard and the jobs

**The leadership requirements model (Table 1-1).** Attributes: character (Army values, empathy, warrior ethos, discipline), presence (military bearing, fitness, confidence, resilience), intellect (mental agility, sound judgment, innovation, interpersonal tact, expertise). Competencies: **leads** (leads others, builds trust, extends influence beyond the chain of command, leads by example, communicates), **develops** (creates a positive environment, prepares self, develops others, stewards the profession), **achieves** (gets results). Levels: direct, organizational, situational. Outcomes: mission success, sound decisions, secured interests, expertly led organizations, fit units, healthy climates.

**Who does what in the patrol.** The platoon leader (PL) is responsible for everything the patrol does or fails to do — tactical employment, training, administration, personnel, logistics — and commands through the squad leaders within the intent of the commander two levels up. The PL builds the time schedule by **reverse planning**, takes initiative in the absence of orders, and positions himself to influence the most critical task, usually with the main effort.

The platoon sergeant (PSG) is second in succession of command: supervises administration, logistics, and maintenance; issues paragraph 4 of the OPORD; maintains personnel/weapon/equipment status; runs casualty reporting (DA Form 1156, Casualty Feeder Card); supervises the medic and aid-and-litter teams; enforces the CASEVAC plan; and owns the patrol-base security plan — interlocking sectors of fire, claymores covering dead space, range cards, alert/evacuation/withdrawal/alternate-base plans, and maintenance, hygiene, messing, water, and rest plans.

The squad leader (SL) is responsible for what the squad does or fails to do, leads by example, inspects weapons and equipment, and submits the **LACE report** (liquids, ammunition, casualties, equipment) to the PSG. The weapons squad leader (WSL) does the same for the weapons squad and controls the machine guns: designates sectors of fire, principal direction of fire, and secondary sectors; gives fire commands (shift fires, correct windage/elevation, alternate guns, control rates of fire); and knows where the assault and security elements are at all times to prevent fratricide. The team leader (TL) controls the fire team's movement and the rate and placement of fire, leads from the front, and maintains accountability of people, weapons, and equipment. The medic treats casualties, conducts triage, supervises combat lifesavers, and requests Class VIII medical supplies through the PSG. The radio operator (RTO) establishes and maintains communications, enters the net at the specified time, tracks time after the assault begins, and records all enemy contact in **SALUTE format**. The forward observer (FO) locates targets and calls for and adjusts indirect fire, and always maintains the eight-digit grid of the current location.

**Assumption of command (Table 1-2).** When a leader falls, the next Ranger takes command and, as the situation permits: **INFORMS** subordinate leaders and higher HQ; **CHECKS** security; **CHECKS** crew-served weapons; **PINPOINTS** location; **COORDINATES AND CHECKS** equipment; **CHECKS** personnel status; **ISSUES** fragmentary orders; **REORGANIZES** as needed, maintaining unit integrity when possible; **MAINTAINS** noise and light discipline; **CONTINUES** patrol-base activities, especially security; **RECONNOITERS** (at least map reconnaissance); **FINALIZES** the plan. Order is flexible; nothing on the list is optional. → `references/leadership-and-orders.md`

## 2. Troop leading procedures and the operation order

**The eight steps of TLP (Table 2-1):** 1. Receive the mission. 2. Issue a warning order. 3. Make a tentative plan. 4. Initiate movement. 5. Conduct reconnaissance. 6. Complete the plan. 7. Issue the operation order. 8. Supervise and refine. Steps 3–8 may be done in any order or simultaneously. TLP starts the moment the leader is alerted for a mission or receives a change to an existing one.

**The 1/3 : 2/3 rule.** The leader uses one-third of the available planning and preparation time; subordinates get the other two-thirds. Parallel planning is the point — never hoard the clock.

**Mission analysis — METT-TC (I).** Mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available, time available, civil considerations, and informational considerations. Within it: identify specified tasks (stated in the order) and implied tasks (apparent from analysis — e.g., "provide security during movement," "conduct resupply"), constraints (prescriptive = must do; prohibitive = must not do), and the mission-essential task(s) — failure on one of these equals mission failure. Know the commander's intent two levels up (OPORD paragraph 1b) and one level up (paragraphs 2 and 3).

**The WARNORD** mirrors the five-paragraph OPORD format and carries the minimum to start preparation: type of operation, general location, initial timeline with critical times, reconnaissance to initiate, movement to initiate, planning/preparation instructions, information requirements, and the commander's critical information requirements.

**The OPORD** is the five-paragraph order: 1. Situation. 2. Mission. 3. Execution (concept of operations, tasks to subordinate units, coordinating instructions). 4. Sustainment. 5. Command and signal. The **FRAGORD** changes an existing order without reissuing it. Annexes and coordination checklists (chapter 2) cover the attachments, adjacent units, and supporting elements a patrol actually has to talk to. The terrain model (Figure 2-2) is built to brief the plan on the ground — a sand table that every Ranger can walk around. → `references/leadership-and-orders.md`

## 3. Fire support — four tasks, danger close, and the CAS call

**The four basic tasks of fire support:** detect, deliver, assess, and — the thread through all three — integrate fires with maneuver. Interdiction limits, diverts, disrupts, delays, destroys, or damages enemy surface military potential before it can be used against friendly forces.

**Risk estimate distance (RED).** RED applies to combat only; minimum safe distances apply to training. RED is the distance at which a friendly element has a 0.1-percent probability of incapacitating casualties from a weapon system's point of impact. **Danger close for all unguided mortars and cannon artillery in the table is 600 meters.** Key REDs (standing / prone, 0.1% PI):

| System | 1/3 range | 2/3 range | Max range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60-mm mortar (M224) | 115 m / 115 m | 125 m / 120 m | 145 m / 145 m |
| 81-mm mortar (M252) | 170 m / 160 m | 195 m / 185 m | — / 190 m |
| 120-mm mortar (M120) | 280 m / 260 m | 395 m / 365 m | 430 m / 410 m |
| 105-mm howitzer HE (M119) | 290 m / 270 m | 300 m / 285 m | 455 m / 430 m |
| 155-mm howitzer HE (M109/M777) | 300 m / 285 m | 460 m / 440 m | 695 m / 665 m |

A fire support overlay is either **sterile** (only the fire support coordination measures that apply) or **nonsterile** (with all graphics); the overlay's contents are standardized in Table 3-4 so any observer can read any other unit's overlay.

**Close air support request.** The immediate-CAS call format: (a) observer identification; (b) WARNORD — "request close air"; (c) target description — type and number of targets, activity or movement, point or area target; (d) target location (grid) including elevation; (e) desired time on target; (f) desired effects on target; (g) final control; (h) remarks — friendly locations, wind direction and hazards, threats such as air-defense artillery or small arms. Fixed-wing CAS in the handbook includes the F-15E Strike Eagle (JDAM, LGB, AGM-130/158, JSOW, 20-mm cannon), AV-8B Harrier II, A-10C Thunderbolt II (30-mm cannon, AGM-65), and EC-130; rotary-wing CAS and Army attack aviation are covered in the same chapter with their own capabilities table. → `references/fire-support-and-demolitions.md`

## 4. Communications — get the message through

Chapter 4 covers the patrol's radios (SINCGARS family, UHF/SATCOM) and, critically, **antennas**: field-expedient antennas built from wire — a vertical antenna with ground-plane wires cut to the operating frequency — and the limits imposed by the curvature of the Earth on line-of-sight radio (Figure 4-1). The RTO weatherproofs all communications equipment, presets frequencies, COMSEC fills, and net identifications, and informs the PL/SL of changes to call signs, frequencies, challenges and passwords, and number combinations from the automated net control device. Rule of thumb for the patrol: comms are a weapon system — a patrol that cannot talk cannot call for fire, cannot report, and cannot be reinforced. → `references/leadership-and-orders.md`

## 5. Demolitions — explosives, initiators, and safety

**Low vs. high explosives.** Low explosives detonate at up to 1,300 feet per second and push or shove. High explosives detonate at 3,280–27,888 feet per second and shatter. Key U.S. demolition explosives (Table 5-1, TNT = 1.00 relative effectiveness):

| Explosive | Detonation velocity | RE factor | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| PETN | 8,300 m/s (27,200 ft/s) | 1.66 | Det cord, blasting caps, demolition charges |
| RDX | 8,350 m/s (27,400 ft/s) | 1.60 | Blasting caps, composition explosive |
| Composition C-4 (M112) | 8,040 m/s (26,400 ft/s) | 1.34 | Cutting and breaching charges |
| Detonating cord | 6,100–7,300 m/s | 1.66 | Priming demolition charges |

All four have excellent water resistance; fume toxicity is slight for PETN, C-4, and det cord, moderate for RDX.

**Initiating systems.** The best way to prime is with **modernized demolition initiators (MDIs)** — blasting caps attached to various lengths of shock tube or detonating cord, used with a fuze igniter. Five MDI blasting-cap types replace the old M6 electric and M7 nonelectric caps. A **shock tube** is a thin plastic tube with a layer of special explosive material on the inside wall; it transmits the initiation signal without an electric current. MDI caps include the M11 (with J-hook for quick attachment to detonating cord) and the M14 (high strength). Firing systems are built as **dual initiating systems** (redundancy — two independent ways to fire the same charge) and the British J system. The chapter also covers the demolitions effects simulator charge (a training device built with an uli knot and 12–18 inches of detonating cord). **Safety (section 5-10) is a standalone section, not an afterthought** — demolitions work is done by the book, with dual initiation where the mission allows, because a misfire in the field is a casualty, not an inconvenience. → `references/fire-support-and-demolitions.md`

## 6. Movement — formations, techniques, danger areas

**Formations** (Figure 6-1): column, line, wedge, vee, echelon left/right, staggered column, herringbone, coil. Each trades security for control and firepower in a different direction; the wedge gives all-around security and is the default patrol formation, the line maximizes firepower to the front, the column maximizes control and speed on a trail.

**Movement techniques:** traveling (speed, low security), traveling overwatch (a middle ground — the lead element moves steadily while the trail element overwatches from a position where it can support), and **bounding overwatch** (maximum security — one element stationary and overwatching while the other bounds forward; squad and platoon examples in Figures 6-2 and 6-3). Technique is chosen by METT-TC, above all by the likelihood of contact.

**Danger areas** are any place the patrol is exposed to enemy observation or fire — linear (roads, trails, streams), small open areas, large open areas. Crossing a linear danger area: near-side security posted, far-side security crosses first and clears, then the main body crosses in file at the designated point, fast and quiet. A small open danger area is crossed by bypassing if possible; if it must be crossed, the same near/far security drill applies with the crossing point chosen where exposure is shortest. **Hand and arm signals** for movement are in chapter 6 (assemble/rally, join me, enemy in sight, take cover, map check, halt, take a knee — see the visual-signals skill for the full set). → `references/patrols-movement-and-tccc.md`

## 7. Patrols — recon, combat, ambush, raid, patrol base

**Principles.** Patrols are planned with TLP and the estimate of the situation. Every patrol has a leader, a plan, a rally point, and a rehearsal. The two kinds: **reconnaissance patrols** (area, zone, route — gather information, avoid decisive engagement) and **combat patrols** (raid, ambush, security — destroy the enemy, capture, or secure).

**The ambush** is the patrol's signature fight: a surprise attack from a concealed position on a moving or temporarily halted enemy. The kill zone is the ground the enemy must be in when the ambush is sprung; the support element provides the base of fire, the assault element closes and finishes, the security element seals the flanks and the far side. An ambush is rehearsed on a terrain model and on the ground, and the patrol's actions after the ambush — clearing the kill zone, searching the dead, collecting EPWs and intelligence, withdrawing on time — are planned before the first shot.

**The raid** is a surprise attack on a fixed objective, ending in a planned withdrawal. The patrol-base chapter gives the **priorities of work** on occupying a patrol base: security first (perimeter, sectors of fire, OPs/LPs), then weapons maintenance, then rest, hygiene, water, and food — in that order, and enforced by the PSG. A patrol base is never occupied the same way twice, and it is always occupied with an alternate base selected and a withdrawal plan ready.

**Movement to contact** is the deliberate movement to find and fix the enemy when his location is uncertain — planned with the same TLP discipline, with contact drills rehearsed so the first seconds of a fight are automatic. **Task standards** (section 7-32) are the measurable pass/fail for each patrol task — the handbook is a training standard as much as a reference. → `references/patrols-movement-and-tccc.md`

## 8. Battle drills — the automatic response

Chapter 8 is the set of **battle drills** — collective actions trained to automaticity so that under fire nobody has to think about what to do first. The drills include reacting to direct-fire contact (platoon and squad, tasks 07-PLT-D9501 / 07-SQD-D9501), reacting to indirect fire, reacting to contact while mounted, breaking contact, reacting to ambush (near and far), reacting to a sniper, and conducting actions on the objective. The pattern in every drill is the same: return fire, seek cover, report (SALUTE), maneuver, and reorganize — in that order, fast, loud, and together. A drill is not learned until it can be executed in the dark, under stress, without an order. → `references/patrols-movement-and-tccc.md`

## 9. Machine gun employment

**Specifications (Table 10-1):**

| | M249 SAW | M240B | M2 (.50 cal) | MK19 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Caliber | 5.56 mm | 7.62 mm | .50 cal | 40 mm grenade |
| Weight (gun + tripod) | 16.4 lb + 16 lb | 27.6 lb + 20 lb | 128 lb w/ tripod | 140.6 lb w/ tripod |
| Max range | 3,600 m | 3,725 m | 6,764 m | 2,212 m |
| Max effective, bipod point / area | 600 / 800 m | 600 / 800 m | 1,500 m point | 1,500 m point |
| Max effective, tripod point / area | 800 / 1,000 m | 800 / 1,100 m | 1,830 m area | 2,212 m area |
| Grazing fire | 600 m | 600 m | 700 m | — |
| Tracer burnout | 900 m | 900 m | 1,800 m | none |
| Sustained rate | 50 rpm | 100 rpm | 40 rpm | 40 rpm |
| Rapid rate | 100 rpm | 200 rpm | 40 rpm | 60 rpm |

**Classes of fire** (by direction, elevation, and distribution): fixed, traversing, searching, traversing-and-searching, swinging traverse, and free gun. **Employment:** in the offense the PL establishes a base-of-fire element with the M240B/M249 (and MK19/M2 where available) while the assault element maneuvers; in the defense, guns are sited for interlocking, grazing fire across the platoon's front, with the M2 and MK19 particularly effective against lightly armored vehicles. On level or uniformly sloping terrain the M240B and M249 achieve a maximum of 600 meters of grazing fire; the M2 achieves 700 meters. **Control:** the WSL designates the principal direction of fire and secondary sectors for every gun, and shifts, corrects, and alternates guns to sustain fire without burning out barrels — sustained rate means 6–9-round bursts, 4–5 seconds, every 10 minutes for the M249/M240B. → `references/fire-support-and-demolitions.md`

## 10. Urban, waterborne, mounted, and aviation operations

**Urban operations (ch. 11):** analyze the urban environment by structure type and function before entering it; close-quarters combat is a distinct skill set — room clearing, stairwells, and rooftops — and the handbook's rule is that precision weapons (M4s, not M249s) are used where civilians may be present.

**Waterborne operations (ch. 12):** the **rope bridge** is the patrol's way across a water obstacle — one rope across, Rangers crossing one at a time with weapons and rucksacks clipped on by carabiner (M240/M249 secured by carabiners on front sight post and rear swivel, pulled across by the gunner). The **poncho raft** is the expedient floatation for a rucksack and weapon. Capsize procedures are rehearsed before the patrol gets wet. River movement uses formations and navigation adapted to current and bank.

**Mounted patrol operations (ch. 13):** planning, the mounted tactical movement brief, reacting to near and far ambush (Figures 13-1/13-2), forced stops (two methods), and breaking contact. **Aviation (ch. 14):** the reverse planning sequence for air assault, pickup-zone operations, air assault formations, and rotary-wing aircraft specifications — the patrol plans backward from the time on the objective. **Mountaineering (ch. 9):** training and planning, dismounted mobility in steep terrain, task organization, rescue equipment, and mountaineering equipment — the companion skill `mountaineering` (TC 3-97.61) goes deep; this chapter is the patrol-level primer. → `references/patrols-movement-and-tccc.md`

## 11. Tactical combat casualty care and evacuation

**Goals and phases.** TCCC has three phases: **care under fire** (return fire first, stop life-threatening hemorrhage, move the casualty to cover), **tactical field care** (the MARCH PAWS assessment and treatment sequence), and **tactical evacuation care** (monitoring and treatment continue until higher care takes over). The medic documents everything on the **DD Form 1380 (TCCC Card)**.

**Casualty collection points (CCPs)** are planned before contact: a marked, covered location where casualties are gathered, triaged (Figure 15-1, triage triangle), and prepared for evacuation. A **mass casualty incident** is handled by the same triage discipline at scale, and **field sanitation** — water, waste, hygiene — is a medical task, not a housekeeping one.

**CASEVAC vs. MEDEVAC.** CASEVAC is any non-medical platform (manual carries, litter teams, a vehicle) — physically demanding, no en route care unless preplanned and staffed, no dedicated medical equipment, and no Geneva Convention protection as a medical vehicle. MEDEVAC is a dedicated medical platform with medical personnel, properly marked and protected under the Geneva Conventions — and therefore **may not be equipped with crew-served weapons**. A 9-line MEDEVAC request is always transmitted by secure means.

**Evacuation precedence (Table 15-1):** Priority I — URGENT: evacuate as soon as possible, within **1 hour**, to save life, limb, or eyesight. Priority IA — URGENT-SURG: same hour limit plus far-forward surgical intervention. Priority II — PRIORITY: within **4 hours**. Priority III — ROUTINE: within **24 hours**. Priority IV — CONVENIENCE: a matter of medical convenience, not necessity.

**The 9-line MEDEVAC request** (Appendix B): the lines cover pickup-site grid, radio frequency/call sign/suffix, number of patients by precedence, special equipment required, number of ambulatory vs. litter patients, security at the pickup site (wartime) or number/types of wounds (peacetime), marking of the pickup site, patient nationality and status, and CBRN contamination. Append a **MIST report** (mechanism of injury, injury type, signs, treatment) so the receiving facility arrives informed. **HLZ markings:** the **NATO T** — center marker, then markers 10 meters left, 10 meters right, 10 meters down, and 10 more meters down; the **inverted Y** — center marker, then 7 meters left, 7 meters right, 14 meters up, and 7 more meters up. → `references/patrols-movement-and-tccc.md`

## Output Contract

When answering from this skill, lead with the doctrine that decides the next action: the TLP step the leader is on, the RED that governs the fire mission, the precedence that governs the evacuation, or the drill that governs the contact. Give the numbers (REDs, rates of fire, precedence times, HLZ dimensions) exactly as tabulated — never round them from memory. Name the role responsible (PL, PSG, SL, WSL, TL, medic, RTO, FO) for every task you assign. If the question crosses into a companion manual, say so and point to it: TCCC depth lives in `tccc-first-aid` (ATP 4-02.11), signaling depth in `visual-signals` (TC 3-21.60), mountaineering depth in `mountaineering` (TC 3-97.61), survival depth in `survival` (ATP 3-50.21).

## Operating Rules

1. **Every Ranger prepares to take command.** Assumption of command is a checklist, not an improvisation — inform, check security and guns, pinpoint location, check people and equipment, issue FRAGORDs, reorganize, finalize.
2. **1/3 : 2/3 — never hoard the clock.** The leader plans in one-third of the time; subordinates get two-thirds to prepare.
3. **Danger close is 600 meters for unguided mortars and artillery.** REDs are 0.1-percent probability numbers, not guesses — use the table.
4. **Security first, always.** Patrol base, halt, danger area, objective — security is the first priority of work and the last thing dropped.
5. **Rehearse until automatic.** Battle drills, ambushes, danger-area crossings, and capsize procedures are trained to execution in the dark, under stress, without an order.
6. **No Ranger left behind, and no casualty unreported.** LACE, SALUTE, DA Form 1156, DD Form 1380, the 9-line — the paperwork is the system that gets people home.
