## Chapter 6 — Movement: outline

- Hand and arm signals (Figures 6-6 to 6-12+).

## Formations — what each buys

| Formation | Best for | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Column | Speed, control, trail movement | Little firepower to the flanks; vulnerable from the side |
| Line | Maximum firepower to the front | Hard to control; exposed flanks |
| Wedge | All-around security, flexible | Default patrol formation |
| Vee | Firepower to the front and flanks | Harder to control than wedge |
| Echelon left/right | Firepower to one flank | Weak on the opposite flank |
| Staggered column | Firepower to both flanks, good control | Slower than column |
| Herringbone | Hasty perimeter at a halt | Static — a halt formation, not a movement formation |
| Coil | Security while halted in a confined area | Static |

## Movement techniques

- **Traveling:** all elements move at once. Fastest, least secure. Used when contact is unlikely.
- **Traveling overwatch:** the lead element moves steadily; the trail element moves to successive positions from which it can overwatch and support the lead. A compromise — speed with a safety net.
- **Bounding overwatch:** one element is stationary and overwatching while the other bounds forward to the next covered position, then the roles reverse. Slowest, most secure. Used when contact is likely or expected. Squad bounding overwatch (Figure 6-2) and platoon bounding overwatch (Figure 6-3) are the two patterns; the platoon version uses one squad overwatching while the other two bound, or one bounding while two overwatch, depending on the threat.

Technique is chosen by METT-TC — above all, by the likelihood of contact. A patrol that travels through likely contact is gambling; a patrol that bounds through empty ground is wasting time.

## Danger areas

A danger area is any place the patrol is exposed to enemy observation or fire: roads, trails, streams (linear), clearings (small open), fields and lakes (large open).

**Linear danger area crossing:**
1. Near-side security is posted (usually the trail squad or team) to cover the crossing point.
2. Far-side security crosses first, clears the far side, and posts security.
3. The main body crosses in file at the designated point — fast, quiet, no bunching.
4. The PSG directs near-side security and maintains accountability of personnel through the crossing.

**Small open danger area:** bypass if possible. If it must be crossed, use the same near/far security drill, choosing the crossing point where exposure is shortest (Figure 6-5). **Large open area:** bypass is the norm; crossing is a deliberate operation with overwatch and smoke if available.

**Hand and arm signals (chapter 6):** assemble or rally, join me/follow me/come forward, enemy in sight, take cover, map check, halt, take a knee — executed exactly as in TC 3-21.60 (see the `visual-signals` skill for the complete catalog).

## Reconnaissance patrols

- **Area recon:** information about a specific area (an objective, a hill, a village).
- **Zone recon:** information about a zone (a band of terrain between two boundaries).
- **Route recon:** information about a route (trafficability, obstacles, enemy along it).
- Recon patrols gather information and avoid decisive engagement. If a recon patrol is decisively engaged, it has failed its primary mission — but it fights to break contact and report, in that order.

## Combat patrols

- **Raid:** a surprise attack on a fixed objective, ending in a planned withdrawal. Phases: approach, actions on the objective, withdrawal.
- **Ambush:** a surprise attack from a concealed position on a moving or temporarily halted enemy. The **kill zone** is the ground the enemy must be in when the ambush is sprung. Elements: **support** (base of fire into the kill zone), **assault** (closes on the kill zone and finishes the enemy), **security** (seals the flanks and the far side, covers the withdrawal). The ambush is rehearsed on the terrain model and on the ground. **Actions after the ambush** — clear the kill zone, search the dead, collect EPWs and intelligence, redistribute ammunition, withdraw on time — are planned before the first shot. A patrol that lingers in a kill zone after the ambush becomes the next ambush's target.
- **Security patrol:** screens, guards, or covers a force or area.

## Patrol base — priorities of work

On occupying a patrol base, work is done in this order:

1. **Security** — perimeter established, sectors of fire assigned, interlocking fires, OPs/LPs out, claymores covering dead space, range cards and sector sketches complete.
2. **Weapons maintenance** — clean and function-check weapons (without violating the security plan).
3. **Rest plan** — by rotation, never all at once.
4. **Hygiene, water, messing** — in that order of priority after security and weapons.

The PSG enforces the priority of work and the security plan (alert plan, evacuation plan, withdrawal plan, alternate patrol base, maintenance, hygiene, messing, water, rest). A patrol base is never occupied the same way twice, and an alternate base is always selected with a withdrawal plan ready. Movement and noise are kept to a minimum; camouflage is supervised and enforced.

## Movement to contact

Deliberate movement to find and fix the enemy when his location is uncertain. Planned with the same TLP discipline; contact drills are rehearsed so the first seconds of a fight are automatic. The patrol moves in a formation and technique matched to the expected contact, with a plan for what happens when contact is made — not just a hope that it won't be.

## Chapter 12 — Waterborne operations

- **Rope bridge:** the patrol's way across a water obstacle. One rope rigged across; Rangers cross one at a time. Special items (M240s, M249s, additional rucksacks) are clipped to the bridge. The M240 and M249 are secured to the bridge by carabiners on the front sight post and rear swivel; the M240 is pulled across by the trailing arm of the gunner. Only one Ranger on the rope bridge at a time.
- **Poncho raft:** expedient flotation built from a poncho wrapped around a rucksack — keeps the rucksack and weapon dry and buoyant while the Ranger swims or wades.
- **Watercraft:** rigging weapons to the boat (two carabiners front and rear on the M240; same for the M249, with parachute cord).
- **Capsize procedures:** rehearsed before the patrol gets wet — who does what when the boat goes over, in what order, and how the patrol recovers weapons and equipment.
- **River movement, navigation, and formations:** movement adapted to current, bank, and the patrol's navigation plan.

## TCCC phases

1. **Care under fire:** return fire first. Stop life-threatening external hemorrhage (tourniquet, direct pressure). Move the casualty to cover. Do not attempt an airway intervention under fire.
2. **Tactical field care:** the MARCH PAWS sequence — Massive hemorrhage, Airway, Respiration, Circulation, Hypothermia/Head injury, Pain, Antibiotics, Wounds, Splinting. Treat in that order; reassess after every intervention.
3. **Tactical evacuation care:** monitoring and treatment continue until medical personnel assume care or the casualty reaches higher care. Document everything on the DD Form 1380 (TCCC Card).

**Casualty collection points:** planned before contact — a marked, covered location where casualties are gathered, triaged (Figure 15-1, triage triangle), treated, and prepared for evacuation. The CCP is established and supervised by the PSG with the medic. **Mass casualty incident:** the same triage discipline at scale — sort by who needs evacuation first (urgent), who can wait (priority/routine), and who is expectant. **Field sanitation:** water, waste, hygiene — a medical task, because disease kills patrols that bullets miss.

## CASEVAC vs. MEDEVAC

- **CASEVAC:** any non-medical platform — manual carries, aid-and-litter teams, a vehicle. Physically demanding (litter teams must be rotated). No en route care unless preplanned and staffed. No dedicated medical equipment suite. No Geneva Convention protection as a medical vehicle. Competing requirements (the same vehicle moves ammunition and water).
- **MEDEVAC:** a dedicated medical platform with medical personnel, properly marked and protected under the Geneva Conventions. Because of that protection, **MEDEVAC vehicles may not be equipped with crew-served weapons**.

## Evacuation precedence (Table 15-1)

| Priority | Category | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| I | URGENT | Evacuate as soon as possible, within a maximum of **1 hour**, to save life, limb, or eyesight, prevent complications of serious illness, or avoid permanent disability |
| IA | URGENT-SURG | Same 1-hour limit **plus** far-forward surgical intervention to save life, limb, or eyesight or stabilize for further evacuation |
| II | PRIORITY | Evacuate within **4 hours**; condition could deteriorate to URGENT, requires special treatment not available locally, or would cause unnecessary pain/disability |
| III | ROUTINE | Evacuate within **24 hours**; condition not expected to deteriorate significantly |
| IV | CONVENIENCE | Evacuation by medical vehicle is a matter of medical convenience rather than necessity (deleted from STANAG 3204 but retained in U.S. Army practice) |

## The 9-line MEDEVAC request

Transmitted **always by secure means**. Lines (U.S. format; NATO differs — no URGENT-SURG category; line 6 is always security at the pickup site; line 8 uses military/civilian rather than U.S. military/U.S. civilian):

1. Pickup-site grid coordinates.
2. Radio frequency, call sign, and suffix.
3. Number of patients by evacuation precedence category.
4. Special equipment required.
5. Number of patients — ambulatory vs. litter.
6. Security at the pickup site (wartime) **or** number and type of wounds/injuries/illnesses (peacetime).
7. Method of marking the pickup site.
8. Patient nationality and status (U.S. military, U.S. civilian, etc.).
9. CBRN contamination at the pickup site (wartime) — in peacetime this line carries terrain information.

Append a **MIST report** (mechanism of injury, injury type, signs, treatment) so the receiving facility arrives informed and prepared. Sample formats for both are in Appendix B (Figure B-4). Steps before transmitting: collect all the information (grid, frequency/call sign/suffix, patient counts by precedence, special equipment, ambulatory/litter counts, escort needs, security, markings, nationality/status, CBRN info) and decide up front whether to append MIST.

## HLZ markings

Two methods, both laid out with markers on the ground:

- **NATO T:** center marker; then 10 meters left, 10 meters right, 10 meters down, 10 more meters down. The markers form a capital T.
- **Inverted Y:** center marker; then 7 meters left, 7 meters right, 14 meters up, 7 more meters up. The markers form a Y on its head.

The T and the inverted Y are the two shapes a helicopter crew recognizes from the air. Mark with lights, panels, or chem lights per SOP and the tactical situation — and remember that a marked HLZ is visible to everyone, not just the helicopter.
