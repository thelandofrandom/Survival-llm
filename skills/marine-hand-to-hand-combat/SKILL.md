# Marine Hand-to-Hand Combat

## Purpose

*Marine Hand-to-Hand Combat* (U.S. Marine Corps, NAVMC 1146-403, undated, 18 pages) — a short, illustrated Marine Corps hand-to-hand manual, reproduced here from a scan of the "Poor Man's James Bond" reprint edition. It teaches a compact, aggressive close-combat system in eight sections: principles and vulnerable targets, the offensive-defensive stance and movement, falls and rolls, deflecting and striking blows, throws and take-downs, breaking holds, disarming a bayonet-armed opponent, and knife and club fighting. Every technique ends the same way — with a finishing blow to a vital target (temple, throat, bridge of the nose, groin, kidney, or base of the skull).

**Source-quality warning — read this first.** The only available copy is a low-quality scan of a reprint, and the extracted text is correspondingly rough: the contents page and first pages are heavily garbled by OCR (misread letters, broken words, interleaved two-column text), and a few words in the body are unreadable. The technique descriptions are largely legible and are distilled faithfully below; where the text is garbled, the skill says so rather than guessing. The full verbatim extraction is in `references/full-text.md`. Page numbers cited below are the manual's own printed page numbers.

**Currency warning.** This is a historic manual, not current Marine doctrine. The modern Marine system is the Marine Corps Martial Arts Program (MCMAP); for current technique use the `combatives` skill (TC 3-25.150, 2017). This skill is for the manual's own system: a short, brutal, stand-up syllabus built on deflection, the back edge of the hand, the knee, and the throw — with almost no ground fighting, which most distinguishes it from the Army's 2002 combatives system.

## 1. Introduction — why hand-to-hand, and the targets (Section 1, ¶101–104)

**Importance (¶101).** Hand-to-hand fighting is "of inestimable value" when weapons are unavailable or their use is impractical or impossible, "particularly when silence is desirable." It gives the individual "a most effective means of executing a decisive attack or assault on an opponent, or of adequately defending himself," and mastery "adds immeasurable assurance, confidence, and self-reliance."

**Purpose and scope (¶102).** The manual teaches "some of the techniques of hand-to-hand fighting." Movements are described and illustrated in sequence as a guide for teaching and learning — it is an instructor's manual as much as a fighter's one.

**Instructional procedures (¶103).** The first period of instruction explains that hand-to-hand fighting is designed to place the user in position to use his full strength and leverage against the opponent's vulnerable, vital, or more sensitive areas. The manual lists the vulnerable targets — arch of the foot, knee, groin, temple, ears, nerves under the ears, solar plexus, throat, bridge of the nose, elbow, collarbone, eyes, neck, side of the jaw, base of the spine, small of the back, kidneys — and notes that "the fingers, wrist, and elbow are the easiest bones to break or dislocate." Students must be cautioned not to accidentally injure or immobilize their training partners.

**Principles (¶104).** Seven principles the fighter must constantly bear in mind:
- **a. Be aggressive.**
- **b. Keep eyes on the opponent.**
- **c. Distract the opponent.**
- **d. Disable or be disabled.**
- **e. Vary the attack to fit the situation.**
- **f. Turn the defense into an unrelenting attack.**
- **g. Feel superior to the opponent, regardless of the latter's size or evidence of strength.**

The through-line is the attack: defense is only the opening of the counterattack, and every deflection in the manual is immediately followed by a strike, a knee, or a throw. → `references/stance-movement-falls.md`

## 2. Stance and movement — the offensive-defensive stance (Section 2, ¶201–202)

**The stance (¶201).** The offensive-defensive stance is the base position for everything in the manual: left foot forward, toe pointed slightly inward, left knee slightly bent inward; right foot to the rear and right of the left foot, right toe pointed straight to the front, right knee slightly bent; lean forward from the hips with the weight balanced on both feet. Both hands are raised — the left hand protects the left side of the face and head, the left forearm and elbow the left side of the body, the right hand and forearm the same on the right. Palms face inward, the lower edge of the hands toward the opponent, fingers together, thumbs held tight against the forefingers. "From this position the hand-to-hand fighter is prepared to strike or grasp his opponent."

**Movement (¶202).** All movement preserves the stance in roughly 14-inch steps: **forward** — left foot forward, right follows; **left and forward** — step left-forward with the left foot, pivoting on the ball of the right foot; **right and forward** — step right-forward with the right foot, pivoting on the ball of the left; **left and to the rear** — step back-left with the right foot, pivoting on the ball of the left; **right and to the rear** — step back-left with the left foot, pivoting on the ball of the right; **directly to the rear** — right foot back about 14 inches, left follows.

The footwork is deliberately small and shuffling — the fighter never crosses his feet or takes a long step that would leave him off balance. → `references/stance-movement-falls.md`

## 3. Falls and rolls (Section 3, ¶301–302)

- **Forward roll (¶301).** Two steps for momentum, dive, chin against the left shoulder, left hand at the left side of and slightly above the head, right arm slightly bent to break the fall with hand and forearm; roll forward on the right shoulder and back; on foot contact, spin left to face the rear, in the offensive-defensive stance.
- **Backward fall (¶302).** Drop back onto the buttocks as in sitting, breaking the shock with the hands and forearms; head on the chest; raise the feet and swing them over the head in a backward somersault when the hands touch the ground; land on the knees or feet facing the opponent, in stance immediately. Both end in the fighting stance, facing the opponent — fall without injury and come up fighting. → `references/stance-movement-falls.md`

## 4. Deflecting and striking blows (Section 4, ¶401–405)

Every defense follows one pattern: **deflect, grasp, strike the vital target, and (often) throw**. The signature strike is the back edge of the hand to the temple, throat, jaw, or base of the skull; the signature follow-up is the knee to the groin.

- **Right hand blow (¶401).** Deflect the straight right to the right with the left hand or forearm, grasp the wrist with the left hand, pull him forward, strike the temple, throat, or side of the neck with the back edge of the right hand.
- **Left hand blow (¶402).** Deflect left with the right hand or forearm, grasp his left arm with the left hand, shift forward and right, strike the temple, jaw, or base of the skull with the back edge of the right hand, using the shift's momentum.
- **Wide swinging right, roundhouse (¶403).** Block with the left hand or forearm, grasp his right wrist or arm with the left hand and pull him forward, striking simultaneously in the groin with the right knee and between the eyes with the back edge of the right hand; pivot left, throwing him over the right thigh or knee.
- **Attacking an opponent (¶404).** Against a threatened left-hand blow, strike the outside of his left wrist with the left hand, shift right and forward, strike the temple, jaw, or base of the skull with the right hand.
- **Deflecting kicks (¶405).** Against a right-foot kick, shift forward and left, deflect the kick right and upward with the left hand, swing or raise his right leg upward, knocking him on his back — then break the leg, kick the groin, or deliver a "karate blow" to the throat or bridge of the nose. Against a left-foot kick, shift right and do the same. → `references/deflecting-striking-throws.md`

## 5. Throws and take-downs (Section 5, ¶501–503)

- **From the opponent's left (¶501).** Grasp his left wrist with the left hand and step in with the right foot; drive the right knee into the outside of his left knee while striking the temple with the outer edge of the right hand; place the right hand on his face, push him backward over the fighter's right knee or thigh and drop him; finish with a blow to the bridge of the nose or throat.
- **From the left, alternate method (¶502).** Grasp the left wrist, step forward with the right foot, knee the outside of the left knee, pivot under his left arm, grasp the inside of his left leg at the knee, and drive the elbow into the solar plexus; lift with the right hand, release the wrist as he falls backward; follow with a knee to his left side and a right-hand blow to the throat or bridge of the nose.
- **From the opponent's right (¶503).** Grasp his right wrist with the left hand, raising it above the head while bending forward; drive the shoulder into his solar plexus while stepping forward with the right foot; as he leans forward, grasp his right leg at the knee, raise him and swing him over the shoulder, retaining the wrist grip; once down, strike temple, throat, or bridge of the nose.

The take-downs are not wrestling for position — each is a knee strike and a face strike built into the throw, and each ends with the opponent on the ground and the fighter delivering a finishing blow. → `references/deflecting-striking-throws.md`

## 6. Breaking holds (Section 6, ¶601–609)

Nine holds, one method: grip the attacking limb, unbalance the opponent with a pivot or twist, break the hold, and finish with a blow to a vital target.

- **Wrist hold (¶601).** Against a two-handed front wrist hold: pull back with the right hand and step forward with the left foot, pushing forward and upward with the left hand; grasp the opponent's left elbow with the left hand, shift right and forward; finish with a blow to the temple, jaw, neck, or kidney.
- **Rear neck hold (¶602).** Grasp the opponent's right elbow with the right hand and his left elbow with the left; pull downward with the left hand and upward with the right, pivoting left as the opponent is forced to twist down and over; follow with a knee blow or throw.
- **Rear choke hold (¶603).** Grasp the opponent's left hand or wrist with the left hand and pivot left, retaining the grip; finish with a blow to the temple, jaw, or throat. (Reverse if he uses his left hand.)
- **Front choke hold (¶604).** Grasp the opponent's right wrist or hand with the left hand, shift left and pivot on the right foot, forcing his hand off and down to the right; finish with a blow to the temple, jaw, or throat.
- **Crossed-wrist front choke hold (¶605).** Grasp his right hand or wrist with the right hand, shift left and forward, pull down with the right hand and lift up with the left hand on his right elbow; finish with a knee to the kidney or a blow to the neck.
- **Rear head lock (¶606).** Grasp his left elbow with the left hand and his left knee with the right; step forward and left, pull right and upward, forcing him off balance and down on his side; finish with a blow to the temple or throat.
- **Front head lock (¶607).** Slide the right hand and arm over the opponent's right elbow with the left hand; push down with the right shoulder and pivot left, forcing him to break the hold; follow with a throw, a blow to the groin, or break his right arm.
- **Hammerlock (¶608).** Step forward with the right foot, bend forward from the waist, then rapidly twist to the left, breaking the hold; finish with a blow to the temple, jaw, or kidney.
- **Come-along (¶609).** Against any come-along hold — bending fingers downward or hand upward — lift upward with the free hand against his hand(s) while driving the right knee into the back of his left knee, forcing him to break the hold and knocking him on his back; follow with a knee or a blow to the bridge of the nose or throat. (If he holds the left hand and arm, use the left knee against his left knee.) → `references/breaking-holds-weapons.md`

## 7. Disarming an opponent armed with a rifle and bayonet (Section 7, ¶701–703)

- **Slow approach — bayonet low (¶701).** Sidestep, reach forward and down with the left hand, hit the weapon on the inside and deflect the bayonet left with a pushing movement; shift right and forward, strike the temple, neck, or left elbow.
- **Slow approach — bayonet high (¶702).** Reach out with the left hand, hit the weapon and push it left; quickly shift right and forward, grasp the rifle near the stock with the right hand and wrest it away; finish with a blow to the elbow, temple, or neck.
- **Charging opponent (¶703).** Shift left and forward to avoid the bayonet, hit and push the weapon downward and to the right with the left hand; finish with a blow to the neck. The rule throughout: never meet the point — step off its line, deflect the weapon with the hand, and strike the man. → `references/breaking-holds-weapons.md`

## 8. Knife and club fighting (Section 8, ¶801–803)

- **Frontal attack with the knife (¶801).** Offensive-defensive stance, knife in the right hand, point toward the opponent, blade flat, cutting edge outward; advance with the left hand high to protect the face or strike. If he grasps the knife hand, strike the bridge of the nose; shift left, place the left hand under his right elbow and lift the arm, plunge the knife upward into the body above the belt, and slash outward or to the right.
- **Frontal disarming — knife or club (¶802).** (a) Against a thrust, shift forward and left, deflect the knife by pushing it with the left hand, strike the back of his hand with the outer edge of the right hand, follow with an arm break or a blow to the jaw, throat, or collarbone. (b) Step in with the left foot, strike the inside of his right wrist with the left hand or wrist to deflect the thrust left, then strike the bridge of the nose with the right hand and simultaneously knee the groin; follow by breaking his arm or throwing him.
- **Attack from the rear (¶803).** (a) With a knife: place a hand over his mouth and face and pull back, plunging the knife upward into his back above the belt. (b) With a club: place the left hand on his arm or shoulder, turn him slightly left, and deliver the blow(s) to the base of the skull.

The knife and club section is the manual's most lethal material, and its most dated: the rear attack (¶803) is a sentry-removal technique, and it assumes a battlefield context in which silence and lethality are the point. Do not mistake it for a self-defense syllabus. → `references/breaking-holds-weapons.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Aggression is the system.** Be aggressive; turn every defense into an unrelenting attack.
2. **Eyes on the opponent, always.** The stance protects the face and body; the eyes never leave the threat.
3. **Deflect, grasp, finish.** Every defense ends with a strike to a vital target — temple, throat, nose, groin, kidney, base of the skull.
4. **Small steps, never crossed feet.** Fourteen-inch shuffling steps keep the fighter balanced and in stance.
5. **Fall into the stance.** Rolls and falls end facing the opponent, in the offensive-defensive stance.
6. **Historic manual, not current doctrine.** The modern Marine system is MCMAP; this manual is a period source.
