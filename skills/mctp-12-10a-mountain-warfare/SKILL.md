# Mountain Warfare

## Purpose

MCTP 12-10A *Mountain Warfare* (U.S. Marine Corps, July 2024, 174 pages) is the Marine Corps' tactical publication for the Marine Air-Ground Task Force (MAGTF) fighting in mountains. Where the Army's ATP 3-90.97 covers the same ground from an Army staff perspective, MCTP 12-10A speaks Marine: expeditionary, combined-arms, and built around the reality that a Marine unit must project force rapidly, sustain itself, and accomplish its mission in any clime and place — including the steepest, coldest terrain on earth. This skill distills it into usable doctrine for a Marine leader or planner: how mountains change operations, intelligence, maneuver, engineering, logistics, aviation, fires, communications, and training.

Core idea: **mountains establish formal and informal boundaries and barriers.** When conflicts erupt in rugged areas, commanders face unique operational challenges — mountainous terrain, snow, and cold weather — that punish every assumption made on flat ground. The MAGTF satisfies rapid-deployment requirements only if its leaders address the environmental challenges and obtain the requisite skills, special equipment, and training. Units must prepare for most contingencies and the unique challenges each contingency brings.

This skill covers all ten chapters and the appendices: overview, operations, intelligence/surveillance/reconnaissance, maneuver and movement, engineering, logistics and sustainment, aviation, fires, communications, training considerations, and environmental threats unique to mountains.

## 1. Overview — The MAGTF in Mountains

Mountains establish boundaries and barriers, and the Marine Corps — an expeditionary force designed to fight in any clime and place — must be able to project force rapidly, sustain itself, and accomplish missions in any conditions. That requirement drives everything in this publication.

- **Environmental challenges are the mission's first problem.** Terrain, snow, and cold weather are not background conditions — they are the dominant factor in planning, and they must be addressed before tactics.
- **Skills, equipment, and training are prerequisites, not enhancements.** A unit that has not trained for mountain operations, and has not obtained the special equipment mountain operations require, cannot be made ready by a good plan.
- **History's lesson:** mountain campaigns have repeatedly produced large numbers of casualties from frostbite and other preventable cold-weather injuries. The injuries are preventable — which means they are a leadership failure when they occur.
- **The MAGTF's advantage is combined arms** — ground, aviation, and logistics in one force — but combined arms in mountains requires more coordination, more planning, and more patience than on flat ground, because every element is degraded by the same environment.

## 2. Operations — Fighting the Mountain First

Mountain operations share the characteristics described in the Army's doctrine — compartmentalization, canalized movement, weather dominance, logistics as the limiting factor — with Marine-specific emphasis on expeditionary tempo and the MAGTF's structure.

- **Compartmentalization:** the battlefield breaks into isolated pockets. Companies and platoons fight semi-independently, often out of sight and out of contact. Mission command is the only workable model.
- **Terrain levels:** Level I (valley floors and main lines of communication), Level II (between valleys and mountain shoulders), and Level III (summit regions). Movement in Level II terrain dictates that elements secure the high ground in Level III terrain. Level III is the most difficult to achieve and maintain, but provides opportunities for well-trained, acclimatized units to attack the enemy from the flanks and rear.
- **Offense and defense:** the same principles as ATP 3-21.50 apply — the attacker fights uphill against an enemy who holds observation and fields of fire; the defender on a reverse slope is nearly invisible until the enemy crests. The Marine emphasis is on the **ambush and the raid** as the characteristic mountain actions for small units: a larger force can be defeated by a smaller one that owns the terrain, the timing, and the withdrawal route.
- **Exploitation and pursuit:** air assault and attack helicopter units can augment exploitation, but their use depends on suitable, secure landing zones and favorable weather. Commanders must prevent the overextension that mountains invite — a pursuing force that outruns its logistics in mountains is a force that will be cut off.
- **Reserves:** helicopters can deploy reserves, but only if LZs and weather cooperate. Ground reserves in mountains move slowly — commit them early or do not count on them.

## 3. Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance

- **Terrain and weather dominate.** The enemy's options are as constrained as yours — the same passes, the same ridgelines, the same water sources.
- **Collection is degraded:** sensors are masked by terrain, weather grounds aerial platforms, and communications are degraded. Patrols, observation posts, and human sources become primary.
- **Surveillance missions** can be flown by attack and reconnaissance helicopters and unmanned systems, but limited visibility (night, rain, snow) degrades them sharply.
- **The enemy's logistics run through the same few routes as yours.** Find the routes and you find his vulnerabilities — and his resupply is as fragile as your own.

## 4. Maneuver and Movement — Time, Elevation, and Trail Breaking

### The time-distance formula

The planning tool, assuming acclimatized forces. Planners account for: altitude of operations, time the unit has had to acclimate, weight each individual carries, unit fitness level, size of the unit, and altitude gain or loss.

- **Foot marches in the mountains are measured in time and elevation, rather than just distance.**
- **Map distance plus one-third** = good estimate of actual ground distance.
- **One hour per 3 kilometers** (about 2 miles).
- Add **one hour per 300 meters (1,000 ft) of ascent** or each **600 meters (2,000 ft) of descent**.
- Place the **shortest person (and shortest gait) in the front** of the formation to maintain a uniform march rate.
- Dismounted movement is slow and requires skilled mountaineering teams to secure the advance. Proper movement techniques and formations and constant security are keys to successful dismounted movement.

### Trail breaking

- When moving through undisturbed (unbroken) snow greater than **30 centimeters (12 inches)**, the lead 2 or 3 persons must pack the snow for the rest of the file. Once accomplished, the trail becomes broken.
- The lead persons exert more effort breaking trail, so they need to be **cycled to the rear of the formation every 15 to 30 minutes** — similar to a "last-man-up" rotation.
- If dismounted troops deploy into wedge formation outside the file formation, the unit moves more slowly — with no one breaking the trail, all personnel move through undisturbed snow.
- Unless the terrain permits mounted movement or sufficient helicopter lift is committed, dismounted movement is the only way to move in mountains — and it is slow.

### Dismounted movement rates

Same table as the Army publications: on foot with no snow cover 2–3 kph cross-country, 3–4 kph on trails; on foot in more than a foot of snow 0.5–1 kph unbroken, 2–3 kph broken; snowshoe 1.5–3 kph unbroken, 3–4 kph broken; skiing 1.5–5 kph unbroken, 5–6 kph broken; skijoring 8–24 kph broken (15 kph maximum recommended for safety). → `references/movement-and-route-planning.md`

## 5. Engineering — Building and Clearing in the Mountains

- **Mobility:** route clearance, road and trail improvement, bridging, avalanche control, and landing-zone preparation. Engineers must be prepared to consider LZs for aviation assets.
- **Landing zones:** engineers design LZs with drainage in mind. An LZ should usually be on the **windward side** of mountain ridges or peaks to ensure reasonably smooth airflow — but in forward areas, concealment from enemy observation is more important. Sites must be well clear of structures and vegetation vulnerable to rotor downwash, particularly in confined spaces.
- **Countermobility:** natural obstacles do most of the work; engineers add mines, demolitions, and wire to canalize the enemy.
- **Survivability:** digging in frozen ground is slow; explosives may be needed. Cold degrades equipment — batteries lose capacity, lubricants thicken, fuels sit at air temperature.

## 6. Logistics and Sustainment — Feeding the Mountain Force

### Calories

- Mountain operations can demand **6,000 or more calories per day**. Despite the greater caloric requirement, high altitude reduces the appetite — leaders must enforce eating.
- The standard MRE contains **1,250 calories** and is insufficient for mountain operations. At high altitudes, an individual would need to consume **more than the standard three MREs per day** to meet energy demands. Each MRE case contains 12 meals and weighs approximately **22 pounds**.
- The meal, cold weather (MCW) ration contains approximately **1,540-calorie menu bags** with a main meal that is dehydrated.
- The first strike ration (FSR) contains **2,850 calories in one ration**. To meet increased calorie needs at high altitude, the FSR needs supplemental items (fruit, vegetables, milk) or the addition of one MRE to increase calories and menu variety.
- The modular operational ration enhancement augments the MRE or FSR with additional calories and nutrients — it is not a replacement.
- Keep food warm where possible, such as by carrying it inside the clothing. Items not kept warm freeze and cannot be eaten.

### Water

- Plan on **four quarts per day stationary, up to eight quarts per day moving**, plus about **two quarts per individual in high mountains** (the same planning factors as ATP 3-90.97).
- Water sources are often frozen, contaminated, or far below the position. Melting snow costs fuel and time — plan for both.

### Acclimatization

- Acclimatization is a logistics and planning factor, not a medical afterthought (see Chapter 6, page 6-20). Personnel must be acclimatized and in a high state of physical fitness, with specialized equipment maintained, before operations at altitude. No shortcuts exist; the schedule governs the mission, not the reverse. See ATP 3-90.97 for the timeline (80% of respiratory acclimatization in 7–10 days; 80–90% overall in two weeks to one month; work high, sleep low).

## 7. Aviation — Helicopters at Altitude

- **Lift is reduced at altitude.** Altitudes restrict helicopter lift capabilities and decrease aircraft payloads. Commanders must plan for fewer Marines and less cargo per lift than at sea level.
- **Weather** grounds aircraft for hours or days. Plan for the window, not the wish.
- **Landing zones:**
  - An LZ should be as level as possible and **may not be on a slope exceeding 7 degrees** in most situations.
  - On slopes **greater than 7 degrees, a helicopter cannot land** — but if rotors are clear of obstructions, it may be able to load and unload in a low hover.
  - Aircrews that experience difficulty landing due to extreme slope angles may request repositioning to a less drastic slope.
  - Snow-covered LZs can be marked with colored dye, timber "letters," or smoke — but should be camouflaged with more snow when not in use.
  - In soft snow, a suitable reference point (such as a vehicle) should be available to the pilot to avoid disorientation in recirculating snow during descent.
  - **Stamping snow to form LZs is dangerous:** compacted snow forms a crust when frozen, which the downdraft of large helicopters breaks up, creating a foreign-object damage hazard.
  - Helicopter-blown sand or ice is a hazard to Marines and weapons, particularly optics. Downwash in freezing conditions can cause frostbite to exposed skin.
  - Fine, soft dirt on frequently trafficked LZs can cause brownout. Prevent it with crushed rock, matting pads, heavy sprays of water, glue or grease-based dust abatement products, or commercial products approved by aviation leadership.
- **Attack reconnaissance helicopter operations** provide fires and surveillance, but are limited by the same altitude and weather constraints.

## 8. Fires — Shooting Over Ridges

- **Masking:** ridges block trajectories. Multiple firing positions are needed to cover masked targets.
- **Observation:** forward observers must be on high ground with observation of the avenues of approach.
- **Reverse slopes:** the enemy on a reverse slope is shielded from direct fire and often from indirect fire. Plan fires to cover the crest and the approaches.
- **Aviation fires** (close air support) supplement artillery but are weather-dependent. Plan fires early, register targets on the approaches, and never assume a fire mission will be available when needed.
- **Space and terrain** limit firing positions: in mountains, there may be only one or two places a gun can be sited to cover a given target — and the enemy knows them too.

## 9. Communications — The Mountain Eats Radios

- Terrain masking creates dead zones; plan retransmission sites on high ground.
- Weather (snow, rain, fog, electrical storms) degrades propagation and can knock out links.
- Cold kills batteries — keep them warm, carry spares.
- Redundancy is the plan: radio, runner, visual signal, wire. Never depend on a single means.
- Messengers must be properly trained in the use of snowshoes or skis and familiar with the route — a runner who cannot move in snow is not a backup.

## 10. Training Considerations

- **Training is the prerequisite.** Mountain operations require specialized skills that cannot be improvised: mountaineering, cold-weather survival, avalanche awareness, skiing and snowshoeing, rope work.
- **Progression:** individual skills first, then team skills, then collective tasks. Skills degrade fast without rehearsal.
- **Acclimatization and training run concurrently** — training during acclimatization is part of the acclimatization process.
- **Environmental threats** (Appendix A) unique to mountains — altitude, cold, avalanche, whiteout, crevasse, rockfall — must be trained against, not just briefed. A threat the unit has not trained for is a threat the unit will not survive.
- **Physical fitness** is the foundation: personnel must be in a high state of physical fitness, acclimatized, and have specialized equipment maintained. Fitness is not a substitute for acclimatization — the fittest Marine still degrades at altitude without it.

## Output Contract

When asked about Marine mountain warfare, answer with the MAGTF lens: (1) name the terrain level and the governing constraint (altitude, cold, slope, weather, LZ availability); (2) give the planning numbers (time-distance formula, movement rates, 6,000+ calories/day, MRE/FSR/MCW values, 7-degree LZ limit, 15–30 minute trail-breaking rotation); (3) state the sustainment and aviation constraints that bound the operation; (4) cite the chapter. Never recommend an operation above the unit's acclimatization and training level without flagging the gap. If the user asks about aviation support, lead with the LZ slope limit and the altitude lift penalty before giving the tactical answer.

## Operating Rules

1. **The mountain is the first enemy.** Terrain, snow, and cold are the dominant planning factor — address them before tactics.
2. **Time and elevation, not distance.** Map distance plus one-third; one hour per 3 km; one hour per 305 m of ascent or 610 m of descent.
3. **Rotate the trail breakers.** Lead 2–3 persons pack snow over 30 cm; cycle them to the rear every 15–30 minutes.
4. **Six thousand calories is the plan, not the exception.** MREs alone are insufficient at altitude — enforce eating, carry the MCW or FSR, and keep food warm.
5. **Seven degrees is the LZ limit.** Above it, a helicopter cannot land — hover load/unload only, rotors clear.
6. **Preventable cold injuries are a leadership failure.** Frostbite has killed more mountain troops than the enemy has — and it is preventable.
