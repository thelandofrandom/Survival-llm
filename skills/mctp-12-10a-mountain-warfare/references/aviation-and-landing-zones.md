# Aviation and Landing Zones — MCTP 12-10A

Source: MCTP 12-10A, Chapters 5 and 7. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Helicopter Lift at Altitude

- Altitudes restrict helicopter lift capabilities and decrease aircraft payloads.
- Plan for fewer Marines and less cargo per lift than at sea level.
- Weather grounds aircraft for hours or days — plan for the window, not the wish.
- Attack reconnaissance helicopter operations provide fires and surveillance but are limited by the same altitude and weather constraints.

## Landing Zone Selection

- An LZ should usually be on the **windward side** of mountain ridges or peaks to ensure reasonably smooth airflow.
- In forward areas, **concealment from enemy observation is more important** than windward placement.
- Sites must be well clear of structures and vegetation vulnerable to rotor downwash, particularly in confined spaces.
- Drainage matters: engineers must consider drainage when designing an LZ.
- An LZ should be relatively clear of fine dirt or sand, which often develops on spaces frequently trafficked by personnel and heavy vehicles.

## Slope Limits

- An LZ should be as level as possible and **may not be on a slope exceeding 7 degrees** in most situations.
- On slopes **greater than 7 degrees, a helicopter cannot land**.
- If rotors are clear of obstructions, a helicopter may be able to **load and unload in a low hover** on a steeper slope.
- Aircrews that experience difficulty landing due to extreme slope angles may request repositioning to a less drastic slope.

## Snow-Covered LZs

- Snow-covered LZs can be marked with colored dye, timber "letters," or smoke — but should be camouflaged with more snow when not in use.
- In soft snow, a suitable reference point (such as a vehicle) should be available to the pilot to avoid disorientation in recirculating snow during descent.
- **Stamping snow to form LZs is dangerous:** compacted snow forms a crust when frozen, which the downdraft of large helicopters breaks up, creating a foreign-object damage hazard.
- If time permits, the area should be damped with water or other dust abatement material.

## Brownout and Whiteout

- Fine, soft dirt on a flat LZ can cause **brownout** conditions for landing and departing aircraft — worse with confined approach and departure paths due to surrounding steep terrain.
- Prevent brownout/whiteout disorientation with: crushed rock, matting pads, heavy sprays of water, glue or grease-based dust abatement products, or commercial products approved by aviation leadership.
- Helicopter-blown sand or ice is a hazard to Marines and weapons, particularly optics.
- Downwash in freezing conditions can cause **frostbite to exposed skin** — keep skin covered during helicopter operations.

## Fires in Mountains (Chapter 8)

- **Masking:** ridges block trajectories; multiple firing positions are needed to cover masked targets.
- **Observation:** forward observers must be on high ground with observation of the avenues of approach.
- **Reverse slopes:** the enemy on a reverse slope is shielded from direct fire and often from indirect fire — plan fires to cover the crest and the approaches.
- **Aviation fires** (close air support) supplement artillery but are weather-dependent; register targets on the approaches early.

## Communications in Mountains (Chapter 9)

- Terrain masking creates dead zones; plan retransmission sites on high ground.
- Weather (snow, rain, fog, electrical storms) degrades propagation and can knock out links.
- Cold kills batteries — keep them warm, carry spares.
- Redundancy: radio, runner, visual signal, wire. Messengers must be trained in snowshoes or skis and familiar with the route.
