# Movement and Route Planning — MCTP 12-10A

Source: MCTP 12-10A, Chapters 2 and 4. Distilled from the manual; see `full-text.md` for the verbatim source.

## Terrain Levels

| Level | Where | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Level I | Valley floors, main lines of communication | Vehicles can operate; civilian population centers; dominated by Level II above |
| Level II | Between valleys and mountain shoulders; narrow roads and trails | Enemy positions on Level III dominate it; units occupy it because it dominates Level I |
| Level III | Summit regions, dominant terrain | Hardest mobility; only acclimatized troops with advanced mountaineering training; flanking and rear attack opportunities |

**Rule:** movement in Level II terrain dictates that elements secure the high ground in Level III terrain.

## The Time-Distance Formula

Assumes acclimatized forces. Planners account for: altitude of operations, time the unit has had to acclimate, weight each individual carries, unit fitness level, size of the unit, altitude gain or loss.

- **Map distance plus one-third** = good estimate of actual ground distance.
- **One hour per 3 kilometers** (about 2 miles).
- Add **one hour per 300 meters (1,000 ft) of ascent**.
- Add **one hour per 600 meters (2,000 ft) of descent**.
- **Foot marches in the mountains are measured in time and elevation, rather than just distance.**
- Place the **shortest person (and shortest gait) in the front** to maintain a uniform march rate.

## Trail Breaking

- In undisturbed (unbroken) snow greater than **30 centimeters (12 inches)**, the lead 2 or 3 persons must pack the snow for the rest of the file.
- Lead persons exert more effort — **cycle them to the rear every 15 to 30 minutes**, similar to a "last-man-up" rotation.
- Once the lead has packed the snow, the trail becomes broken and the rest of the file moves faster.
- Deploying into wedge formation outside the file formation slows the unit — with no one breaking the trail, everyone moves through undisturbed snow.
- Unless the terrain permits mounted movement or sufficient helicopter lift is committed, dismounted movement is the only way to move in mountains.

## Dismounted Movement Rates

| Movement mode | Unbroken trail | Broken trail |
|---|---|---|
| On foot, no snow cover | 2–3 kph cross-country | 3–4 kph trail walking |
| On foot, <1 ft of snow | 1.5–3 kph | 2–3 kph |
| On foot, >1 ft of snow | 0.5–1 kph | 2–3 kph |
| Snowshoe | 1.5–3 kph | 3–4 kph |
| Skiing | 1.5–5 kph | 5–6 kph |
| Skijoring | n/a | 8–24 kph (15 kph max recommended) |

## Route Planning Checklist

1. Identify terrain level for every leg — and the Level III ground that dominates it.
2. Apply the time-distance formula with the actual altitude, load, fitness, and acclimatization state.
3. Plan trail-breaking rotations before the march starts; brief the 15–30 minute cycle.
4. Assess the route for mine/IED locations: chokepoints, compartmented terrain, restricted roads.
5. Identify avalanche paths and route around them.
6. Identify water sources and resupply points.
7. Plan communications retransmission sites on high ground; assume dead zones.
8. Build in weather branches: pass closes, LZ unusable, storm arrives early.
9. Use trained mountain leaders for route planning — the manual directs it explicitly.
