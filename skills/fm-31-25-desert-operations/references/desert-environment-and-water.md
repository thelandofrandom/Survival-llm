# The Desert Environment and Water — FM 31-25

## The Desert Environment

- The desert proper is treeless and barren of turf and sod, because the natural water table lies too far beneath the desert floor — except in the Gobi Desert, where water is nearer the surface, and in irrigated areas.
- Defining military characteristic: openness — reasonably unrestricted lines of communication, many avenues of mounted and dismounted approach, long observation.
- The manual notes a phenomenon it calls agoraphobia — fear of open spaces — that can develop after days in the desert's featureless openness, a counterpart to the jungle manual's fear of enclosure.

## Temperature Extremes

- During the day, avoid operations requiring great or prolonged physical exertion; conduct strenuous activity at night, dawn, early morning, late afternoon, or twilight when possible.
- Night temperature drops of **60° to 80°** are not uncommon; "rarely is the desert night comfortable for a man without heavy clothing or [a] blanket."
- Guard against troops throwing away warm clothing during the hot daylight hours.

## Water — Availability

- The few natural water sources are usually inadequate for sizable military forces and are of prime military and tactical value.
- Natural water transmits dysentery, typhoid fever, and infectious hepatitis; snail fever may be caught by wading, swimming, bathing, or washing clothes in irrigation ditches. Water often contains excessive harmful mineral salts.
- Filtering and sterilizing desert water will not always make it safe — it can still contain poisonous chemicals and acids.
- Usually water must be transported from outside the desert and stored in special tanks and containers. Subsoil water may be found in limited quantities; engineers are equipped to bring it to the surface.

## Water — Requirements

| Situation | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Man resting at high temperature | Up to **a pint of water an hour** lost by sweating |
| Hard-working units (engineers, units on the march) | **More than 24 quarts per man per day** |
| Short periods (1–3 days) at 100° daily mean temperature | **11 or 12 quarts a day** |
| Heavy work shifted to morning/evening | Up to **40 percent of the daily water supply saved** |

- Thirst is not a good index of water need; frequent drinking, even when not thirsty, is the best practice.
- No advantage in thirst quenchers such as chewing gum or fruit drops.
- Water consumption is substantially the same whether taken only at mealtimes or whenever thirsty — those who delay drinking until mealtime experience discomfort without advantage in water economy.

## Water Discipline

- Conserve water; drink only from approved sources; do not pollute available sources.
- When rationing is necessary, issue water under close supervision.
- If insufficient water for bathing, an occasional damp rubdown may be substituted.
- If water is short, extra salt should not be taken.
- Brackish or impure water may be used to wet clothing so the body can be cooled by evaporation, saving the water it would otherwise lose as sweat.
- Containers for drinking water must be clearly marked.
- A water "still" from simple materials to trap condensed water vapor or dew is detailed in FM 21-76.

## Salt

- Most heat injuries except heatstroke result from a deficiency of water or salt or both.
- Coated salt tablets: **one per quart of water consumed** (they do not cause the nausea of plain salt tablets).
- 0.1 percent salt solution for large numbers of troops:

| Salt | Dissolved in |
|---|---|
| 2 ten-grain salt tablets | Canteen |
| 1/4 teaspoonful | Canteen |
| 1 1/3 level messkit spoons | 5-gallon can |
| 9 level messkit spoons (0.3 pound) | Lyster bag (36 gallons) |
| 1 level canteen cup | 250 gallons (water trailer) |

## Acclimation

- Two weeks should be allowed, even though the major part of the adjustment takes place in **5 to 7 days**, with workload and exposure increased gradually by alternating work and rest, closely supervised by commanders and medical officers.
- The physically fit acclimate faster and can do more work in the heat — but the period cannot be shortened by cool-environment training.
- Risk is much greater for the overweight, fatigued, sick, sunburned, convalescing, those using alcohol to excess, those who have had heatstroke, or those with diarrhea.

## Heat Injuries

| Disorder | Cause | Symptoms | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heat cramps | Excessive salt loss by sweating | Painful spasms, usually legs, arms, stomach | Salt water in amounts specified by unit medical authorities; unsalted water may aggravate by diluting body fluids |
| Heat exhaustion | Excessive loss of water and salt | Cold skin wet with sweat, slight temperature rise, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, headache, dizziness, mental confusion, weakness | Shade; elevate feet, massage legs; salted water if not nauseated and vomiting (otherwise IV by medical personnel); evacuate |
| Heatstroke | Overheated body loses ability to cool by sweating | Hot, dry skin; temperature **105°F or higher**; may occur within minutes after sweating stops; exhilaration and unawareness, then collapse, headache, dizziness, mental confusion, delirium, unconsciousness, convulsions | Shade, remove clothing, sprinkle body repeatedly with water head to foot, fan, massage arms and legs, cool water by mouth if able; evacuate with treatment continued on the way |

Watch for the absence of sweating while doing work that normally causes sweating — with the footnote that in low humidity and high temperatures, absence of sweat may not be abnormal, since very rapid evaporation is common.

## Sunburn, Wind, Sand, Dust

- Sunburn increases susceptibility to heat injury; severe and even fatal burns may result from short exposure. Fair skin, ruddy complexion, and red hair are especially susceptible. Keep skin covered; lotions only limit, never prevent.
- Never sleep outdoors during the day except in shade, and never in an isolated spot away from the rest of the unit.
- Wind, sand, and dust irritate mucous membranes and may cause near-disabling local irritation; abrasive particles penetrate clothing and goggles; irritative conjunctivitis from fine dust is a frequent complaint of vehicle drivers even when they wear goggles.
