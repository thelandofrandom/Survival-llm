# FM 31-25 Desert Operations

## Purpose

FM 31-25, *Desert Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 29 February 1972, 88 pages) — the Army's desert doctrine manual, written from the only major desert campaign in modern times involving the U.S. Army: North Africa, from the first landings at Port Safi, Morocco, on 8 November 1942 to the surrender of Germany's Panzer Army Africa and the fall of Tunisia on 20 May 1943. The manual's scope is all deserts, but its primary focus is the regions south and east of the Mediterranean Sea — North Africa and the Near and Middle East — with the experiences of other armies in other deserts used as general reference material. It supersedes FM 31-25 of 26 January 1964.

**Supersession note — read this first.** For current doctrine, the governing references are **ATP 3-90.99, *Desert Operations* (2021)** and **MCTP 12-10D, *Desert Operations* (2021)**, both already skills in this vault; where FM 31-25 disagrees with them, follow the newer publications. What remains durable in FM 31-25 is its treatment of the desert environment and its effects on men — temperature extremes, water requirements and discipline, salt, acclimation, and heat injuries — and its operations fundamentals, which are built on the desert's openness rather than on any particular weapon or organization. What is dated is its organization and equipment (1972-era weapons, radios, and vehicles), its CBR material, its North Africa historical framing, and its medical specifics, which should be checked against current guidance. The manual itself warns that its technical data are "intended for general information and guidance" and that commanders must consult area studies and related scientific documents for important specific details before deploying to any desert region.

**Sibling note.** FM 31-25 is the only desert manual in this batch; the other four are jungle manuals (and one of those, FM 31-36, is actually a night-operations manual). Its closest sibling in the vault is FM 90-3, *Desert Operations*, and the current ATP 3-90.99 / MCTP 12-10D pair. The desert and jungle manuals are mirror images in one respect: the jungle manual's problem is too much water and too little visibility; the desert manual's problem is too little water and too much visibility. Read them together and the contrast is the lesson.

## 1. The Desert Environment

**1.1. What a desert is.** The desert proper is treeless and barren of turf and sod, because the natural water table lies too far beneath the desert floor — except in the Gobi Desert, where water is nearer the surface, and in irrigated areas, where man has raised the water table. Soils, vegetation, and the absence of man-made features are the manual's terrain elements, and the desert's defining military characteristic is openness: reasonably unrestricted lines of communication, many avenues of mounted and dismounted approach, and long observation.

**1.2. Temperature extremes.** The extremes of desert temperature adversely affect men and thus military operations. During the day, operations requiring great or prolonged physical exertion should be avoided to preclude heat casualties; strenuous activity should be conducted at night, during dawn or early morning, or in the late afternoon or at twilight when possible. At night, drops in temperature of **60° to 80°** are not uncommon, and "rarely is the desert night comfortable for a man without heavy clothing or [a] blanket." Commanders must guard against the temptation of troops to throw away their warm clothing during the hot daylight hours. Individual protection against desert cold at night and during winter is a command responsibility.

**1.3. Water is the decisive problem.** The few natural water sources in the desert are usually inadequate for sizable military forces and are of prime military as well as tactical value. Natural water, when drunk, transmits dysentery, typhoid fever, and infectious hepatitis; parasitic diseases such as snail fever may be caught by wading, swimming, bathing, or washing clothes in irrigation ditches or other bodies of water. The water often contains excessive concentrations of harmful mineral salts or is otherwise polluted. Such water must be purified before it is drunk — but filtering and sterilizing desert water will not always make it safe, since it can still contain poisonous chemicals and acids. Usually, water for military forces must be transported from sources outside the desert and stored in special tanks and containers. Subsoil water may be found in limited quantities, and engineers are equipped to bring it to the surface.

**1.4. The psychological effect.** The manual notes a phenomenon it calls agoraphobia — fear of open spaces — that can develop after days in the desert's featureless openness, a counterpart to the jungle manual's fear of enclosure. Both are treated as normal reactions to an unfamiliar environment, to be trained out rather than ignored. → `references/desert-environment-and-water.md`

## 2. Effects of the Desert on Men — Heat, Water, Salt

**2.1. Acclimation.** Acclimation — nature's way of adjusting the body's cooling system to desert heat — is real even for troops in good physical condition. The acclimated soldier is alert, performs his work energetically, and is without abnormal symptoms. Two weeks should be allowed for acclimation, even though the major part of the adjustment takes place in **5 to 7 days**, with workload and exposure to heat increased gradually by a schedule of alternating work and rest periods, closely supervised by commanders and medical officers. The physically fit soldier becomes acclimated more rapidly and is capable of more work in the heat — but the period cannot be shortened, or the ability to work in heat increased, by continued training in cool environments beyond that required to attain physical fitness. The risk of heat injury is much greater for persons who are overweight, fatigued, sick, sunburned, or convalescing from sickness, and for those who use alcohol to excess, have had heatstroke, or have diarrhea.

**2.2. How the body cools — and fails.** The body produces heat even at rest, and gains heat directly from the sun, the ground, and a breeze hotter than the body's surface. It must constantly balance heat gain with heat loss, or its temperature will rise to levels at which the individual suffers cellular damage or dies. Heat can be lost directly to cooler air, as during the desert night, but when air, ground, and surrounding objects are hotter than the body, heat can be lost only by sweating — evaporation of sweat cools the skin and the blood that has brought body heat to the surface.

**2.3. Water requirements — the numbers.** At high temperatures, a man resting may lose **as much as a pint of water an hour** by sweating; working, he loses more. Hard-working units such as engineer battalions and units on the march may require **more than 24 quarts of drinking water per man per day**. For short periods of 1 to 3 days, reduced quantities may be sufficient — **11 or 12 quarts a day at 100° daily mean temperature**. Up to **40 percent of the daily water supply may be saved** by performing heavy work and strenuous training during morning or evening rather than the hottest part of the day. Thirst is not a good index of water need, and frequent drinking, even when not thirsty, is the best practice. There is no advantage in thirst quenchers such as chewing gum or fruit drops. For a given amount of work under high temperature, water consumption is substantially the same whether water is taken only at mealtimes or whenever one is thirsty — those who delay drinking until mealtime experience considerable discomfort without any apparent advantage in water economy.

**2.4. Water discipline.** Water discipline must become part of a soldier's daily life: conserve water and do not waste it; drink only from approved sources; do not pollute available sources; when rationing is necessary, issue water to individual soldiers under close supervision. If there is insufficient water for bathing, an occasional damp rubdown may be substituted. If there is a shortage of water, extra salt should not be taken. Water not fit for drinking may be used for other purposes — in emergencies, brackish or impure water may be used to wet clothing so the body can be cooled by evaporation, saving the water it would otherwise lose as sweat. To prevent confusion, containers for drinking water must be clearly marked. Details for constructing a water "still" from simple materials to trap condensed water vapor or dew are referred to FM 21-76.

**2.5. Salt.** Most heat injuries except heatstroke result from a deficiency of water or salt or both, caused by inadequate intake during heavy sweating. Coated salt tablets do not cause the nausea sometimes felt after taking plain salt tablets; they should be taken at the rate of **one per quart of water consumed**. A convenient way to provide adequate salt for a large number of troops is to add a **0.1 percent salt solution** to all drinking water: 2 ten-grain salt tablets dissolved in a canteen; 1/4 teaspoonful dissolved in a canteen; 1 1/3 level messkit spoons dissolved in a 5-gallon can; 9 level messkit spoons (0.3 pound) dissolved in a Lyster bag (36 gallons); 1 level canteen cup dissolved in 250 gallons (water trailer).

**2.6. The heat injuries.** Heat cramps are caused by excessive loss of salt by sweating: painful spasms, usually of the legs, arms, and stomach, relieved by drinking salt water in amounts specified by unit medical authorities — drinking unsalted water may aggravate the situation by diluting body fluids and increasing the relative deficiency. Heat exhaustion is caused by excessive loss of water as well as salt: cold skin wet with sweat, a slight rise in body temperature, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, headache, dizziness, mental confusion, and weakness. It may occur suddenly or gradually, and in even the best acclimated individuals doing extremely heavy work in severe heat; mild cases incapacitate for a few hours, severe cases may be fatal if untreated. Treatment: place the victim in the shade, elevate his feet and massage his legs to assist the return of blood to the heart, give salted water to drink if not nauseated and vomiting (otherwise qualified medical personnel should inject salt fluid intravenously), then evacuate to the nearest medical installation. Heatstroke occurs when the overheated body loses its ability to cool itself by sweating: the skin becomes hot and dry, body temperature **105°F or higher**, and it is a true emergency — untreated cases may die. It may occur within a few minutes after sweating has stopped, and the victim may become exhilarated and unaware of the dangerous condition developing, then collapse suddenly or develop headache, dizziness, mental confusion, even delirium, before unconsciousness and convulsions. Treatment: place the victim in the shade, remove his clothing, sprinkle his body repeatedly with water from head to foot, increase evaporation by fanning, massage the arms and legs, give cold or cool water by mouth if he is able to drink, and evacuate as soon as possible with treatment continued on the way. All personnel must be instructed to watch for the absence of sweating while doing work that normally causes sweating — with the footnote that in low humidity and high temperatures, absence of sweat may not be abnormal, since very rapid evaporation of moisture from the skin is common in such conditions.

**2.7. Sunburn and wind, sand, and dust.** Sunburn increases susceptibility to heat injury and causes significant damage of its own; severe and even fatal skin burns may result from short periods of exposure. Persons with fair skin, ruddy complexion, and red hair are especially susceptible. Sunburn can be prevented by keeping the skin covered; it can only be limited, not prevented, by lotions. Individuals who sleep outdoors during the day should do so only in shade and never in an isolated spot away from the rest of the unit. Wind, sand, and dust are particularly irritating to mucous membranes and may cause local irritation of a near-disabling nature: the abrasive wind carries dust and sand particles that penetrate clothing and goggles, and irritative conjunctivitis from fine dust in the eyes is a frequent complaint of vehicle drivers even when they wear goggles. → `references/desert-environment-and-water.md`

## 3. Operations in the Desert

**3.1. Principles and fundamentals.** Chapter 2 applies the principles of war — objective, offensive, mass, economy of force, maneuver, unity of command, security, surprise, and simplicity — and the fundamentals of combat to the desert. The principles tend to reinforce one another but may conflict depending on circumstances, so the degree of application of any specific principle varies with the situation. The fundamentals include employing the soldier effectively and using the environment to best advantage: commanders use every advantage afforded by terrain, air space, weather, and time.

**3.2. What the desert gives the attacker.** Desert terrain offers opportunities for imaginative and efficient employment of weapons, provides reasonably unrestricted lines of communication essential to control decisive maneuver and its support, and provides many avenues of mounted and dismounted approach permitting application of combat power through mobility and maneuver. In offensive operations, the tactical advantages of retaining terrain are not normally exploitable in the desert, since there is generally a lack of key terrain — except in the vicinity of communication centers, logistical areas, port facilities, and mountain passes. Seizure of terrain dominating these tactically and strategically critical objectives is usually important.

**3.3. The forms of desert operations.** Chapter 2 covers offensive operations, defensive operations, retrograde operations, airmobile operations, counterguerrilla operations, and chemical, biological, and radiological defense. The defensive problem in the desert is the opposite of the jungle's: there is little concealment and little cover, so the defense depends on dispersion, depth, and the ability to move — and on water and supply, which are the defender's vulnerability as much as the attacker's. → `references/desert-operations.md`

## 4. Support and Training

**4.1. Support.** Chapter 3 covers combat support and combat service support in the desert: the same water, fuel, and maintenance problems that dominate the environment chapter, now as a sustainment problem — how to keep a force supplied, its vehicles running, and its men fit in a place where water must be brought in, sand destroys machinery, and distances are measured in hours of driving over open ground. Appendix B gives maintenance techniques and procedures for desert conditions.

**4.2. Training.** Chapter 4 covers individual and unit training for desert operations: individual training in the desert environment, water discipline, heat-injury prevention, and navigation in featureless terrain; unit training in desert maneuver, with the emphasis on the fundamentals of Chapter 2 — mobility, maneuver, and the use of the environment — practiced before deployment, since the manual's North Africa lesson is that a force that learns the desert in contact pays for the lesson in heat casualties and lost vehicles. → `references/desert-operations.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a desert question from FM 31-25, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (water quantities in quarts per man per day, salt concentrations, acclimation days, temperature drops at night), then the cautions. State the priority order explicitly: water and heat discipline first, then movement and maneuver, then the fighting technique. Name the environment the advice belongs to — desert — and say which manual it comes from when a sibling manual differs. Where current doctrine (ATP 3-90.99 / MCTP 12-10D, 2021) differs from FM 31-25, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Water is the decisive problem.** Storage and conservation of the little available — not finding it — is the desert's water problem.
2. **Drink on schedule, not on thirst.** Thirst is not a good index of water need; frequent drinking, even when not thirsty, is the best practice.
3. **Work the cool hours.** Up to 40 percent of the daily water supply can be saved by doing heavy work in the morning or evening.
4. **Two weeks to acclimate.** The major adjustment takes 5 to 7 days, but two weeks should be allowed, with workload increased gradually under medical supervision.
5. **Watch for the sweating that stops.** Heatstroke may follow within minutes of sweating stopping — cool the victim immediately and evacuate.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-90.99 / MCTP 12-10D (2021) govern desert operations today; FM 31-25 is the 1972 doctrine, not the authority.
