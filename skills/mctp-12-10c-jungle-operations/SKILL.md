# Jungle Operations

## Purpose

MCTP 12-10C, *Jungle Operations* (also published as ATP 3-90.98; Headquarters, Department of the Army and Headquarters, U.S. Marine Corps, 24 September 2020, 208 pages), is the joint Army–Marine Corps doctrine for fighting in jungle and tropical forest. It supersedes FM 90-5 and the earlier MCTP 12-10C, both dated 16 August 1982. The manual's governing idea is that the jungle is a close, wet, disorienting battlefield where the environment degrades everything — visibility, movement, communications, equipment, and the human body — and the force that adapts to the jungle's rules wins, while the force that fights the jungle as if it were open ground is defeated by the terrain before it meets the enemy.

In the jungle, visibility is measured in meters, movement in hundreds of meters per hour, and the most dangerous animal is a mosquito.

## 1. The Jungle Environment

**Jungle classifications.** Three main types, and the leader must know which one the unit is in:
- *Primary jungle* — old-growth trees undisturbed by logging, farming, or other human activity. Tall trees, thin undergrowth at ground level because the canopy blocks the light. Classified further as tropical rain forest or deciduous forest depending on the predominant trees.
- *Secondary jungle* — areas previously cleared for farming or logging. Younger, shorter trees and much thicker undergrowth. Harder to move through than primary jungle despite the smaller trees.
- *Savanna* — natural grassland with few trees. Open by jungle standards, but still a jungle environment for heat, disease, and navigation purposes.
Common land features in the jungle are swamps, savannas, and cultivated areas. The large rivers that drain jungle regions are the highways — and the obstacles.

**Weather.** Jungle weather is heat, humidity, and rain in combinations that never fully stop. The canopy moderates temperature but traps humidity; the rain is frequent, heavy, and often daily; the ground is wet for months at a time. The weather's military effect is not the rain itself but what the rain does: it rots equipment, breeds disease, floods low ground, and turns trails into streams.

**Terrain.** Jungle terrain is compartmented by rivers, swamps, ridges, and vegetation boundaries. The ground that looks passable on a map may be a swamp in reality, and the ground that looks impassable may be a trail the locals have used for generations. Terrain analysis in the jungle is done on the ground, not from the map — the map shows the rivers, the ground shows whether they can be crossed.

**Wildlife.** The jungle's animals are hazards to be briefed, and the manual's wildlife section is written for leaders to brief, not for troops to study:
- *Mosquitoes are the most dangerous of the tropical insects.* The largest health threat is malaria, but mosquitoes also carry chikungunya, dengue, and yellow fever — and heartworm larvae that can infect military working dogs.
- *Flies* carry and spread disease and can cause lesions and infections.
- *Wasps and bees* rarely attack unless their nests are disturbed. When a nest is disturbed, leave the area and reassemble at the last rally point; stings require medical attention.
- *Centipedes, spiders, and scorpions* inflict painful stings and prefer dark places — shake out sleep systems and check clothing and boots for insects before dressing. Spider bites can be painful and often require medical treatment for necrotic tissue and secondary infection. Ants are dangerous to troops lying prone, sleeping, or injured and unable to move; place wounded troops in an area free of ants.
- *Snakes:* stay alert and avoid disturbing them. Treat all snakebites as venomous (refer to TC 4-02.1 for treatment). Antivenin is specific to a snake species — there is no universal snake antivenin — and depending on availability and storage, victims may require urgent medical evacuation. Units need an established procedure for treating snakebites before they happen.
- *Leeches* are common in many jungles, particularly throughout the Southwest Pacific and Southeast Asia. Watch for leeches on the body and brush them off before they bite. Once they have taken hold, do not pull them off forcibly — part of the leech may remain in the skin. They will release if touched with insect repellent, a moist piece of tobacco, or similar. Leech straps wrapped around the lower legs prevent them crawling up.

**Malaria discipline.** The precautions, in the manual's order:
1. Take prescribed antimalarial prophylactic medication.
2. Use insect repellent.
3. Wear permethrin-impregnated clothing covering as much of the body as possible.
4. Use nets or screens at every opportunity.
5. Avoid the worst-infested areas when possible.
Mosquitoes are most prevalent early at night and just before dawn — troops must be especially cautious at these times. Malaria is more common in populated areas than in uninhabited jungle, so be especially cautious around villages. Malaria discipline is a command responsibility: a unit that stops taking prophylaxis stops being a fighting unit within weeks.

**Environmental effects on personnel.** Heat and humidity are the primary casualty producers, and the jungle's heat injury problem is worse than the desert's because the humidity prevents sweat from evaporating — the body's cooling mechanism fails even at moderate temperatures. The manual's heat-injury ladder: heat rash (dots or tiny pimples, from sweating and increased risk factors), heat exhaustion (the most common form, generally not associated with evidence of organ damage), and heat stroke (the most dangerous, the body's heat-balance mechanism failing). Risk factors include lack of acclimatization, dehydration, alcohol, and prior heat injury (any heat stroke, or more than two episodes of heat exhaustion).

**Acclimatization.** Approximately **one week of daily heat exposure** is necessary to induce heat acclimatization; Soldiers and Marines unusually susceptible to heat injuries require additional time. Acclimatization lets the body reach and maintain efficiency in its cooling process — but situations arise where troops cannot become fully acclimatized before leaders require them to operate, and leaders must plan for that. **Dehydration nullifies the benefits of heat acclimatization and physical fitness.** Leaders monitor troops carefully for signs of heat distress and adjust work/rest cycles and water consumption by the WBGT index (see the companion desert skill for the full WBGT table; the same doctrine governs here).

**Water.** Never consume water not yet treated by water purification specialists or medics. Specialists and medics treat and disinfect water by adding chlorine to a level prescribed by the command surgeon — the chlorine kills the organisms present and protects against organisms that get in later. Heat can contaminate water: heat reduces the disinfecting power of dissolved chlorine, and if air temperature exceeds **100°F (38°C)**, specialists must measure and maintain safe chlorine levels in storage containers. For personal consumption, water from suspect sources must be treated with iodine purification tablets (ATP 4-25.12). Purified water is used for hand washing and food preparation as well as drinking.

**Environmental effects on equipment.** Heat and humidity degrade nearly all military equipment: metal corrodes, wood swells and rots, optics fog, electronics fail, ammunition deteriorates, and lubricants wash out. The manual's answer is preventive maintenance done to the equipment technical manual and lubrication order for hot-climate operations — and done daily, because in the jungle a day's skipped maintenance is a week's corrosion.

## 2. Foundations for Jungle Operations

**Understanding jungle operations.** The jungle compresses the battlefield: engagements happen at close range, often at tens of meters, and the side that sees first usually shoots first. The operational framework is the same as anywhere — deep, close, rear, support — but in the jungle the deep fight is fought by patrols and aircraft, not by massed maneuver, and the close fight is fought by small units that may be out of contact with each other for hours.

**Multiple-domain operations in an extended jungle battlefield.** As in the desert manual, the domains beyond land — cyberspace, space, the electromagnetic spectrum — are part of the jungle battlefield. The thick canopy degrades GPS-enabled navigation aids and satellite communications, so the jungle force must be able to operate when its networks are degraded or absent. Joint operations in a jungle environment are the norm: the jungle is rarely a single-service fight.

**Fundamentals of jungle operations.** The fundamentals are the doctrine's answer to the environment: security (the jungle hides the enemy until he is close — security is a 360-degree, close-range problem), movement (slow, deliberate, and noisy if done wrong), surprise (the jungle's concealment works both ways — the side that moves silently and appears where the enemy is not wins), and sustainment (everything must be carried in, because the jungle provides almost nothing a modern force can use without treatment).

## 3. Combat Power in Jungle Regions

The chapter walks the warfighting functions through the jungle, and the pattern is consistent: every function is degraded by the environment and must be deliberately compensated.

- **Leadership:** jungle leadership is disease discipline, heat discipline, and navigation discipline enforced by example. A leader who does not take prophylaxis, drink, and navigate correctly has no authority to enforce any of it.
- **Information and the information warfighting function:** in the jungle, information is scarce and hard-won — the canopy defeats aerial observation, and the intelligence problem is getting any reliable picture of ground the enemy knows better than you do.
- **Command and control:** short distances and poor communications stretch C2 in a different way than the desert — units are close together and still out of contact, because the vegetation blocks radio and the terrain blocks line of sight.
- **Movement and maneuver:** the jungle's decisive limitation. Movement is slow (see the table below), and the force that can move through the jungle — and sustain the movement — can choose the fight.
- **Intelligence:** the canopy defeats most collection; human intelligence and patrol reporting become the primary sources.
- **Fires:** short fields of fire and poor observation limit fires; the canopy defeats air observation and degrades air support, and artillery must be adjusted by observers who can see only a few meters.
- **Sustainment/logistics:** everything is carried, by foot or by helicopter, and the helicopter needs a landing zone the jungle does not provide — emphasis on air evacuation is necessary, and where the jungle is too dense to prepare a landing zone, other means must be planned.
- **Protection/force protection:** camouflage is easy in the jungle and concealment is nearly total — the protection problem is the enemy's concealment, not your own, and the environment itself: disease, heat, and the animals.

## 4. Jungle Offensive Operations

**Characteristics of the offense.** The jungle offense is a close fight: movement to contact, hasty or deliberate attacks, exploitations, and pursuits, all at short range and in limited visibility. The attacker must find the enemy before the enemy finds him, which makes reconnaissance and patrols the decisive element. Surprise is achievable in the jungle precisely because observation is so limited — a force that moves silently can appear at close range before the enemy knows it is there.

**Types of offensive operations and forms of maneuver.** The standard types and forms apply, but the jungle favors infiltration and the envelopment at small scale: the wide turning movement of the desert becomes the silent movement through the jungle around a flank the enemy cannot observe. The frontal attack in the jungle is a close-range brawl with no room to maneuver — a last resort.

**Warfighting function considerations and consolidate gains.** Each function is planned for the offense: C2 that works when units are out of contact, fires that can be adjusted by observers who can barely see, sustainment that pushes forward with the maneuver force through terrain that resists it. Consolidation in the jungle means securing the ground and the trails, reorganizing, and preparing for the counterattack that will come through the same concealment the attacker used.

## 5. Jungle Defensive Operations

**Defensive characteristics.** The jungle defense is a defense of positions that dominate trails, rivers, and clearings — the movement corridors of the jungle. Depth is achieved by successive positions along the corridors, not by a continuous line. **Defending encircled** is a distinct jungle problem: a position cut off in the jungle can be sustained by air and can hold out far longer than a position cut off in open ground, because the enemy cannot mass against it without exposing himself on the same trails.

**Forms of the defense and transition.** Area defense, mobile defense, and retrograde apply; the transition to the offense is planned from the start, because the jungle punishes the force that sits still — disease, heat, and the enemy's infiltration all work against a static position over time.

## 6. Jungle Stability Operations

Stability operations in the jungle are a population problem in a landscape where the population is scattered in villages along the rivers and roads. The stability tasks are the standard ones — security, governance, development — adapted to a place where the government may never have reached the villages and the insurgent may have lived there for years. The chapter's warfighting function considerations and transitions apply as in the other chapters.

## 7. Training for Jungle Operations

The training chapter is the manual's most practical section, and it reads as a checklist for any unit deploying to the jungle.

**Training adapted for jungle operations.** Every skill must be re-learned for the jungle: the rifle that works on a range must work wet, muddy, and corroded; the radio that works in the open must work under triple canopy; the march that works on a road must work through secondary jungle at 500 meters an hour.

**Camouflage and concealment.** The jungle provides concealment in abundance; the training problem is discipline — keeping it. Movement discipline, noise discipline, light discipline, and the camouflage of positions, vehicles, and equipment.

**Survival, evasion, and escape; water survival.** Jungle survival is a water problem and a disease problem before it is a food problem. Water survival — river crossings, swamp movement, and the hazards of jungle waterways — is a distinct skill set.

**Land navigation.** The jungle's navigation problem is covered in Appendix A and below — it is the skill the whole operation depends on, and it is the skill most degraded by the environment.

**Jungle field craft.** The small skills that keep a person alive and effective in the jungle: living in the wet, keeping equipment working, moving silently, and sleeping in a hammock or on the ground without becoming a casualty of insects or animals.

**Medical training considerations.** The jungle's medical problem is disease and heat, not just wounds. Medics must be trained in malaria, diarrheal disease, immersion foot, jungle rot, and heat injury — and every Soldier and Marine must be trained in self-aid and buddy aid for those conditions.

**Physical conditioning and acclimatization.** Conditioning for the jungle is conditioning for heat, humidity, and load carriage over broken ground. Acclimatization takes about a week of daily heat exposure.

**March discipline.** The jungle march is slow, with frequent halts, water discipline, and security at every halt. A unit that marches in the jungle the way it marches on a road will arrive exhausted, dehydrated, and ambushed.

**Adjustment and conduct of fire.** Firing in the jungle is close-range, fast, and often unaimed by open-ground standards — train for the engagement that actually happens at 20 meters, not the one on the qualification range.

## Navigation and Tracking in Jungle Regions (Appendix A)

This is the skill the manual returns to most insistently, because navigation failure in the jungle is a mission failure.

**Dead reckoning.** Although slower, dead reckoning is the most effective navigation method in the jungle. Terrain analysis, constant use of the compass, and an accurate pace count are essential. The thick canopy may degrade GPS; when it does, the compass, the map, and the pace count are all that remain.

**Pace count.** Often the best method for measuring distance traveled. Each Soldier or Marine makes a personal pace table, practiced over different types of terrain. At least two Soldiers or Marines in each patrol should be compass-qualified, and three or four should keep pace count. The artillery fire support team chief keeps an accurate fix on location, as should the platoon forward observers.

**Attack point method.** Where rivers, streams, or mountains exist, use attack points — identifiable terrain features adjacent to the objective — to locate it. Resection (determining position from grid azimuths to two or more well-defined locations, three or more for greater accuracy) is the fallback when a patrol is uncertain of its location.

**Daylight movement rates (Table A-1).** The maximum distance a unit might cover in one hour of daylight — and the normal error is overestimating distance traveled:

| Terrain | Maximum distance (meters/hour) |
|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | up to 1,000 |
| Deciduous forest, secondary jungle, tall grass | 500 |
| Swamps | 100–500 |
| Rice paddies (wet) | 800 |
| Rice paddies (dry) | 2,000 |
| Plantations | 2,000 |
| Trails | up to 3,000 |

Plan movement by this table, not by road-march rates. A 5-kilometer movement through secondary jungle is a ten-hour movement, not a one-hour movement.

## Negotiating Jungle Obstacles (Appendix B) and Individual Combat Load (Appendix C)

**Obstacles.** Rivers, swamps, steep banks, fallen trees, dense thickets, and cultivated areas are the jungle's obstacles, and the appendix is the technique set for each: crossing methods, rope work where needed, and the discipline of crossing as a team with security. A river crossing in the jungle is a tactical operation, not a hike.

**Combat load.** The individual combat load in jungle operations is the load a Soldier or Marine can actually carry through the jungle at the movement rates above, in the heat and humidity, day after day. The appendix's load tables are the authority — and the principle is that the load that cannot be carried through secondary jungle at 500 meters an hour is not a load, it is a liability.

## Fratricide Reduction (Appendix D)

The jungle's short fields of fire and limited visibility make fratricide a constant risk: the enemy is close, friendly units are close, and nobody can see either. The appendix is the countermeasure set — identification, coordination, and control measures — and the discipline that goes with them. In the jungle, positive identification before firing is not a nicety; it is the only thing standing between a patrol and its own sister unit.

## Output Contract

When asked about jungle operations, answer from this doctrine: lead with the environment's numbers (movement rate for the terrain type, acclimatization time, water treatment requirement), then the discipline (malaria prophylaxis, pace count, dead reckoning), then the operation. Quote the movement-rate table exactly — a plan built on road-march rates in secondary jungle is a plan that fails by a factor of ten. Distinguish doctrine (the tables, the treatment rules, the navigation methods) from judgment (which route, which attack point, whether to cross here or there).

## Operating Rules

1. **Malaria discipline is a command responsibility.** Prophylaxis, repellent, permethrin clothing, nets — every day, no exceptions.
2. **Navigate by compass, map, and pace count.** Dead reckoning is the most effective jungle method; GPS degrades under canopy.
3. **Plan movement by the table, not by road rates.** Secondary jungle is 500 meters an hour; swamps can be 100.
4. **Treat all water.** Chlorine by specialists, iodine tablets for personal use; heat above 100°F degrades chlorine.
5. **Treat all snakebites as venomous.** Have the treatment procedure established before the bite happens.
6. **Acclimatize for a week.** Dehydration nullifies acclimatization and fitness.
7. **Shake out your boots and sleep system.** The jungle's small animals are a daily hazard, not a rare one.
