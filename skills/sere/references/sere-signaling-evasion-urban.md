# SERE Signaling, Recovery, Evasion, and Urban Survival

Source: AFH 10-644, Chapters 21 (Signaling and Communication), 22 (Recovery), 23 (Evasion), 24 (Urban).

## Signaling and communication (Ch. 21)

The most complete signaling chapter in the vault.

### Ground-to-air signals (GTAS) — the standards

- **Size:** at least **3 feet wide and 18 feet long**; keep symbols in proportion (roughly a 2:3 ratio).
- **Angularity:** straight lines and square corners — not found in nature, so they attract attention.
- **Contrast:** stand out sharply against the background — outline with green boughs, pile brush and rocks for shadow, stamp or burn grass, trample snow. Avoid orange parachute material on a green or brown background (it blends in).
- **Location:** large high open area, visible from all directions, away from shadows and overhangs. It can double as a landing zone.
- **Meaning:** tell rescue forces something — "require medical assistance" or a coded evasion symbol.

### Standard GTAS symbols

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| V | Require assistance |
| X | Require medical assistance |
| N | No / negative |
| Y | Yes / affirmative |
| Arrow | Proceeding in this direction |

### Other signals

- **Three of anything is distress:** three fires in a triangle, three whistle blasts, three gunshots, three flashes.
- **Signal mirror:** visible for miles on a sunny day; sweep the horizon, then flash the aircraft.
- **Smoke by day, flame by night:** green boughs for white smoke; rubber or oil for black smoke.
- **Parachute signals:** rectangular sections of canopy make large, high-contrast pattern signals; allow material to move with the wind — movement attracts attention and indicates wind direction to rescuers.

### Aircraft acknowledgement

Rescue personnel signal that they have seen you by radio, by **flying low with landing lights on**, and/or by **rocking their wings**.

## Recovery (Ch. 22)

The IP's role in their own rescue: know the procedures of the international search-and-rescue system (ICAO, IMCO and national SAR organizations); be prepared for helicopter pickup (approach from the front, secure loose gear, follow crew signals); understand that success depends on rescue-force availability, enemy proximity, and weather — and above all on the IP's knowledge of what to do.

## Evasion (Ch. 23)

The longest single chapter in the manual. Evasion is the art of not being found.

- **Camouflage principles:** match the darker and lighter qualities of the background; avoid contrasts of hue (especially in heavy vegetation); avoid light-toned colors such as leaf bottoms; eliminate tone contrast with matching coloration and disruptive patterns.
- **Shine:** skin, clean clothing, metallic insignia, rings, glasses, watches, buckles, and ID bracelets all produce shine — neutralize by staining, covering, or removing, especially at night. In undisturbed nature, almost nothing shines.
- **Movement:** travel at night, rest by day; use covered routes; avoid ridgelines, open ground, and predictable paths; cross danger areas quickly at the narrowest point.
- **Counter-tracking:** vary your route; avoid leaving sign; be aware of dogs and trackers.
- **Governing principle:** the best evasion is never being detected in the first place.

## Urban survival (Ch. 24)

- Shelter in abandoned structures; avoid occupied buildings and predictable locations.
- Water from urban sources — purify it; urban water is as contaminated as any surface water.
- Food from markets and discarded sources; cook everything.
- Movement through urban terrain: blend with the local population; move with crowds when possible; avoid checkpoints and surveillance.
- Specific urban dangers: disease, violence, surveillance, and the difficulty of concealment in a built environment.
- This chapter has no counterpart in the Army's field manual and is the most directly useful for a civilian disaster scenario.
