# SERE

## Purpose

AFH 10-644, *SERE Operations* (U.S. Air Force Handbook, March 2017, 652 pages) — the Air Force's comprehensive handbook for Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape. This is the deepest single manual in the vault: 24 chapters covering everything from the psychology of isolation to open-sea survival, urban evasion, and recovery by friendly forces. This skill is for aircrew, isolated personnel, and anyone who wants the full doctrine behind the Army's shorter survival manual — the `survival` skill (ATP 3-50.21) is the concise field version; this is the reference work behind it.

The handbook defines its subject precisely. **Isolated personnel (IP)** are persons separated from their unit who must survive, evade, resist, or escape. The IP's mission, from the moment of the isolating event, is to **"return to friendly control without giving aid or comfort to the enemy, to return early and in good physical and mental condition."** That mission breaks into three goals: **maintain life, maintain honor, and return.** Everything in the 24 chapters serves one of those three goals.

## 1. Mission, Conditions, and the IP's Needs

**1.1. The mission and the goals.** The mission has three parts: return to friendly control; without giving aid or comfort to the enemy (fulfilled most effectively by following the Code of Conduct); early and in good physical and mental condition (which depends above all on the will to survive). "Friendly control" does not only mean a combat situation — a pilot down in the Arctic at −40°F or a crew separated by a roadside bomb in 120°F desert is in a hostile environment even in peacetime.

**1.2. Conditions affecting survival (Ch. 2).** Survival is shaped by the environment (climate, terrain, vegetation, animals), the IP's physical condition, the equipment available, and the threat. The handbook stresses preparation: knowing the area of operations before the mission, carrying the right equipment, and having an evasion plan of action (EPA) and isolated-person guidance (ISG) already made. Most survival failures are preparation failures.

**1.3. The IP's needs (Ch. 3).** In priority order: water, food, shelter and clothing, fire, health, and security. The handbook quantifies water: **about 2 quarts per day in relatively cold climates to remain efficient, rising to 5–6 quarts per day with heat and exertion.** The needs are the same as in the Army survival manual; this handbook adds the operational context — what the needs mean for planning, movement, and recovery.

**1.4. The Code of Conduct.** The six-article moral guide for all U.S. service members in captivity or isolation: (I) I am an American, fighting in the forces which guard my country and our way of life; (II) I will never surrender of my own free will; (III) if captured, I will continue to resist by all means available and escape; (IV) if captured, I will keep faith with my fellow prisoners and give no information except name, rank, service number, and date of birth; (V) if captured, I will evade answering further questions to the utmost of my ability; (VI) I will never forget that I am an American, responsible for my actions, and dedicated to the principles that made my country free. The handbook stresses that the Code is useful at all times and in all situations — moral obligations apply in peacetime as well as in wartime. → `references/sere-mission-psychology.md`

## 2. Psychology — The Decisive Factor

**2.1. The psychological aspects of SERE (Ch. 4).** The will to survive is present in varying degrees in all human beings, and those who maintain it increase their chances of success. The handbook's treatment of psychology is the most detailed in any of these manuals: it covers fear, anxiety, anger, guilt, depression, loneliness, boredom, and the specific psychological effects of isolation, captivity, and interrogation.

**2.2. The survival stressors.** Pain, cold, heat, thirst, hunger, fatigue, boredom, loneliness, and fear. Each degrades judgment; several together degrade it catastrophically. The countermeasures are the same as in the Army manual but spelled out in operational detail: establish a routine, set goals, keep busy with survival tasks, maintain hope through a realistic plan, and conserve energy.

**2.3. Captivity psychology.** If captured, the handbook's guidance is: maintain military bearing and discipline; organize with fellow prisoners; communicate; resist exploitation; do not give information beyond name, rank, service number, and date of birth; and never forget that prisoners of war will not be forgotten — the commitment to recover them is real, and knowing that sustains resistance. Interrogation resistance is built on preparation: know the Code, know what you will and will not say, and know that the interrogator's job is to make you talk — your job is to make that difficult without being punished for it.

**2.4. The will to survive.** The handbook returns to this theme in every chapter: skills matter, but the decisive factor is the decision to live and return. People who have a plan, a purpose, and a reason to return survive at far higher rates than people with better equipment and no will. → `references/sere-mission-psychology.md`

## 3. SERE Medicine

**3.1. What it covers (Ch. 5).** A full field-medicine chapter: lifesaving steps, shock, wounds, fractures, burns, heat and cold injuries, bites and stings, water and food discipline, hygiene, and the medicinal use of plants. The principles match the `first-aid` and `survival` skills; the handbook adds the SERE context — care with no equipment, no evacuation, and no resupply, often while evading.

**3.2. Key points.** Prevention dominates: foot care (blisters cripple evaders), hydration discipline, hygiene (dysentery and skin infections are the great killers of isolated personnel), and rest. Treat injuries early and completely — a small wound left dirty becomes a crippling infection in days. Know the signs of the injuries that kill quietly: internal bleeding, heatstroke, hypothermia, and dehydration.

**3.3. Weather and environment (Ch. 6–8).** Weather reading (clouds, wind, pressure changes), global climate characteristics by region — arctic, desert, jungle, temperate, mountain, and ocean — and what each climate demands: water discipline in the desert, warmth in the arctic, dryness in the jungle. The handbook's climate chapters are the reference for planning any movement: know the climate you are entering before you enter it. → `references/sere-medicine-climate-environments.md`

## 4. The Environments — Sea, Land, People

**4.1. Open seas (Ch. 9).** Survival at sea: the raft, protection from sun and cold, water discipline (never drink seawater; collect rain; use the solar still), fishing, sharks, and signaling. The sea kills by exposure and dehydration faster than by hunger — shade and water come first.

**4.2. Local people (Ch. 10).** The people in the area of operations are the most important factor after the environment. They can feed you, hide you, guide you — or betray you. The handbook's guidance: observe before approaching; respect local customs; never assume friendliness; trade fairly; and remember that local people have already solved the problems of this environment — watch what they eat, wear, and avoid. This is the operational version of the Army manual's "act like the natives."

**4.3. Body temperature and clothing (Ch. 11–12).** Proper body temperature is maintained by insulation, activity, and shelter. Clothing is a system: layers, loose fit, dry, windproof outer, insulation inner. In the cold, protect the extremities and the head; in the heat, cover the skin and wear a hat. Wet clothing is the enemy in every climate.

**4.4. Shelter, firecraft, and equipment (Ch. 13–15).** Shelter types by environment (lean-to, debris hut, snow cave, raised platform, below-ground desert shelter); firecraft (fire lays, ignition methods including friction, fire as signal and tool); and the equipment chapter — what to carry, how to maintain it, and how to improvise what you lack. The survival kit is built around the same core as the Army manual: cutting tool, fire starter, water container, cordage, signaling device, first-aid kit, navigation aids. → `references/sere-medicine-climate-environments.md`

## 5. Water and Food — The Long Game

**5.1. Water (Ch. 16).** Requirements (2 quarts/day cold, 5–6 quarts/day hot/exertion), sources and indicators, collection (rain, transpiration, solar still, dew, bamboo, vines), and purification — boiling is safest (1 minute at sea level, 3 minutes above 8,000 feet), iodine 5 drops per quart (10 if cloudy or cold). Treat all surface water as contaminated.

**5.2. Food (Ch. 17).** The longest chapter in the food section of any of these manuals: plants by region, the Universal Edibility Test (24 hours, one plant at a time), traps and snares, fishing, hunting, butchering, cooking, and preservation. The priorities are the same as the Army manual — plants first, traps second, hunting last — but the plant coverage is global, with regional tables. The handbook's rule: **never eat what you cannot positively identify, and never test mushrooms.** → `references/sere-water-food-navigation.md`

## 6. Land Navigation, Travel, and Rivers

**6.1. Land navigation (Ch. 18).** Map and compass, dead reckoning, terrain association, celestial navigation (Polaris, Southern Cross, sun, moon), the shadow-stick and watch methods, pace count, and field-expedient direction finding. The handbook's navigation chapter is a full course in itself; the concise field version is the `land-navigation` skill (FM 3-25.26).

**6.2. Land travel and evacuation techniques (Ch. 19).** Movement discipline: travel at night when evading, rest by day; move along covered routes; avoid ridgelines and open ground; cross danger areas quickly and at the narrowest point; mark your trail for rescuers but not for the enemy. Evacuation techniques for the injured: carries and improvised litters.

**6.3. River travel (Ch. 20).** Rivers are highways in some environments and barriers in others. Crossing: choose the slowest, shallowest section, cross at an angle downstream, use a stick as a third leg, unbuckle the pack. Travel by raft or improvised flotation where the river is the route — but never tie yourself to a rope across a river. → `references/sere-water-food-navigation.md`

## 7. Signaling, Recovery, Evasion, Urban

**7.1. Signaling and communication (Ch. 21).** The handbook's signaling chapter is the most complete in the vault: signal mirrors, fires (three fires in a triangle is distress), smoke (green boughs for white smoke, rubber/oil for black), whistles (three blasts), ground-to-air signals (GTAS), and radio procedures. **GTAS standards: at least 3 feet wide and 18 feet long, straight lines and square corners, high contrast against the background, in a large open area visible from all directions.** Standard symbols: V = require assistance; X = require medical assistance; N = no/negative; Y = yes/affirmative; arrow = proceeding in this direction. **Aircraft acknowledgement:** rescue aircraft signal that they have seen you by radio, by flying low with landing lights on, and/or by rocking their wings.

**7.2. Recovery (Ch. 22).** The IP's role in their own rescue: know the procedures of the international search-and-rescue system (ICAO, IMCO); be prepared for helicopter pickup (approach from the front, secure loose gear, follow the crew's signals); and understand that the success of a rescue depends on the availability of rescue forces, the proximity of enemy forces, and the weather — and above all on the IP's knowledge of what to do.

**7.3. Evasion (Ch. 23).** The longest single chapter. Evasion is the art of not being found: camouflage (match the darker and lighter qualities of the background; avoid contrasts of hue; eliminate shine — skin, metal, glass, watches, and buckles all betray you, especially at night), movement (travel at night, rest by day, use covered routes), concealment, deception, and the evasion plan of action. The chapter covers tracking and counter-tracking, dogs, and the psychology of the evader. The governing principle: **the best evasion is never being detected in the first place.**

**7.4. Urban (Ch. 24).** Survival and evasion in a city: shelter in abandoned structures, water from urban sources (with purification — urban water is as contaminated as any surface water), food from markets and discarded sources, movement through urban terrain, blending with the local population, and the specific dangers of urban environments (disease, violence, surveillance). The urban chapter is the one the Army's field manual does not have, and it is the most directly useful for a civilian disaster scenario. → `references/sere-signaling-evasion-urban.md`

## 8. The Survival Kit and How This Handbook Relates to the Army Manual

**8.1. The kit (Ch. 15).** The handbook's equipment chapter builds the kit around the same core as the Army manual, with the Air Force's emphasis on compactness and weight — an aircrew kit must fit in a seat pan or vest:

| Item | Why it is non-negotiable |
|---|---|
| Cutting tool | Knife — the single most valuable survival tool |
| Fire starter | Matches in a waterproof container, ferro rod, lighter |
| Water container + purification | Canteen or bag; iodine tablets; a way to boil |
| Cordage | Paracord or improvised plant-fiber cord |
| Signaling device | Mirror, whistle, strobe, panel or dye marker |
| First-aid kit | Dressings, bandages, pain relief, blister care |
| Navigation aids | Compass, map of the area, and the knowledge to use them without either |

Maintain the kit, inspect it on a schedule, and know where every item is by touch. A kit you cannot find in the dark is a kit you do not have.

**8.2. How to use this skill alongside the others.** The Army's ATP 3-50.21 (`survival` skill) is the concise field version of this material — read it first for a quick answer. This handbook is the deep reference: use it when the question is about evasion, captivity and the Code of Conduct, recovery procedures, open-sea survival, or urban survival — topics the Army manual covers briefly or not at all. For medical emergencies, the `first-aid` and `tccc-casualty-response` skills are the treatment references; this handbook's medicine chapter is the improvisation reference for when there is no medic and no evacuation. For pure navigation technique, the `land-navigation` skill (FM 3-25.26) is the detailed course; this handbook's navigation chapter is the field-expedient version.

## Output Contract

When asked a SERE question, answer in this order: (1) which of the three IP goals is at stake (maintain life, maintain honor, return) and what the immediate priority is; (2) the procedure with the numbers that matter; (3) the environment-specific variation (the answer changes between desert, jungle, arctic, sea, and city); (4) what NOT to do. Cite the chapter (e.g., "AFH 10-644, Ch. 21 — Signaling and Communication"). If the question is a short field problem (how to purify water, start a fire, splint a fracture), answer from the `survival` or `first-aid` skills first — this skill is the deep reference behind them. If the question is about evasion, captivity, recovery, or urban survival, this skill is the primary source.

## Operating Rules

1. **The mission is return — early, and in good physical and mental condition.** Every decision is judged against it.
2. **Will to survive is the decisive factor.** Skills without will are wasted; will without skills is limited — build both.
3. **Prepare before the isolating event.** An evasion plan made on the ground is a plan made too late.
4. **Water before food, shelter before comfort, concealment before movement.** Priorities are set by what kills first and what exposes you.
5. **The Code of Conduct governs captivity.** Name, rank, service number, date of birth — nothing more.
6. **Signal for recovery, but signal smart.** A signal that brings rescuers is good; a signal that brings the enemy is fatal. Know which you are signaling.
7. **Act like the natives.** Local people have already solved this environment.
8. **Never eat what you cannot identify.** The Universal Edibility Test is a 24-hour process; never shortcut it, and never test mushrooms.
