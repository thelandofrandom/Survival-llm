# Preparing for War (MCDP 1, Chapter 3)

## Chapter outline

Force Planning — Organization — Doctrine — Professionalism — Training — Professional Military Education — Personnel Management — Equipping.

## Force planning

- Force planning is creating and maintaining the military means: size, composition, capabilities.
- It must be driven by strategy, not budget inertia or institutional preference.
- The force must be balanced across the warfighting functions and sized for the conflicts it may actually face.

## Organization

- Structure follows how the force fights: simple, flexible, task-organized around missions.
- Clear command relationships; logistics and support integrated, not bolted on.
- The MAGTF (Marine Air-Ground Task Force) is the organizational expression of combined arms: ground, aviation, and logistics under one commander, scalable from a Marine Expeditionary Unit to a Marine Expeditionary Force.

## Doctrine

- Doctrine = fundamental principles that guide action. It tells Marines *how to think*, not *what to think*.
- Authoritative but not prescriptive. Subordinates use judgment within the framework.
- Living document: revised as experience and technology change. MCDP 1 is the capstone; all other Marine doctrine hangs from it.

## Professionalism

- Warfighting is a profession. Study, self-discipline, ethical conduct, mastery of the craft.
- Officers bear a particular duty of professional study: history, past campaigns, critical thinking about war.
- A Marine who does not study war is unprepared for it.

## Training — principles

1. Train as you fight: realistic, demanding, focused on combat conditions.
2. Train friction in: degraded communications, operating without orders, night operations, plan failure.
3. Train leaders to decide under pressure with incomplete information.
4. Scripted, safe, predictable training produces forces that collapse when reality departs from the script.
5. Live-fire, force-on-force, and free-play exercises matter more than demonstrations.

## Professional military education

- Formal education builds judgment, historical perspective, and strategic thinking — things field drill cannot provide.
- Expected throughout a career: reading, writing, debating, wargaming.
- Education is what lets a commander recognize a situation he has never personally experienced.

## Personnel management

- People are the decisive element.
- Recruiting, assignment, promotion, and retention must select for and reward initiative, judgment, boldness, adaptability, and moral courage.
- A system that promotes caution and punishes honest mistakes produces cautious commanders, whatever the doctrine says.

## Equipping

- Equipment serves doctrine, not the reverse.
- Marine priorities: mobility, flexibility, expeditionary capability — light enough to deploy fast, rugged for austere conditions, simple to maintain in the field.
- Overly complex or exquisite systems that cannot be sustained in combat are liabilities.
- Test of any weapon system: its contribution to combat power at the decisive point, not test-range performance.

## Pre-combat preparation checklist (peacetime readiness)

- [ ] Force sized and balanced to strategy, not to habit
- [ ] Command relationships clear; task organization rehearsed
- [ ] Doctrine understood at every level, not just memorized
- [ ] Training includes friction, night, degraded comms, free-play opposition
- [ ] Leaders studying war (history, current campaigns, wargames)
- [ ] Promotion and assignment rewarding initiative, not just compliance
- [ ] Equipment tested in field conditions, sustainable without a depot
