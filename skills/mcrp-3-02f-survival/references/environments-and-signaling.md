# Environments, Crossings, Signaling, Hostile Areas — MCRP 3-02F

## Desert Survival (Chapter 13)

- Water is the decisive factor; shade is life.
- Travel at night; rest in shade during the day.
- Cover the body to reduce sweat loss — long sleeves and head covering beat bare skin.
- Conserve water: limit activity, breathe through the nose, avoid alcohol.
- Water sources: animal trails and birds; dig in dry streambeds at the outside of bends; collect dew.
- **Heat injuries kill fast:** heat cramps → heat exhaustion → heatstroke. Confusion and hot, dry skin are an emergency — cool the casualty immediately.

## Tropical Survival (Chapter 14)

- Heat, humidity, insects, and disease dominate.
- The jungle gives abundant water and food but hides hazards: leeches, venomous snakes, waterborne disease.
- Keep the body dry when possible; treat all water; protect food from insects; move slowly.
- Build shelters off the ground to avoid flooding and ground insects.
- Dense plant growth makes navigation and movement hard — see Appendix C for tropical plants.

## Cold-Weather Survival (Chapter 15)

- Cold kills through hypothermia and frostbite, and it dehydrates — the thirst response weakens in cold, so drink on schedule.
- Priorities reverse: **fire and shelter before water and food**.
- Dress in layers; keep dry — sweat is the enemy, ventilate before you sweat.
- Insulate from the ground; build snow shelters (snow cave, snow trench).
- Eat more — the body burns extra fuel to stay warm.
- Never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze; never rub frostbitten tissue.

## Sea Survival (Chapter 16)

- Get out of the water and into a raft or onto flotsam — immersion in cold water kills in minutes to hours.
- Protect against sun, salt-water sores, and dehydration; do not drink seawater.
- Collect rainwater; use the solar still; fish.
- Signal with mirror, dye marker, and flares.
- Sharks: avoid splashing and blood; if attacked, strike eyes, gills, or nose.

## Expedient Water Crossings (Chapter 17)

- Cross at the safest point — a wide, slow stretch rather than a narrow, fast one.
- Use expedient flotation (poncho raft, log raft, flotation bag) for equipment and non-swimmers.
- Never cross alone if it can be avoided; use a rope or line where the current is strong.
- Keep footwear on for footing and protection; remove heavy clothing that will drag you down.
- In hostile areas a crossing is a danger area: reconnoiter both banks, cross quickly, move clear of the far bank before resting.

## Field-Expedient Direction Finding (Chapter 18)

- **Stick-and-shadow:** mark a shadow tip, wait 15–30 minutes, mark again — the line runs west to east.
- **Watch method:** hour hand at the sun; halfway between the hour hand and 12 o'clock is south (Northern Hemisphere).
- **Stars:** Polaris in the north; the Southern Cross in the south.
- Moss does **not** reliably grow on the north side of trees.

## Signaling Techniques (Chapter 19)

- Signal only when ready to be found — in hostile areas, signaling invites the enemy as well as rescue.
- **Signaling mirror** — most effective daytime visual signal.
- **Smoke** by day (dark smoke against light background, light smoke against dark); **fire** by night.
- **Ground-to-air signals** — large, contrasting symbols in open ground.
- **Sound** — whistle blasts in groups of three, gunshots; sound carries farther than shouting and saves energy.
- Carry at least one signaling device on your body at all times.

## Survival Movement in Hostile Areas (Chapter 20)

- The "rescue at any cost" philosophy of previous conflicts is not likely to be possible in future conflicts. Adversaries have made great progress in air defense and radio direction finding (RDF).
- U.S. forces trapped behind enemy lines may not experience quick recovery — expect to move for extended times and distances to places less threatening to the recovery forces, and do not expect to know the type of recovery in advance.
- **Phases of planning:** decide the objective, plan the route, prepare the party, move, recover.
- Move at night when possible; use concealment; avoid trails and open ground; cross danger areas quickly; establish hole-up sites to rest, reassess, treat injuries, and plan.

## Camouflage (Chapter 21)

- Break up your outline with natural materials, mud, and charcoal on skin and equipment.
- Avoid shiny objects and strong smells.
- Camouflage is a discipline, not a coat of paint — it fails the moment you move carelessly, leave a trail, or silhouette yourself.

## Contact with People (Chapter 22)

- Observe before approaching: watch what they eat, wear, and avoid.
- Approach openly but cautiously; respect customs; never take food or shelter by force.
- In occupied territory, assume some locals may report you — balance the need for help against the risk of betrayal.

## Survival in Man-Made Hazards (Chapter 23)

- **Nuclear, biological, chemical:** find protective shelter immediately — an existing structure improved against fallout, or a trench/foxhole.
- Protection depends on **time, distance, and shielding** — limit exposure time, maximize distance, use shielding.
- Avoid detection and capture while sheltering.
- Decontaminate by washing with water and removing contaminated clothing; treat contaminated water as undrinkable until purified.
