# MCRP 3-02F Survival

## Purpose

MCRP 3-02F, *Survival* (U.S. Marine Corps, undated, 571 pages) — the Marine Corps survival manual. It is the Marine edition of the same classic survival system as FM 21-76 (the document is headed "FM 21-76/MCRP 3-02F"): 23 chapters built entirely on the keyword **SURVIVAL**, from the psychology of survival through medicine, shelter, water, fire, and food, the four environment chapters, and the hostile-area chapters on movement, camouflage, contact with people, and man-made hazards. Where FM 21-76 reads as an Army field manual, MCRP 3-02F reads as a combat manual — its first chapter opens on the battlefield, not the classroom: if you are in a combat situation, find a place where you can conceal yourself from the enemy, because security takes priority.

The manual's opening warning sets its tone. Chapter 1 states the whole doctrine in one word: "This manual is based entirely on the keyword SURVIVAL. The letters in this word can help guide you in your actions in any survival situation. Whenever faced with a survival situation, remember the word SURVIVAL."

**Relationship to other skills in this vault.** MCRP 3-02F and FM 21-76 (1992) are the same manual lineage — same chapter structure, same procedures, same numbers. If you have the FM 21-76 skill, you have most of this content; the difference is framing and emphasis, not substance. For current Army doctrine, ATP 3-50.21 (2018) supersedes FM 21-76; the Marine Corps has no newer standalone survival manual in this vault, so MCRP 3-02F remains the Marine reference. For the condensed multiservice procedures, see the FM 21-76-1 skill; for the full SERE system including resistance and escape, see the SERE skill (AFH 10-644).

## 1. Introduction — The SURVIVAL Actions (Chapter 1)

**1.1. S — Size up the situation.** In combat, find concealment first — security takes priority. Use hearing, smell, and sight to get a feel for the battlefield: what is the enemy doing — advancing, holding, retreating? Consider what is developing when you make your survival plan. **Size up your surroundings:** every environment has a rhythm or pattern — animal and bird noises and movements, insect sounds, enemy traffic, civilian movements. Learn the pattern, and a break in it warns you. **Size up your physical condition:** the pressure of battle or the trauma of the situation may have made you overlook wounds. Check yourself and give first aid; prevent further harm — in any climate, drink plenty of water; in cold or wet climates, put on additional clothing to prevent hypothermia. **Size up your equipment:** in the heat of battle you may have lost or damaged equipment. Check what you have and what condition it is in.

**1.2. U — Undue haste makes waste.** Panic-driven rushing wastes energy and creates mistakes. Think before you act.

**1.3. R — Remember where you are.** Orient yourself; know your location relative to safety, water, and danger.

**1.4. V — Vanquish fear and panic.** Fear is normal. Acknowledge it, then act on training.

**1.5. I — Improvise.** Everything is a potential tool. Ordinary objects have second uses.

**1.6. V — Value living.** The will to live is a decision. People who give up psychologically die long before their bodies fail.

**1.7. A — Act like the natives.** Local people have already solved the problems of this environment — watch what they eat, wear, and avoid.

**1.8. L — Learn basic skills.** Skills practiced in training become confidence in isolation. → `references/survival-pattern-and-kits.md`

## 2. Psychology of Survival (Chapter 2)

**2.1. Stress is a condition, not a disease.** Stress is the body's reaction to pressure — fight, flight, or freeze. In moderate doses it sharpens performance; in excess it becomes distress and causes panic, forgetfulness, carelessness, angry outbursts, withdrawal, and thoughts of death. The survivor's job is to manage stress, not eliminate it — through relaxation skills, time management, assertiveness, and cognitive restructuring (controlling how you view the situation). You can control your response even when you cannot control your circumstances.

**2.2. The refusal to give up.** "The will to survive" can also be considered "the refusal to give up." Common survival stressors — fear, anxiety, anger, frustration, guilt, depression, loneliness — are all normal reactions; expect them and do not mistake them for failure. Countermeasures are routine and purpose: set small achievable goals, keep a schedule, stay busy with survival tasks, maintain hope through a realistic plan for recovery. A survivor working a plan is far harder to break than one waiting passively.

## 3. Survival Planning and Survival Kits (Chapter 3)

**3.1. Preparation.** Survival planning is nothing more than realizing something could happen that would put you in a survival situation and taking steps to increase your chances. Include survival considerations in mission planning; know your route and area; keep dental problems fixed and immunizations current. All Army aircraft normally carry survival kits for the type of area they fly over — over-water, hot-climate, and aviator survival vest kits (Appendix A). Knowing what those kits contain helps you build your own.

**3.2. Layering.** The environment decides what you need; how you carry it decides how much. Most important items on the body (map and compass always on the body), less important items on load-bearing equipment, bulky items in the rucksack. Pick multi-use items; never duplicate a function. The case should be water-repellent or waterproof, easy to carry or attach to the body, able to accept variously sized components, and durable.

**3.3. Contents.** First aid items; water purification tablets or drops; fire-starting equipment; signaling items; food-procurement items; shelter items. Examples: lighter, metal match, waterproof matches; snare wire; signaling mirror; wrist compass; fish and snare line; fishhooks; candle; small hand lens; oxytetracycline tablets; solar blanket; surgical blades; butterfly sutures; condoms for water storage; Chap Stick; needle and thread; knife. Include a weapon only if the situation dictates. → `references/survival-pattern-and-kits.md`

## 4. Basic Survival Medicine (Chapter 4)

**4.1. Health maintenance.** Foremost among the problems that compromise a survivor are medical problems from parachute descent and landing, extreme climates, ground combat, evasion, and illnesses contracted in captivity. Survivors have reported that the inability to treat themselves produced apathy and helplessness that led to capture or surrender; the ability to treat themselves raised morale and aided return. One person with a fair amount of basic medical knowledge can make the difference for many.

**4.2. Water and hygiene.** At 20°C (68°F) with average daily exertion, the average adult loses — and therefore requires — **2 to 3 liters of water daily**. Heat, cold, intense activity, high altitude, burns, or illness increase losses. Dehydration decreases efficiency and increases susceptibility to shock if injured. Maintain high personal hygiene standards: wash hands, keep feet dry and sound, protect against insects and parasites.

**4.3. Lifesaving priorities.** Airway and breathing first, then stop severe bleeding with direct pressure and pressure dressings, then treat for shock (warm, flat, legs elevated unless head, chest, or abdominal injury forbids it). Splint fractures as found — do not straighten a deformed limb. Cool a heat casualty in shade with water and airflow; warm a cold casualty gradually; never rub frostbitten tissue and never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze. Field remedies (chlorine bleach, alcohol, vinegar, concentrated salt water, iodine for fungal infections) are used with caution. For rashes: if moist, keep dry; if dry, keep moist; do not scratch. → `references/survival-pattern-and-kits.md`

## 5. Shelters, Water, and Fire (Chapters 5–7)

**5.1. Shelters.** A shelter protects against weather, cold, heat, insects, and observation. Site selection matters more than construction: high, dry ground away from standing water and animal trails, near water and materials, not in a drainage or avalanche path, concealed from the enemy in hostile areas. Types: poncho lean-to, poncho tent, natural-material shelters; snow cave and snow trench in cold weather; below-ground shade shelter in the desert. Insulate from the ground (ground contact steals heat faster than air); low entrance away from wind; build just big enough — a shelter too large cannot be warmed.

**5.2. Water procurement.** Find water by following animal trails downhill, watching birds at dawn and dusk, noting green vegetation, and watching insects. Collect rain, dew, transpiration bags, solar stills, green bamboo, vines (cut high first, then low — avoid milky or bitter sap), and banana stumps.

**5.3. Water purification — the numbers.**

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Boiling (safest) | Rolling boil **1 minute at sea level; +1 minute per additional 300 m altitude**; or 10 minutes anywhere |
| Iodine, 2% tincture | **5 drops per quart clear water; 10 drops if cloudy or cold**; wait **30 minutes** |
| Purification tablets | Follow directions provided |
| Filtration (clearing only) | Settle 12 hours or filter through sand, crushed rock, charcoal, cloth — still must purify after |
| Deodorizing | Charcoal from fire; stand 45 minutes |

Rainwater in clean containers or from plants is usually safe. Purify all lake, pond, swamp, spring, and stream water. Never drink urine or seawater. Untreated water risks dysentery, cholera, typhoid, blood flukes, and leeches.

**5.4. Firecraft.** Three materials, prepared before the first spark: **tinder** (absolutely dry, ignites from a spark), **kindling**, **fuel**. Fire lays: tepee (lights easily, burns hot), lean-to (good in wind), cross-ditch (ventilated by a shallow ditch). Ignition, most reliable first: **flint and steel** (most reliable direct-spark method), **convex lens** (bright sunny days only), **battery and wire**, **gunpowder** (extremely careful), and friction methods (bow drill, fire plow, hand drill — learn in training, not in an emergency). Carry at least two ignition methods. → `references/water-fire-shelter.md`

## 6. Food, Plants, and Animals (Chapters 8–11)

**6.1. Food procurement.** Food is the lowest immediate priority — a healthy person can survive weeks without it — but it sustains energy, warmth, and morale for movement and work. Fish, trap, snare, and gather. Snares work while unattended: set them on trails to water, feeding, and bedding areas and at den mouths. With wire, use a figure-8 (locking) loop — once tightened it locks and the animal cannot escape.

**6.2. Survival use of plants.** Learn the edible plants of your area of operations before you deploy — Appendix B gives detailed descriptions and photographs of common edible plants, and Appendix C covers tropical plants. Identification in the field is a training task, not an improvisation.

**6.3. The Universal Edibility Test.** Test only one part of one plant at a time, and only if the plant is abundant — each part takes more than 24 hours to test. Avoid plants with milky sap (dandelion is a safe, recognizable exception), spines, fine hairs, or thorns (prickly pear and thistles are exceptions), mushrooms and fungus (avoid entirely), and umbrella-shaped flower clusters (hemlock). Even after a food tests safe, eat in moderation. The full step-by-step test is in `references/food-plants-animals.md`.

**6.4. Poisonous plants and dangerous animals.** Danger signs for plants: milky sap, almond scent, spines or fine hairs, umbrella-shaped flower clusters, beans or bulbs, seeds inside pods (Appendix C has photographs and descriptions). For animals: know the dangerous species of the area before operating there (Appendix D covers dangerous animals; Appendix E covers snakes; Appendix F covers venomous fish and toxic fish and mollusks). Prevention beats treatment — watch where you put hands and feet, shake out clothing and bedding, avoid carrion and dens, use care turning over rocks and logs. For snakebite: keep the casualty calm and still, keep the bitten limb below heart level, remove constricting items, evacuate — do not cut, suck, or apply a tourniquet.

**6.5. Field-expedient weapons, tools, and equipment (Chapter 12).** Improvise clubs, spears, slings, and traps from available material; fashion cutting and digging tools from stone, bone, and metal scrap. The knife is the single most valuable field tool — protect it, keep it sharp, use it for everything. → `references/food-plants-animals.md`

## 7. The Environments (Chapters 13–16)

**7.1. Desert survival.** Water is the decisive factor and shade is life. Travel at night, rest in shade during the day, cover the body to reduce sweat loss, conserve water by limiting activity and breathing through the nose, avoid alcohol. Water sources: animal trails and birds, digging in dry streambeds at the outside of bends, dew. Heat injuries kill fast — confusion and hot, dry skin are an emergency; cool the casualty immediately.

**7.2. Tropical survival.** Heat, humidity, insects, and disease dominate. The jungle provides abundant water and food but hides hazards: leeches, venomous snakes, waterborne disease. Keep the body dry when possible, treat all water, protect food from insects, move slowly, build shelters off the ground to avoid flooding and ground insects. The density of plant growth makes navigation and movement hard — see Appendix C for tropical plants.

**7.3. Cold-weather survival.** Cold kills through hypothermia and frostbite, and it dehydrates — the thirst response weakens in cold, so drink on schedule. Priorities reverse: fire and shelter before water and food. Dress in layers, keep dry (sweat is the enemy — ventilate before you sweat), insulate from the ground, build snow shelters, eat more. Never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze; never rub frostbitten tissue.

**7.4. Sea survival.** Get out of the water and into a raft or onto flotsam — immersion in cold water kills in minutes to hours. Protect against sun, salt-water sores, and dehydration; do not drink seawater. Collect rainwater, use the solar still, fish. Signal with mirror, dye marker, and flares. Sharks: avoid splashing and blood; if attacked, strike the eyes, gills, or nose. → `references/environments-and-signaling.md`

## 8. Water Crossings, Direction Finding, and Signaling (Chapters 17–19)

**8.1. Expedient water crossings.** Rivers and streams are obstacles and routes at once. Cross at the safest point — a wide, slow stretch rather than a narrow, fast one — and use expedient flotation (a poncho raft, a log raft, a flotation bag) for equipment and non-swimmers. Never cross alone if it can be avoided; use a rope or line where the current is strong; remove heavy clothing that will drag you down but keep footwear on for footing and protection. In hostile areas, a crossing is a danger area: reconnoiter both banks, cross quickly, and move clear of the far bank before resting.

**8.2. Field-expedient direction finding.** Without a compass: stick-and-shadow (mark a shadow tip, wait 15–30 minutes, mark again — the line runs west to east), watch method (hour hand at the sun, halfway to 12 o'clock is south in the Northern Hemisphere), stars (Polaris in the north; the Southern Cross in the south). Moss does not reliably grow on the north side of trees — do not rely on folklore.

**8.3. Signaling techniques.** Signal only when ready to be found — in hostile areas, signaling invites the enemy as well as rescue. The signaling mirror is the most effective daytime visual signal; smoke by day (dark smoke against light background, light smoke against dark); fire by night; ground-to-air signals in large, contrasting symbols; sound — whistle blasts in groups of three, gunshots — carries farther than shouting and saves energy. Carry at least one signaling device on your body at all times. → `references/environments-and-signaling.md`

## 9. Survival in Hostile Areas (Chapters 20–23)

**9.1. Survival movement in hostile areas.** The manual is blunt about the new reality: the "rescue at any cost" philosophy of previous conflicts is not likely to be possible in future conflicts. Adversaries have made great progress in air defense and radio direction finding (RDF). U.S. forces trapped behind enemy lines may not experience quick recovery — Marines may have to move for extended times and distances to places less threatening to the recovery forces, and will not likely know the type of recovery to expect. Preparation and training improve the chances of success. Phases of planning: decide the objective, plan the route, prepare the party, move, and recover. Move at night when possible, use concealment, avoid trails and open ground, cross danger areas quickly, establish hole-up sites to rest, reassess, treat injuries, and plan.

**9.2. Camouflage.** Break up your outline with natural materials, mud, and charcoal on skin and equipment. Avoid shiny objects and strong smells. Camouflage is a discipline, not a coat of paint — it fails the moment you move carelessly, leave a trail, or silhouette yourself.

**9.3. Contact with people.** Observe before approaching: watch what they eat, wear, and avoid. Approach openly but cautiously, respect customs, never take food or shelter by force. In occupied territory, assume some locals may report you — balance the need for help against the risk of betrayal. Goodwill may be the difference between recovery and capture.

**9.4. Survival in man-made hazards.** Nuclear, biological, and chemical hazards demand immediate protective action: find shelter (an existing structure improved against fallout, or a trench/foxhole), limit exposure time, maximize distance from the source, and use shielding — protection depends on time, distance, and shielding. Avoid detection and capture while sheltering; decontaminate by washing with water and removing contaminated clothing; treat contaminated water as undrinkable until purified. → `references/environments-and-signaling.md`

## Output Contract

When answering from MCRP 3-02F, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (water quantities, purification doses and wait times, boil times), then the cautions — and state the priority order explicitly, since desert, tropical, cold, and sea priorities differ. Lead with the hostile-area framing when the question is about evasion or movement behind enemy lines: the manual's own warning is that quick rescue may not come, and the survivor may have to move for extended times and distances. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote sparingly and briefly. Where FM 21-76 and MCRP 3-02F agree (they almost always do), either citation is fine; where current doctrine (ATP 3-50.21) differs, follow the newer manual and say so.

## Operating Rules

1. **Security takes priority.** In combat, conceal yourself from the enemy before anything else.
2. **Remember SURVIVAL.** The whole manual is one keyword; when in doubt, work the letters.
3. **Quick rescue may not come.** Plan to move for extended times and distances to a place less threatening to the recovery forces.
4. **Priority by what kills fastest.** Medicine first, then protection, then sustenance, then navigation — reassessed constantly.
5. **Purify all water.** Boiling is the safest method; iodine is 5 drops per quart (10 if cloudy or cold), 30 minutes.
6. **Never eat an unknown plant whole.** One part, one plant, more than 24 hours to test; mushrooms never.
7. **Signal only when ready to be found.** In hostile areas, a signal is as visible to the enemy as to rescue.
