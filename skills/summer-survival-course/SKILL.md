# USMC MWTC Summer Survival Course Handbook (2002)

## Purpose

Summer Survival Course Handbook, U.S. Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center (MWTC), Bridgeport, California, 2002, 180 pages — the student handout set for the summer survival course taught at 6,000+ feet in the Sierra Nevada. Sixteen chapters plus six appendices, each chapter built as a terminal learning objective with enabling objectives: the Marine must be able to do the thing, without references, to pass. This is a practical field course in staying alive and getting recovered in mountain terrain in summer conditions — which at Bridgeport still means cold nights, sudden weather, and hypothermia risk.

Two facts frame the whole handbook. First, the will to survive is the precondition: "if you do not have the right attitude, you may not survive." Second, hypothermia is the number-one killer of people in a survival situation — and it does not require extreme cold: most cases occur when the temperature is between 30 and 50°F, the normal temperature range at MWTC outside the harshest winter. Summer survival in the mountains is largely cold-weather survival with longer days.

Note the companion volume: the Winter Survival Course Handbook (2002, same course series, `winter-survival-course` skill) shares Chapters 1–11 and 13–16 almost verbatim and adds expedient snowshoes and avalanche/ice hazards. Where the two differ, this skill covers the summer-specific material: foraging for plants and insects, survival fishing, and summer tracking.

## 1. Requirements for survival — SURVIVAL, stressors, priorities

**The SURVIVAL acronym** is the mental checklist: **S**ize up the situation (conceal yourself from the enemy; use your senses before making a plan), **S**ize up your surroundings (determine the rhythm/pattern of the area; note animal and bird noises and movement; note enemy traffic and civilian movement), **S**ize up your physical condition (check wounds, give yourself first aid, prevent further harm, evaluate self and unit), **S**ize up your equipment — then **U**ndue haste makes waste, **R**emember where you are, **V**anquish fear and panic, **I**mprovise, **V**alue living, **A**ct like the natives, **L**earn basic skills. The acronym is a memory aid for a sequence: assess before acting, and act deliberately.

**Survival stressors** — the forces that kill attitude: injury, illness, and death; uncertainty and lack of control; environment (heat, cold, rain, wind, altitude); hunger and thirst; fatigue; isolation. The handbook treats stressors as predictable and therefore manageable: name the stressor, counter it with a task.

**Priorities of work (Chapter 1, section 5).** Every situation is unique, but the general guideline is blocks of time:

- **First 24 hours (critical):** make an initial estimate — enemy, weather, terrain, time of day, available resources determine order — then: (1) shelter, (2) fire, (3) water, (4) signaling.
- **Second 24 hours:** expand knowledge of the area — (1) tools and weapons (travel a short distance; notice edible food and game trails), (2) traps and snares (moving further out locates your shelter from various vantage points and identifies likely avenues of approach), (3) pathguards (noise and casualty-producing guards on those avenues to secure the shelter).
- **Remainder:** continuously improve the situation until rescue.

**Group survival** depends on organizing activity: an emergency does not initially bring people together for a common goal — the more difficult and confusing the situation, the greater the group's problems. High morale must come from internal cohesiveness, not merely from circumstances. → `references/requirements-and-kit.md`

## 2. Survival kit — layered, multipurpose, six components

**Layer your kit:** keep the most important items on your body; a body-carried kit is much smaller than a vehicle-carried one. The environment dictates the items. Select items usable for more than one purpose. The case should be water-repellent or waterproof, easy to carry or attach to the body, able to accept various sized items, and durable — a first aid case, ammunition pouch, or similar.

**The six components:**

1. **Fire starting:** matches, magnifying glass, flint and steel, lighter, potassium permanganate with sugar or antifreeze, prepackaged tinder (commercial, or cotton balls and petroleum jelly).
2. **Water procurement:** disinfecting chemicals (iodine tablets, Betadine solution, iodine solution); metal container for boiling (canteen cup, kit container, any can that held no petroleum products); water-carrying items (canteen, plastic bag, clean containers).
3. **Food procurement:** fish hooks (various sizes), sinkers/weights, metal leaders and swivels, small weighted jigs; snares; 550 cord for gill net and trap construction.
4. **Signaling:** mirror, pyrotechnics (smoke, pen flares), lights (flashlight, strobe, chemlight).
5. **First aid items.**
6. **Shelter items:** cord and water-repellent material (poncho, tarp, parachute).

The kit is a system, not a shopping list: every component maps to a priority of work (shelter, fire, water, signaling, food, first aid). → `references/requirements-and-kit.md`

## 3. Water procurement — drink, don't ration

**Water intake.** Thirst is not a strong enough sensation to judge need. The plan is the **over-drink method**: drink plenty whenever water is available, particularly when eating. Water requirements increase with fever, fear or anxiety, evaporation (poor shelter, improper clothing), rationing water itself, and overwork.

**Dehydration scale — memorize it:** 5% loss of body fluids causes thirst, irritability, nausea, weakness; 10% causes dizziness, headache, inability to walk, tingling in limbs; 15% causes dim vision, painful urination, swollen tongue, deafness, numbness of skin; more than 15% can result in death.

**Incidental water.** While moving, ration until a reliable source is reached; incidental sources stretch the supply: **dew** (tie rags or tufts of fine grass around ankles, walk through dewy grass before sunrise, wring into a container, repeat) and **rainfall** (collected directly in a clean container or in non-toxic plants, generally safe without disinfecting; an inverted poncho collects it — always be ready to collect rain at a moment's notice).

**Hazardous fluids — do not drink:** seawater (sodium/magnesium salts so concentrated the body must draw fluid to eliminate them; kidneys eventually cease to function); alcohol (dehydrates, clouds judgment; super-cooled liquid can cause immediate frostbite of the throat); blood (salty, and a food — the body expends fluid to digest it); urine (body waste — drinking it returns waste the body must spend more fluid to process again).

**Water quality.** Pathogens are the primary concern, in four groups: virus, cysts, bacteria, parasites. Some resist chemicals, some are small enough to pass through microscopic holes in improvised filters, some survive extremely cold water; pathogens generally do not live in snow and ice. Water is graded in three levels of safety, with disinfection the most desired — boil where possible (the metal container in the kit exists for this), chemically disinfect otherwise. → `references/water-shelter-fire.md`

## 4. Expedient shelters and fires — shelter first, fire second

**Shelter site selection:** near firewood, water, and signaling position; with ventilation (a shelter without ventilation accumulates carbon monoxide and smoke, which is dangerous); protected from wind, falling limbs, and runoff.

**Shelter types:** **poncho shelter** (easiest — cord plus any water-repellent material: poncho, parachute, tarp); **lean-to** (heavily forested areas, limited cordage, effective but limited protection); **double lean-to** (two lean-tos together, for 2–5 individuals); **hollow logs** (cleaned or dug out, enhanced with ponchos or tarps). Insulate yourself from the ground — conduction steals heat through direct contact with cold ground, snow, or rock; a mat or pack between you and the ground is not comfort, it is survival.

**Fire lays.** Two types: **fire pit** and **Dakota hole**. The Dakota hole is the tactical fire lay — a hole with a separate air-draft tunnel: it burns with less smoke, less visible light, and less spark, and concentrates heat for cooking. No fire is 100% tactical, but the Dakota hole is the closest.

**Fire building, in order:** gather a double handful of tinder and an extra double handful stored dry for the following morning (dew moistens tinder); tinder is fine and dry (sticks no larger than a toothpick's diameter); kindling is ignited by the tinder — softwoods burn hot and fast but do not last; hardwoods (broadleaf trees) burn slower with less smoke and leave a good bed of coals. Heating and signaling fires are built differently from cooking fires — a signaling fire is built to make smoke.

**Ignition methods:** matches, lighter, flint and steel (sparks onto prepared tinder; some designs include magnesium), magnifying glass on bright sunny days, and friction — bow drill (smooth motion, smoke appears, more downward pressure, saw the bow until a thick layer of smoke accumulates around the depression, then an ember) and fire plow. Troubleshooting is taught explicitly: no smoke means drill and fire board are the same wood or the wood is wrong; smoke but no ember means the U-shaped notch was not cut into the center. → `references/water-shelter-fire.md`

## 5. Core values and mountain leadership challenges

Leadership challenges peculiar to mountain operations: altitude, cold, steep terrain, and isolation degrade judgment before they degrade the body. The chapter pairs the Marine Corps core values with the practical leadership problem of a survival group — organizing activity, sustaining morale from internal cohesiveness, making decisions with incomplete information, and keeping the group working the priorities instead of arguing about them. A leader who cannot keep the group on shelter-fire-water-signaling in the first 24 hours has failed at the only leadership test that matters in a survival situation. → `references/fieldcraft.md`

## 6. Signaling and recovery — be found

**Methods of communication:** pyrotechnics (star clusters and smoke grenades — in snow, smoke grenades need flotation or they sink out of sight), mirror, lights (flashlight, strobe, chemlight), smoke from a signaling fire, ground-to-air panels, and improvised signals. The kit's signaling items (mirror, pyrotechnics, lights) exist because signaling is a first-24-hours priority — rescue cannot happen if no one knows where you are.

**Recovery operations:** the survivor's job is to make himself findable and then to follow the recovery force's instructions exactly. Do not move away from a signaled position without leaving a clear trail and message. The chapter covers immediate actions on contact, rally points, and communication and signaling discipline during evasion and recovery. → `references/fieldcraft.md`

## 7. Survival navigation — day, night, and improvised

**Daytime navigation:** sun, shadows, and terrain association; the sun's path as a compass (with the seasonal and latitude corrections the handbook teaches); using prominent terrain features as handrails and backstops. **Nighttime navigation:** stars (including the North Star), moon, and the discipline of moving slowly and checking often — night movement in mountains is how ankles and lives are lost.

**Survival navigation techniques:** dead reckoning with pace count and time, natural direction indicators (wind-shaped trees, snowmelt patterns, animal movement), and the cardinal rule — know where your shelter is from every point you reach, so you can always get back. The second-24-hours work of traps and snares doubles as navigation training: moving out and back locates the shelter from various vantage points. → `references/fieldcraft.md`

## 8. Survival traps and snares — snaring is the most effective means

In any environment, **snaring is the most effective means to take game**. Snares are much easier and less time-consuming to construct than traps. Three things determine success: **location, presentation, and construction**.

**Kill methods:** strangle (snare around the neck), crush (deadfall), and the spring-pole family. A snare is a loop that closes around the animal's neck; wire works better than rope or cord. A **locking device is imperative** — it secures the snare around the neck so it cannot slip open. Construction rules: support the snare with a split stick so it fires correctly (the locking device sits next to the split, or at 12 o'clock for a weighted snare); anchor the snare or attach it to a drag; load it so it fires quickly; size the loop to the intended animal so it holds around the neck.

**Traps:** deadfalls and their variants crush the animal; they take more time and material than snares and are used where snares cannot be set. Set on game trails and near water and food sources — location is found by reading sign (see Chapter 13, Tracking), not by guessing. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 9. Survival uses of game — butchering, cooking, using everything

Game is food, but also tools and material. The chapter covers field dressing and butchering, **cooking of meats** (thorough cooking — game meat carries parasites that heat kills), and **specific parts**: hides for shelter and cordage, bones for tools, sinew for binding, fat for calories and fire. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be replaced. The caution throughout: a kill you cannot preserve is a kill wasted — cook and eat, or smoke and dry, promptly. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 10. Expedient tools, weapons, and equipment

Improvise what the kit does not contain: cutting tools from stone and metal, cordage from plant fiber and sinew, containers from bark and hide, weapons for defense and for taking game. The design rule is the same as the kit's: multipurpose, durable, and built from what the environment actually offers. A tool that does one thing well beats a tool that does five things badly — except in the kit, where weight forces multipurpose choices. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 11. Foraging for plants and insects — the rules of avoidance

**Determining edibility.** About 300,000 classified plants exist; a plant must be edible (preferably palatable), fairly abundant, and — if it has an inedible or poisonous relative — distinguishable from it by the average eye. Usually one special part is edible: stalk, fruit, or nut.

**Types of plants to avoid (memorize the list):** mushrooms and fungi (toxic peptides, a protein-based poison with no taste); plants with umbrella-shaped flowers (poison water hemlock is in this family, along with carrots, celery, dill, and parsley); beans, peas, and seeds in pods — the whole legume family (they absorb minerals such as selenium; locoweed's fame comes from selenium); 3-leafed and whorled-leaf growth patterns (Lupinas genus and other poisonous plants); all bulbs as a general rule (tulips, death camas); white and yellow berries (almost all poisonous; about half of all red berries are poisonous); plants with milky sap; plants with shiny leaves; plants that irritate the skin (poison ivy and relatives).

**Preparing an unknown plant:** any plant that cannot be positively identified is prepared properly before testing — boil one part (leaves, flowers, stems, or roots) in a canteen cup of water. Many harmful toxins are destroyed by heat or are water-soluble, though some are exceptions. Do not prepare plants from the avoid list at all. Insects are a parallel food source — abundant, high in protein, and available when plants are not; the chapter covers which to take and how to prepare them. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 12. Survival fishing — lines, nets, and patience

Fish are the most reliable animal protein in mountain country: streams and lakes are everywhere, fish are less wary than game, and fishing gear (hooks, sinkers, leaders, swivels, jigs) is light enough to live in the kit permanently. The chapter covers improvised lines and poles, trotlines and gill nets (550 cord serves for net construction), bait from insects and grubs, and the reading of water — where fish hold in current, depth, and cover. A set line fishes while you do other priorities; that is its advantage over hunting. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 13. Tracking — reading sign

Tracking is reading the story the ground tells: footprints, scat, browse, trails, beds, and feeding sign. It serves three survival purposes: finding game (where to set traps and snares), finding water (animal trails converge on it), and finding people (or avoiding them — tracking is also counter-tracking, knowing what sign you leave). The second-24-hours trap work and the tracking chapter are one skill: set where the sign says animals go, not where you hope they go. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 14. Survival medicine — heat loss, hypothermia, frostbite

**Five ways the body loses heat:** **radiation** (direct heat loss to colder surroundings — a nude man loses about 60% of total body heat by radiation); **conduction** (direct transfer to a colder object — sitting or sleeping directly on cold ground, snow, or rock without an insulating mat or pack); **convection** (heat loss to moving air or water — moving fluid can never equalize temperature, so loss continues); **evaporation** (sweat turning to vapor — one quart of sweat, easily produced in an hour of hard work, carries away about 600 calories of heat); **respiration** (inhaled air warmed and saturated, then exhaled — a combination of convection and evaporation inside the body).

**Physical responses:** to heat — blood vessels dilate, then sweating begins. To cold — vessels constrict (blood goes to the core: brain, heart, lungs, liver, kidneys; fingers and toes get cold), then shivering (reflexive muscular contractions that generate heat).

**Hypothermia** is the state when core temperature falls to **95°F or less**. Causes: heat loss exceeding heat production, with contributing factors of ambient temperature, wind chill (affects only the improperly clothed), wet clothing, cold-water immersion, improper clothing, exhaustion, alcohol/nicotine/drugs, and injuries causing immobility, major bleeding, major burns, or head trauma. **Signs and symptoms:** the number-one sign is altered mental status — the brain literally getting cold: confusion, slurred speech, strange behavior, irritability, impaired judgment; worsening hypothermia brings loss of consciousness, and a severely hypothermic victim may appear quite dead. **Prevention:** stay dry, stay fed, stay out of the wind, use the buddy system to check each other for signs. **Treatment:** rewarm gently and gradually, never give alcohol to a hypothermia victim, handle the victim gently (a cold heart is irritable and can arrest).

**Frostbite** is the actual freezing of tissues — an entirely preventable injury: keep extremities warm, dry, and moving; do not warm fingers and toes by direct high heat once frozen. The chapter also covers heat injuries, dehydration (see Chapter 3), and the field treatment of common survival injuries. For current clinical detail, defer to the TCCC/first-aid skills; this chapter is the field-expedient version. → `references/medicine-weather-evasion.md`

## 15. Mountain weather — pressure, patterns, prediction

Mountain weather changes fast and kills the unprepared. The chapter covers pressure (reading a barometer's trend, not its absolute value — falling pressure means deteriorating weather), the mountain effects that create local weather (orographic lift, valley winds, afternoon thunderstorms), and field forecasting from clouds, wind, and temperature trends. The practical rule: plan movement and shelter around the afternoon thunderstorm pattern, and never be caught on high ground or in a drainage when the weather turns. → `references/medicine-weather-evasion.md`

## 16. Intro to evasion — immediate actions and the EPA

**Immediate actions** on contact with the enemy; **rally point** procedures; **movement** (when, how far, and how to move without leaving sign); **chance contact** (what to do when the enemy appears without warning); **communication and signaling** discipline; **recovery operations** (making contact with friendly forces and following their procedures). The **Evasion Plan of Action (EPA, Appendix A)** is the pre-written plan: where you are going, how you will signal, and what you will do at each decision point — written before it is needed, because it cannot be written well under pursuit. The **"The Edge"** guided discussion (Appendix B) and the **Survival Quick Reference Checklist** (Appendix C) are the course's memory aids; **Animal Habits** (Appendix D), **Tactical Considerations** (Appendix E), and **Grading Standards** (Appendix F) complete the handout set. → `references/medicine-weather-evasion.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a survival problem in summer mountain conditions, answer in the handbook's order: attitude and SURVIVAL assessment first, then the priorities of work by time block (first 24 hours: shelter, fire, water, signaling; second 24: tools/weapons, traps/snares, pathguards), then the chapter technique. Give the numbers the handbook gives (dehydration percentages, hypothermia at 95°F core, 60% radiant heat loss nude, 600 calories per quart of sweat) and say when a number comes from the handbook versus from general knowledge. For winter mountain conditions, use the `winter-survival-course` skill instead — same framework, different chapters (snowshoes, avalanche).

## Operating Rules

1. **Attitude first.** Without the will to survive, technique does not matter.
2. **Work the priorities in time blocks.** First 24 hours: shelter, fire, water, signaling — in the order the situation dictates.
3. **Over-drink.** Thirst is not a reliable gauge; drink whenever water is available.
4. **Hypothermia is the killer, even in summer.** 30–50°F is the danger band; stay dry, stay insulated from the ground, watch your buddy.
5. **Snares over traps.** Location, presentation, construction — in that order.
6. **Never eat from the avoid list.** Mushrooms, umbrella flowers, legumes, bulbs, white/yellow berries, milky sap, shiny leaves, skin irritants — and boil any unknown plant before testing.
7. **Signal early.** Rescue cannot happen if no one knows where you are.
