# Life in the Jungle — FM 90-5

## Fear Itself Is the Enemy

A 25th Infantry Division report from Vietnam: operating against the North Vietnamese Army along the Cambodian border, U.S. units "found that they had as much difficulty operating in the area as we did. The prisoners we captured were, as a rule, undernourished, emaciated, and sick with malaria. They stated that almost everyone in their unit had malaria, and many had died from it." Two lessons: the enemy suffers the jungle as much as you do, and "there is very little to fear from the jungle environment. Fear itself can be an enemy. A man overcome with fear is of little value in any situation."

## The Stories Are Exaggerated

Most stories about out-of-the-way jungle places were written by writers "in search of adventure rather than facts" who "exaggerated or invented many of the thrilling experiences they relate." Most Americans, especially those raised in cities, have lost the knack of taking care of themselves under all conditions — but they would be in just as much danger lost in the mountains of western Pennsylvania as in the jungles of Southeast Asia. The only difference is that a man is less likely to panic when lost in his homeland than when lost abroad.

## Climate

- The discomforts of tropical climates are often exaggerated, but the heat is more persistent, and in humid air it seems worse than the same temperature in a dry climate.
- "There may be more suffering from cold in the tropics than from the heat": chilly days and nights are common, and in some jungles in the winter months the nights are cold enough to require a wool blanket or poncho liner for sleeping.
- Rainfall in many parts of the tropics is much greater than in most temperate zones.
- Tropical dwellers plan activities to stay under shelter during the rainy and hotter portions of the day.

## Insects and Malaria

- Malaria-carrying mosquitoes are probably the most harmful of the tropical insects.
- Precautions: taking Dapsone and chloroquine-primaquine; using insect repellent; wearing clothing that covers as much of the body as possible.
- Malaria is more common in populated areas than in uninhabited jungle; be especially cautious at dawn and dusk.
- Insects also cause yellow fever, dengue, and other diseases; dysentery, cholera, and hepatitis come from impure water and food.

## Leeches and Other Hazards

- Leeches are common in many jungle areas. Do not pull one off forcibly — part of the leech may remain in the skin. Leeches will release themselves if left alone; leech straps around the legs prevent them attaching.
- Sweat bees are tiny, stingless, and annoying, but leave when sweating stops.
- Poisonous vegetation, including tree nettles, can be avoided by learning what to look for and wearing protective clothing.

## Heat Disorders

| Disorder | Cause | Symptoms | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heat exhaustion | Loss of too much water and salt | Dizziness, nausea, headache, cramps, rapid weak pulse, cool wet skin | Cool shaded rest, loosen clothing, elevate feet, cool salt water — **two salt tablets dissolved in a canteen of water** (natural seawater should not be used) |
| Heat cramps | Loss of too much salt | Painful muscle cramps, relieved as soon as salt is replaced | Same as heat exhaustion |
| Heatstroke | Breakdown of the body's heat control; most likely in the unacclimated or those recently ill with diarrhea; can kill if not treated quickly | Hot, red, dry skin (most important sign); no sweating when sweating would be expected; very high temperature (**105 to 110 degrees**); rapid pulse; spots before eyes; headache, nausea, dizziness, mental confusion; sudden collapse | Cool immediately — creek or stream, pour canteens of water over him, fan, ice if available; cool salt water if conscious; rub arms and legs rapidly; evacuate to medical aid as soon as possible |

**Caution:** the Dapsone/chloroquine-primaquine regimen and the salt-tablet treatment are 1982 practice. Check both against current medical guidance before use.
