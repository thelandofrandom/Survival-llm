# FM 90-5 Jungle Operations

## Purpose

FM 90-5, *Jungle Operations (How to Fight)* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 16 August 1982, 191 pages) — the post-Vietnam rewrite of Army jungle doctrine, and the last of the FM 31-series jungle manuals. It supersedes FM 31-35 of 26 September 1969. Where its predecessors were written for the soldier and junior leader (FM 31-30) and for the staff (FM 31-35), FM 90-5 is written for the fighting unit: "How to Fight" is in the title, and the manual's voice is the after-action voice of the 25th Infantry Division in Vietnam.

**Supersession note — read this first.** For current doctrine, the governing reference is **MCTP 12-10C, *Jungle Operations* (2020)**, already a skill in this vault; where FM 90-5 disagrees with MCTP 12-10C, follow the newer publication. What remains durable in FM 90-5 is its treatment of life in the jungle (hazards, survival, and the psychology of fear), its four requirements for jungle fighting, its catalog of what the jungle does to weapons and fire support, and its appendices — navigation and tracking, jungle obstacles, waterborne operations, fighting positions and shelters, NBC warfare, communications techniques, the individual soldier's combat load, adjustment of fire by sound, and defensive formations. What is dated is its threat chapter (a Cold War enemy picture), its equipment (1982-era weapons, radios, and helicopters), its NBC material, and its medical specifics, which should be checked against current guidance.

**Sibling note — the four jungle manuals in this vault.** FM 31-30 (1965) is the foundation and the training manual: the fullest treatment of heat acclimation and the recommended training program. FM 31-35 (1969) is the mid-war tightening: regional chapters, riverine and stability operations, and training-priority checklists. FM 90-5 (1982) is the fighting manual: it drops the regional survey, keeps the environment and survival chapters, and spends its weight on tactical operations, the threat, and the appendices — the only one of the four with a dedicated chapter on the enemy and a full appendix set for the practitioner. FM 31-36 (TEST) (1968), despite its vault label, is not a jungle manual at all — it is *Night Operations*, and its skill is filed under that title. Read FM 90-5 when you want to know how to fight in the jungle, not just how to live in it.

## 1. The Jungle Environment

**1.1. Slim's warning.** Chapter 1 opens with Field Marshal Slim on the Burma campaign: "To our men… the jungle was a strange, fearsome place; moving and fighting in it were a nightmare. We were too ready to classify jungle as 'impenetrable'… To us it appeared only as an obstacle to movement; to the Japanese it was a welcome means of concealed maneuver and surprise… The Japanese reaped the deserved reward… we paid the penalty." The manual's point is Slim's: the jungle is not an obstacle to be endured, it is ground to be used — and the enemy will use it if you do not.

**1.2. Where jungles are.** Jungles in their various forms are common in tropical areas of the world — mainly Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America. At first the jungle seems very hostile, "but the hostility wanes as troops learn more about the jungle environment." The chapter introduces the environments that later chapters build on: the climate, the vegetation types, the terrain, and the water.

## 2. Life in the Jungle

**2.1. Fear itself is the enemy.** Chapter 2 opens with a 25th Infantry Division report from Vietnam: operating against the North Vietnamese Army along the Cambodian border, U.S. units "found that they had as much difficulty operating in the area as we did. The prisoners we captured were, as a rule, undernourished, emaciated, and sick with malaria. They stated that almost everyone in their unit had malaria, and many had died from it." The lesson the manual draws is twofold: the enemy suffers the jungle as much as you do, and "there is very little to fear from the jungle environment. Fear itself can be an enemy. A man overcome with fear is of little value in any situation."

**2.2. The stories are exaggerated.** Most stories written about out-of-the-way jungle places were written by writers "in search of adventure rather than facts" who "exaggerated or invented many of the thrilling experiences they relate." Most Americans, especially those raised in cities, have lost the knack of taking care of themselves under all conditions — but they would be in just as much danger lost in the mountains of western Pennsylvania as in the jungles of Southeast Asia. The only difference is that a man is less likely to panic when lost in his homeland than when lost abroad.

**2.3. Climate — heat, cold, and rain.** The discomforts of tropical climates are often exaggerated, but the heat is more persistent, and in humid air it seems worse than the same temperature in a dry climate. Strange as it seems, "there may be more suffering from cold in the tropics than from the heat": very low temperatures do not occur, but chilly days and nights are common, and in some jungles in the winter months the nights are cold enough to require a wool blanket or poncho liner for sleeping. Rainfall in many parts of the tropics is much greater than in most temperate zones. Tropical dwellers plan their activities to stay under shelter during the rainy and hotter portions of the day.

**2.4. Insects and malaria.** Malaria-carrying mosquitoes are probably the most harmful of the tropical insects. Precautions against malaria: taking Dapsone and chloroquine-primaquine; using insect repellent; wearing clothing that covers as much of the body as possible. Malaria is more common in populated areas than in uninhabited jungle, and soldiers must be especially cautious at dawn and dusk, when mosquitoes are most active. Insects also cause yellow fever, dengue, and other diseases; dysentery, cholera, and hepatitis come from impure water and food.

**2.5. Leeches and other hazards.** Leeches are common in many jungle areas. Do not pull a leech off forcibly, because part of the leech may remain in the skin — leeches will release themselves if left alone, and leech straps around the legs will prevent them attaching. Sweat bees are tiny, stingless, and annoying but leave when sweating stops. Poisonous vegetation — including tree nettles — can be avoided by learning what to look for and by wearing protective clothing.

**2.6. Heat disorders.** The manual's table gives cause, symptoms, and treatment for each disorder. Heat exhaustion is caused by loss of too much water and salt: dizziness, nausea, headache, cramps, rapid weak pulse, cool wet skin. Treatment: move the victim to a cool, shaded place for rest, loosen the clothing, elevate the feet to improve circulation, and give cool salt water — two salt tablets dissolved in a canteen of water (natural seawater should not be used). Heat cramps are caused by loss of too much salt; the symptom is painful muscle cramps, relieved as soon as salt is replaced; treatment is the same as for heat exhaustion. Heatstroke is caused by a breakdown in the body's heat control mechanism, most likely in those not acclimated or who have recently had bad diarrhea; it can kill if not treated quickly. Symptoms: hot, red, dry skin (the most important sign), no sweating when sweating would be expected, very high temperature (**105 to 110 degrees**), rapid pulse, spots before the eyes, headache, nausea, dizziness, mental confusion, sudden collapse. Treatment: cool the victim immediately — put him in a creek or stream, pour canteens of water over him, fan him, use ice if available; give cool salt water if he is conscious; rub his arms and legs rapidly; evacuate to medical aid as soon as possible. → `references/life-in-the-jungle.md`

## 3. Preparation and Training to Deploy

**3.1. The commander's questions.** When a unit is alerted for jungle training or combat, the commander must first consider: where will the unit train or operate; what are the climatic and terrain conditions of the objective area; how much time does the unit have to prepare; what available training areas resemble the objective area; what type of operations — conventional or counterguerrilla; will the unit take its own equipment; does any equipment require modification (including camouflage painting); what special equipment is required; does the unit have jungle warfare instructors, soldiers with jungle experience, or linguists; what training assistance is available; does higher headquarters have special SOPs for jungle war; are all soldiers physically fit; what information is available about the enemy, local civilians, and allied forces.

**3.2. Training tips.** Units committed to jungle operations may have to fight as soon as they arrive, so commanders must make the best use of preparation time: use garrison time for classroom instruction on jungle subjects, so time in jungle training areas can go to advanced techniques; use local training areas that resemble jungle terrain to introduce jungle techniques; integrate individual training into unit training exercises rather than devoting field time to individual skills; begin physical training in the local training area as early as possible before deployment. The key, the manual says, lies with the chain of command developing a "will to win" as well as a "will to train to win" — mental, physical, and psychological preparation together. Individual training subjects include the jungle environment and acclimation, jungle maintenance and supply techniques, and communications; staff and leader subjects include the same at the planning level. → `references/preparation-and-training.md`

## 4. The Threat in Jungle Areas

**4.1. A chapter none of the siblings has.** Chapter 4 is devoted entirely to the threat — the only one of the four jungle manuals with a dedicated enemy chapter. It treats the enemy as a jungle fighter in his own right, trained to exploit the jungle, and warns against assuming that superior firepower or technology removes the threat: the enemy's advantages are knowledge of the terrain, ability to live off the land, and willingness to fight at close quarters on ground of his choosing. The chapter is a Cold War product — its enemy picture is dated — but its premise is durable: potential jungle enemies train to exploit the jungle, "so must the US Army."

## 5. Tactical Operations — How to Fight

**5.1. The shape of jungle combat.** "Combat in the jungle is characterized by long periods of developing the situation and looking for the enemy; and short periods of violent, and sometimes unexpected, combat." Although jungle operations follow the basic guides of FM 7-8, FM 7-10, FM 7-20, FM 90-4, and FM 100-5, there are special techniques that result from restricted maneuver, slow tempo, close combat, and limited visibility.

**5.2. The four requirements.** To meet jungle conditions, units must have: aggressive intelligence-gathering procedures; disciplined soldiers; solid SOPs proven in training and updated on a continuous basis; and aggressive and tough-minded leadership. Discipline matters because of the extended periods of looking, often fruitlessly, for the enemy; when contact is made, maximum advantage comes only through aggressive and violent action predicated on solid SOPs. Aggressive leadership at the small-unit (squad and platoon) level is the element that ties discipline and training together.

**5.3. What the jungle does to weapons.** The manual's catalog of limitations is the most concrete in any of the four manuals:

- Lack of line-of-sight and clearance may prevent visual contact between units, interlocking fires, and the use of TOW or Dragon missiles.
- Tree limbs may block mortars, flame weapons, 40-mm grenades, and hand grenades.
- Machineguns may not be able to attain grazing fire.
- Adjustment of indirect fire support is difficult due to limited visibility and may have to be accomplished by sound (Appendix I).
- Noise conditions differ: there are large numbers of animals, and their noise — or lack of it — can indicate something out of the norm. Sounds do not carry as far as on the conventional battlefield, so noises are closer than first believed.
- Movement is very difficult: heat, thick vegetation, and rugged terrain tire troops rapidly, especially those carrying heavy weapons or radios.

**5.4. Reconnaissance, offense, defense, retrograde.** Chapter 5 covers reconnaissance, surveillance, and security operations; offensive operations; defensive operations; retrograde; and other combat operations. The emphasis throughout is on developing the situation before contact, violent action on contact, and SOPs that let small units fight without orders when communications fail. → `references/tactical-operations.md`

## 6. Helicopters, Armor, Mechanized Infantry, and Combat Support

**6.1. The supporting fight.** Chapter 6 covers helicopter, armor, mechanized infantry, and combat support operations in the jungle — the arms that FM 31-30 and FM 31-35 treated in a single chapter, now given a chapter of their own. The helicopter is the jungle's answer to the absence of roads, but landing zones are scarce and must be cut or found; armor and mechanized infantry are limited to ground that will carry them and to targets that can be definitely located; combat support must be planned around the jungle's restrictions on movement, observation, and communications rather than in spite of them.

## 7. Combat Service Support and the Appendices

**7.1. Sustaining the fight.** Chapter 7 covers combat service support: supply (including the weight and bulk problem and high fuel consumption rates), maintenance in a climate that rots equipment, and medical service, including the treatment of wounds and the prevention of infection in a hot, wet environment. The jungle's logistics problem is the same one the earlier manuals stated — anticipate well in advance, use waterways and air resupply, and expect rapid deterioration of clothing and equipment — but FM 90-5 states it as a sustainment problem for a force already in contact, not a planning problem for a force preparing to deploy.

**7.2. The appendices — the practitioner's toolkit.** FM 90-5's ten appendices are its most useful durable content: A (references); B (navigation and tracking — compass, map, and pace count, including resection using artillery marking rounds); C (jungle obstacles); D (waterborne operations); E (fighting positions and shelters); F (NBC warfare in jungle areas); G (communication techniques in a jungle environment); H (the individual soldier's combat load); I (adjustment of fire by sound); J (defensive formations). Appendix B's navigation method — azimuth and pace count, with four soldiers keeping a pace count — is the answer to the jungle's core navigation problem: no landmarks, no visibility, and no reliable resection. → `references/combat-support-sustainment-and-appendices.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a jungle question from FM 90-5, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (heatstroke temperatures of 105–110°F, the two-salt-tablet canteen), then the cautions. State the priority order explicitly: discipline and SOPs first, then the fighting technique, then the sustaining detail. Name the environment the advice belongs to — wet tropical jungle — and say which manual it comes from when a sibling manual differs. Where current doctrine (MCTP 12-10C, 2020) differs from FM 90-5, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter or appendix the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **The jungle is ground to use, not an obstacle to endure.** The enemy will use it if you do not.
2. **Fear itself is the enemy.** A man overcome with fear is of little value in any situation — and the enemy suffers the jungle as much as you do.
3. **Four requirements.** Aggressive intelligence-gathering, disciplined soldiers, solid SOPs proven in training and updated continuously, aggressive and tough-minded leadership.
4. **Long search, short violence.** Jungle combat is long periods of developing the situation and short periods of violent, unexpected combat — be ready for the second while enduring the first.
5. **Sounds are closer than they seem.** Jungle foliage shortens the range of sound; animal silence is a warning.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** MCTP 12-10C (2020) governs jungle operations today; FM 90-5 is the 1982 fighting manual, not the authority.
