# Conditioning and Climbing — FM 70-10

## Conditioning and Acclimatization

### The acclimatization period

Training in mountains of low or medium elevation requires no special conditioning — altitude sickness is extremely rare there. But for troops to be trained at altitudes of **8,000 feet or more, a conditioning and acclimatization period of 10 to 14 days is needed**. After a week or two at high altitude, the average soldier is less exhausted, his headache is gone, he sleeps better and his appetite is normal; the essential reason is that the number of red blood cells in his blood has increased, enabling more oxygen to be carried from lungs to tissues. During acclimatization the training program should provide graduated physical exercises, including short marches, with appropriate rest periods.

### Water and salt

In high mountains the air is dry, and perspiration evaporates quickly — misleading a man into thinking he is not perspiring freely, since his clothes are less frequently moist even after strenuous exertion. This loss of fluid plus the loss of salt through perspiration soon leads to acute fatigue, muscle cramps, and heat exhaustion unless replaced. **The proper maintenance of the water and salt balance is imperative in the mountains.**

### Water discipline

The general concept that mountain water is safe for consumption is a fallacy: strict water discipline must be exercised, since the water demand of an individual in the mountains is great and, unless closely controlled, he may drink polluted water. Fluids are lost through respiration, perspiration, and urination, and this loss must be replaced if the soldier is to operate with normal efficiency.

### Psychological adjustment and physical conditioning

- Men from low altitudes may have preconceived notions about the supposedly harmful effects of high altitude, and may construe any trivial complaint as an ill effect of altitude; an active educational campaign prevents this.
- Men from flat terrain often cannot approach steep slopes or cliffs without inner qualms and a sense of insecurity — they must be slowly introduced to them and their confidence progressively developed until they can negotiate a passage with assurance and ease. Fear of height, in varying degrees, is overcome only by familiarity through practice.
- Regardless of previous training, the soldier newly initiated into the mountains finds mountain marching arduous: a new group of muscles is called into play and must be developed and hardened, and a new technique of moving rhythmically must be learned — attained only through daily marches and climbs.
- Alpine territory is relatively germ-free, but low mountain areas are in the same sanitary category as lowlands; the principles of personal hygiene and sanitation that govern lowland operations apply in the mountains as well.

## Military Rock-Climbing

### Mountain walking

Mountain walking is similar to walking in any rough country: the long swinging stride can seldom be used, the feet must be lifted to clear obstructions or swung around them, the lift or rise of each step is kept as low as possible to avoid unnecessary fatigue, the sole of the foot is placed flat to distribute weight and obtain better purchase, the weight is directly over the feet on level walking, a forward lean in descending brings the body more nearly perpendicular to the slope and relieves shock to knees and spine, and the walker uses a steady rhythmic motion. On steep slopes a zig-zag course is less fatiguing than a straight upward climb.

### Balance climbing

Balance climbing is the movement used on steep slopes — a combination of the balanced movement of a tight-rope walker and the unbalanced climbing of a man ascending a tree or ladder. The climber's **weight should be in poise over his feet or just ahead of them** as he moves; his **feet, not his hands, carry his weight**, except on the steepest cliffs; his hands are for balance. Feet will not hold well when the climber leans in toward the rock. He uses **three points of support** (two feet and one hand, for example) whenever possible, prefers handholds waist to shoulder high, relaxes because tensed muscles tire quickly, rests with arms low where circulation is not impaired, uses small intermediate holds rather than stretching between widely separated big holds, avoids the spread-eagle position, climbs sidewise to the rock where he can rather than hugging it, and in descents faces out where the going is easy, sidewise where it is hard, and faces in where it is difficult, using the lowest possible handholds.

### Types of holds

- **Pull holds** — pulled down upon; the easiest holds to use, but also the most likely to break out.
- **Push holds** — pushed down upon; they help keep the arms desirably low and rarely break out, but are more difficult to hold to in case of slip; often used in combination with a pull hold.
- **Friction holds** — depend solely on friction of hands or feet against a smooth surface; difficult because they give a feeling of insecurity, which the climber tends to correct by leaning closer to the rock, thereby only increasing his insecurity; they often serve well as intermediate holds.
- **Cross-pressure holds** — do not exist as useful single holds but must be used in combinations: the pinch hold (pinching a protruding part between the fingers); pressing outward or pulling inward with the arms; the **lie-back** (leaning to one side of an offset crack, hands pulling and feet pushing against the offset side); inverted pull or push holds (underholds); and **chimney climbing** (cross pressure between back and feet, or hands or knees).
- **Footholds:** the service shoe with rubber sole will hold on slabs up to about **45°**; on such steep slopes the body should be kept vertical, using small irregularities to aid friction. Footholds less than **½ inch wide** can be sufficient for intermediate holds, even when they slope out.
- **Shoulder stand** (human ladder): overcomes a holdless lower section to reach easier climbing above.

### Use of holds

A hold need not be large to be good, nor solid, so long as the pressure is in the direction that holds it to the mountain. Experienced climbers use holds so small they can scarcely be seen. The climber must roll over his holds, not skip or jump from one to another. In traverses, the **hop step** is often desirable: a slight upward hop followed by precise foot work, changing feet on a small hold so the climber can move sideways more easily.
