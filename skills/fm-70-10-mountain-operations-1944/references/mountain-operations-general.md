# Mountain Operations — General — FM 70-10

## Terrain and Weather

Terrain and weather are the important factors in mountain operations. Mountainous terrain is usually characterized by one or more of: exaggerated terrain features, heavy woods or jungle, rocky crags and glaciated peaks, compartmentation, routes of communication limited in extent and of poor quality, extreme weather conditions, or high altitudes. Mountain weather is characterized both in summer and winter by inclemency, by large temperature differences between day and night, and by sudden and localized atmospheric disturbances such as violent rain and snowstorms.

## Organization

The organization of the standard infantry division is suitable for operations in low mountain terrain, with only minor modifications — usually the addition of pack transportation and elimination of vehicles and weapons not suitable for the terrain. Complete reorganization and specialized technical training, plus special clothing and equipment, are required for alpine terrain. Tactical units may be organized especially to fit the different terrain compartments.

## Command

**Decentralization of command is characteristic of mountain operations.** All combat troops, equipment, and services in a terrain compartment are placed under a single unified command. Since echelons are often isolated by the terrain, by difficulties of communication, and by weather, subordinate commanders must often assume more responsibility than in other terrain. All commanders must possess, to a high degree, character and initiative. Combat in high mountains demands great detail in planning and preparation.

## Tactics

The same tactical principles govern mountain warfare as any other terrain — but their application differs. In rugged mountains there are definite limits to the employment of large forces: deployment is greatly hindered and restricted, adjacent units often cannot provide mutual support, and rapid employment and shifting of reserves are difficult. **Small units have many occasions for sudden and bold action**, and opportunities to deceive the enemy are many. Small forces of mountain troops can prevent, impede, harass, or canalize the movements of the main enemy force so that when the decisive battle takes place his troops are dissipated and compelled to fight under unfavorable conditions. The tank plays a relatively unimportant role; heavy infantry weapons and artillery are hampered by bulk and weight, dead space in their fields of fire, and difficulties of observation. **It is the infantry, above all, that must bear the brunt of the battle**, and the importance of close combat increases as the efficiency of other methods decreases. The focal points of mountain combat are the heights: gun emplacements and observation posts on commanding heights can dominate the foreground, and advances are made along ridges rather than through the natural avenues of approach.

## Logistics

Time and space factors are never fixed in mountains — they vary constantly with the terrain, altitude, scarcity of roads, and season. A comparatively great amount of time must be allowed for the movement of troops and supplies. **Distance is measured in time rather than space**, and vertical distance between points is often greater than horizontal distance.

## Operations

- **Troop movements and marches:** the long swinging stride can seldom be used; the feet must be lifted to clear obstructions; the lift of each step is kept as low as possible; the sole of the foot is placed flat to distribute weight and obtain better purchase; the weight is directly over the feet on level walking; a forward lean in descending brings the body more nearly perpendicular to the slope; a steady rhythmic motion; on steep slopes a zig-zag course is less fatiguing than a straight upward climb.
- **Security:** compartmentation means adjacent units cannot always see or support each other, and the enemy can approach unseen through dead ground.
- **Bivouacs:** sited with shelter from wind, water, and avalanche danger in mind, as in FM 70-15, adapted to mountain ground.
- **Offensive combat:** fought for the heights, along ridges, by small units acting with initiative.
- **Defensive combat:** exploits compartmentation, dead space, and the difficulty of the attacker's movement, with positions sited to dominate the approaches and with all-round defense against infiltration.

## Employment of the Arms and Services

Chapter 3 covers the infantry, artillery, cavalry, armored and tank-destroyer units, antiaircraft artillery, air forces, engineers, medical corps, and quartermaster corps in mountain operations — each adapted to the same constraints: limited routes, pack transport, dead space, and the dominance of the infantry and of close combat.

## Administration and Communications

- **Administration** (chapter 8): motor transportation, animal transport, supply, and evacuation in mountains — constrained by time measured in distance, vertical distance exceeding horizontal, and routes limited in extent and quality. Supply moves by pack animal and man-carry where vehicles cannot go; evacuation is slow and laborious, and medical installations must be divided to serve isolated compartments.
- **Communications** (chapter 9): terrain compartmentation, weather, and the isolation of echelons make wire, radio, and visual signaling difficult and intermittent — a further reason for the decentralization of command.
