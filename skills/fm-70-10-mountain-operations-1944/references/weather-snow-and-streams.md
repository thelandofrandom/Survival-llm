# Weather, Snow, and Streams — FM 70-10

## Weather

### General

Mountain weather is characterized by inclemency, large day-night temperature differences, and sudden localized disturbances. The manual covers weather determinants, storms and weather types, and weather-prediction methods.

### Causes of bad weather

Most bad weather in mountain regions is caused by: local storms (thunderstorms, with or without showers); traveling storms, accompanied by radical and severe weather changes over a broad area, usually identifiable by their associated clouds; and seasonal moisture-bearing monsoon-type winds, which bring consistently bad weather to some mountain ranges for weeks at a time.

### Cloud types

Local and traveling storms can be differentiated, and their probable occurrence estimated, by cloud recognition:

- **Cirrus** — ice crystals, 4–7 miles high, detached white clouds with fibrous structure or extensive thin veils.
- **Alto stratus** — dark, continuous uniform sheet, 8,000–16,000 feet, covering the sky completely.
- **Cumulus** — great patches of cotton or giant cauliflower heads, 1,500–7,000 feet, flat bases, brilliant white in direct sunlight, dark on the shaded side.
- **Alto cumulus** — similar but smaller, in a uniform layer or deck at 8,000–16,000 feet.
- **Cumulo nimbus** (thunderheads) — overgrown, darkened cumulus yielding or likely to yield precipitation.

### Forecasting

The portable aneroid barometer, thermometer, and hygrometer can be of great assistance in making local weather forecasts; reports from other localities and from the Air Forces' weather service are also of great value. The forecast must get down to the small-unit leaders who are expected to make use of favorable weather for specific missions.

## Snow and Avalanches

### Causes

Avalanches occur when the weight and pressure of the snow overcome the resistance and friction of the under layer, or when the tension in the snow cover is disturbed. The chief influences are the composition and slope of the under layer, the composition of the snow, and the thickness of the snow cover. Boulders, shrubs, trees, and especially woods tend to keep avalanches from forming; bushes hold snow back only when not completely buried, and their elastic branches later favor avalanches by counterpressure. The exact angle at which snow may slide is hard to determine: **there is doubtless avalanche danger on a 24° slope under certain conditions** (smooth ground, grass, hard snow). A **convex surface is much more likely to avalanche than a concave one**.

### Types

Avalanches are classified by the type of snow: new snow (dry, powder snow; wet, new snow) and old snow or wet spring snow.

- **Dry snow avalanches are the most dangerous**: the snow is powdery, fine-grained, loose, fluffy, and dry; it does not pack well; the farther it slides, the more snow it picks up; a very strong gust of wind rushes ahead of the falling avalanche, usually with such power that houses are swept away and woods laid flat. Powdery snow avalanches occur most often in winter after a snowfall and a rapid drop in temperature, and in high mountains must be considered likely during cold weather at every season of the year.
- **Snow-cushion and wind-slab avalanches** belong to the powdery class: wind drifts snowflakes across ridges, and on the leeward side huge masses of loose snow come to rest; wind-slab avalanches occur where wind drifts fine powdery snow onto an old, firm underlayer and hardens it — the most dangerous feature is the **false impression of solidarity** from the hard surface overlying uncompacted snow or air pockets. A wind slab settles suddenly with a dull crack as soon as it is weighted, or even as soon as it is cut by the sharp edge of a ski; the whole slope breaks off on a definite line, more or less straight across the slope, and the snow breaks into cracks and angular blocks that slide, shoved beside and over each other, rushing toward the valley.
- **Crossing with care:** if the ice-ax can be forced through the crust into deep, soft snow below, it is a snow cushion; breaking into small blocks with a definite cleavage across the slope indicates a wind slab. **No slope that appears to be a wind slab should be approached from below; traversing such a slope is even more dangerous.** When the dull sound of the settling snow mass is heard, it is usually too late to withdraw.
- **Wet or damp new-snow avalanches**, which pack well because of their moisture, settle fast, and start sliding because of their weight, occur when wet snow falls and is worked on by sun and wind.

### Cornices and glaciers

Cornices — overhanging masses of snow on ridge crests — are a companion hazard to avalanches and are covered with them in Section III of chapter 7. Glacial regions present their own dangers (crevasses, ice falls, meltwater), and movement over glaciers is covered in Section VI of chapter 7.

## Mountain Stream Crossings

### The hazard

Sudden rains or thaws can change placid streams into roaring torrents, which may have to be crossed without special equipment or the assistance of specialized troops. All mountain soldiers should be trained in crossing mountain streams.

### When and where

- The best time for crossing is **early morning, when the water is low**: as glaciers, snow, or ice melt during the day, rivers rise, reaching maximum height between midafternoon and late evening, depending on distance from the source.
- Reconnoiter possible crossings before the main body arrives. Choose a crossing point, if possible, at the **widest point** on the stream, or where it branches into several smaller streams; cover should be available on the banks; select a point with few, if any, large stones in the river bed — water is turbulent over large stones and flows smoothly over small ones, and large stones increase the difficulty of maintaining footing. Mark the crossing place clearly on both banks.
- **Shoes should be worn to prevent foot injuries, but socks should be removed and kept dry.**

### Fording a shallow stream

A shallow stream with a moderate current can be forded without ropes or logs. Men cross in a **line four abreast without linking arms or affording each other support**, the strongest and most able men at the ends of the line. Cross at an angle, downstream. Feet wide apart, kept flat with the bed of the stream, always set down on the upper side of any obstruction; **drag the legs through the water, not lifted**, so the current will not throw the individual off balance and drag him under.

### Group crossings

For swifter and deeper rivers, groups of men assist each other. In the **ring method**, a squad forms in a ring, locking hands with each other and placing the arms behind their backs, the body bent well forward. In the **chain crossing**, a squad forms in line, locks arms with each other, then crosses their own arms and locks hands in front for added support; the line moves across diagonally downstream. Wherever possible and when experience permits, torrential streams should be forded individually for a speedier crossing — but only experienced soldiers should attempt an individual crossing of a strong, deep current. A balance pole may be used, worked ahead of the individual, always on the upstream side, the weight of the body evenly distributed between pole and feet to maintain two points of support.
