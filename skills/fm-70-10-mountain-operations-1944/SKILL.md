# FM 70-10 Mountain Operations

## Purpose

FM 70-10, *Mountain Operations* (War Department Field Manual, December 1944, 253 pages) — the WWII Army manual for fighting in mountains. It covers general considerations, operations (troop movements and marches, security, bivouacs, offensive and defensive combat), employment of the arms and services, conditioning and acclimatization, military rock-climbing, weather, winter characteristics (snow classification, avalanches and cornices, movement over snow and ice, glacial dangers), administration (motor and animal transport, supply, evacuation), and communications, with a glossary of mountain-climbing terms.

> **HISTORIC WWII-ERA DOCTRINE — READ THIS FIRST.** This is a 1944 manual. Its weapons, transport (mules, pack animals, motor transport of the era), and some of its medical and climbing equipment are dated. Its durable value is in principles and fieldcraft: how mountain terrain and weather shape operations, how to move and climb safely on rock and snow, how avalanches form and how to read them, and how altitude affects men. Where a current manual governs, it governs: the current mountain references are **TC 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (2025)** and the vault's current mountain skills — **ATP 3-90.97, *Mountain Warfare and Cold Weather Operations***, **MCTP 12-10A, *Mountain Warfare***, and **FM 3-97.6, *Mountain Operations***. Use the current manuals as the authority and FM 70-10 as the deep historical field reference.

## 1. General Considerations — Terrain, Command, Tactics, Logistics

**1.1. Terrain and weather.** Terrain and weather are the important factors in mountain operations. Mountainous terrain is usually characterized by one or more of: exaggerated terrain features, heavy woods or jungle, rocky crags and glaciated peaks, compartmentation, routes of communication limited in extent and of poor quality, extreme weather conditions, or high altitudes. Mountain weather is characterized both in summer and winter by inclemency, by large temperature differences between day and night, and by sudden and localized atmospheric disturbances such as violent rain and snowstorms.

**1.2. Organization.** The organization of the standard infantry division is suitable for operations in low mountain terrain, with only minor modifications — usually the addition of pack transportation and elimination of vehicles and weapons not suitable for the terrain. Complete reorganization and specialized technical training, plus special clothing and equipment, are required for alpine terrain. Tactical units may be organized especially to fit the different terrain compartments.

**1.3. Command.** **Decentralization of command is characteristic of mountain operations.** All combat troops, equipment, and services in a terrain compartment are placed under a single unified command. Since echelons are often isolated by the terrain, by difficulties of communication, and by weather, subordinate commanders must often assume more responsibility than in other terrain. All commanders must possess, to a high degree, character and initiative. Combat in high mountains demands great detail in planning and preparation.

**1.4. Tactics.** The same tactical principles govern mountain warfare as any other terrain — but their application differs. In rugged mountains there are definite limits to the employment of large forces: deployment is greatly hindered and restricted, adjacent units often cannot provide mutual support, and rapid employment and shifting of reserves are difficult. **Small units have many occasions for sudden and bold action**, and opportunities to deceive the enemy are many. Small forces of mountain troops can prevent, impede, harass, or canalize the movements of the main enemy force so that when the decisive battle takes place his troops are dissipated and compelled to fight under unfavorable conditions. The tank plays a relatively unimportant role; heavy infantry weapons and artillery are hampered by bulk and weight, dead space in their fields of fire, and difficulties of observation. **It is the infantry, above all, that must bear the brunt of the battle**, and the importance of close combat increases as the efficiency of other methods decreases. The focal points of mountain combat are the heights: gun emplacements and observation posts on commanding heights can dominate the foreground, and advances are made along ridges rather than through the natural avenues of approach.

**1.5. Logistics.** Time and space factors are never fixed in mountains — they vary constantly with the terrain, altitude, scarcity of roads, and season. A comparatively great amount of time must be allowed for the movement of troops and supplies. **Distance is measured in time rather than space**, and vertical distance between points is often greater than horizontal distance. → `references/mountain-operations-general.md`

## 2. Operations — Movement, Security, Bivouacs, Combat

**2.1. Troop movements and marches.** March technique in mountains is covered in paragraph 8 of the manual and in the climbing chapter: the long swinging stride can seldom be used, the feet must be lifted to clear obstructions, the lift of each step kept as low as possible to avoid unnecessary fatigue, the sole of the foot placed flat to distribute weight and obtain better purchase, the weight directly over the feet on level walking, a forward lean in descending to bring the body more nearly perpendicular to the slope, and a steady rhythmic motion. On steep slopes a zig-zag course is less fatiguing than a straight upward climb.

**2.2. Security and bivouacs.** Security in mountains must account for the compartmentation of the terrain: adjacent units cannot always see or support each other, and the enemy can approach unseen through dead ground. Bivouacs are sited with the same considerations as in FM 70-15 — shelter from wind, water, and avalanche danger — adapted to mountain ground.

**2.3. Offensive and defensive combat.** Offensive combat in mountains is fought for the heights, along ridges, by small units acting with initiative; defensive combat exploits the compartmentation, the dead space, and the difficulty of the attacker's movement, with positions sited to dominate the approaches and with all-round defense against infiltration. → `references/mountain-operations-general.md`

## 3. Conditioning and Acclimatization — Altitude Is a Real Enemy

**3.1. The acclimatization period.** Training in mountains of low or medium elevation requires no special conditioning — altitude sickness is extremely rare there. But for troops to be trained at altitudes of **8,000 feet or more, a conditioning and acclimatization period of 10 to 14 days is needed**. After a week or two at high altitude, the average soldier is less exhausted, his headache is gone, he sleeps better and his appetite is normal; the essential reason is that the number of red blood cells in his blood has increased, enabling more oxygen to be carried from lungs to tissues. During acclimatization the training program should provide graduated physical exercises, including short marches, with appropriate rest periods.

**3.2. Water and salt.** In high mountains the air is dry, and perspiration evaporates quickly — misleading a man into thinking he is not perspiring freely, since his clothes are less frequently moist even after strenuous exertion. This loss of fluid plus the loss of salt through perspiration soon leads to acute fatigue, muscle cramps, and heat exhaustion unless replaced. **The proper maintenance of the water and salt balance is imperative in the mountains.** The manual also warns that the general concept that mountain water is safe for consumption is a fallacy: strict water discipline must be exercised, since the water demand of an individual in the mountains is great and, unless closely controlled, he may drink polluted water.

**3.3. Psychological adjustment and physical conditioning.** Men from low altitudes may have preconceived notions about the supposedly harmful effects of high altitude, and may construe any trivial complaint as an ill effect of altitude; an active educational campaign prevents this. Men from flat terrain often cannot approach steep slopes or cliffs without inner qualms and a sense of insecurity — they must be slowly introduced to them and their confidence progressively developed until they can negotiate a passage with assurance and ease. Fear of height, in varying degrees, is overcome only by familiarity through practice. Regardless of previous training, the soldier newly initiated into the mountains finds mountain marching arduous: a new group of muscles is called into play and must be developed and hardened, and a new technique of moving rhythmically must be learned — attained only through daily marches and climbs. Alpine territory is relatively germ-free, but low mountain areas are in the same sanitary category as lowlands. → `references/conditioning-and-climbing.md`

## 4. Military Rock-Climbing — The Core Skill

**4.1. Mountain walking.** Mountain walking is similar to walking in any rough country, with the technique of §2.1: low lift, flat sole, weight over the feet, rhythmic motion, zig-zag on steep slopes.

**4.2. Balance climbing.** Balance climbing is the movement used on steep slopes — a combination of the balanced movement of a tight-rope walker and the unbalanced climbing of a man ascending a tree or ladder. The climber's **weight should be in poise over his feet or just ahead of them** as he moves; his **feet, not his hands, carry his weight**, except on the steepest cliffs; his hands are for balance. Feet will not hold well when the climber leans in toward the rock. He uses **three points of support** (two feet and one hand, for example) whenever possible, prefers handholds waist to shoulder high, relaxes because tensed muscles tire quickly, rests with arms low where circulation is not impaired, uses small intermediate holds rather than stretching between widely separated big holds, avoids the spread-eagle position, climbs sidewise to the rock where he can rather than hugging it, and in descents faces out where the going is easy, sidewise where it is hard, and faces in where it is difficult, using the lowest possible handholds.

**4.3. Types of holds.** **Pull holds** are pulled down upon — the easiest holds to use, but also the most likely to break out. **Push holds** are pushed down upon — they help keep the arms desirably low and rarely break out, but are more difficult to hold to in case of slip; often used in combination with a pull hold. **Friction holds** depend solely on friction of hands or feet against a smooth surface — difficult because they give a feeling of insecurity, which the climber tends to correct by leaning closer to the rock, thereby only increasing his insecurity; they often serve well as intermediate holds. **Cross-pressure holds** do not exist as useful single holds but must be used in combinations: the pinch hold (pinching a protruding part between the fingers); pressing outward or pulling inward with the arms; the **lie-back** (leaning to one side of an offset crack, hands pulling and feet pushing against the offset side); inverted pull or push holds (underholds); and **chimney climbing** (cross pressure between back and feet, or hands or knees). **Footholds:** the service shoe with rubber sole will hold on slabs up to about **45°**; on such steep slopes the body should be kept vertical, using small irregularities to aid friction. Footholds less than **½ inch wide** can be sufficient for intermediate holds, even when they slope out. The **shoulder stand** (human ladder) overcomes a holdless lower section to reach easier climbing above.

**4.4. Use of holds.** A hold need not be large to be good, nor solid, so long as the pressure is in the direction that holds it to the mountain. Experienced climbers use holds so small they can scarcely be seen. The climber must roll over his holds, not skip or jump from one to another. In traverses, the **hop step** is often desirable: a slight upward hop followed by precise foot work, changing feet on a small hold so the climber can move sideways more easily.

**4.5. Movement of men and equipment.** The manual covers movement of trained personnel, mass troop movement, movement of equipment, tactical employment of climbers, mountain stream crossings, special clothing and equipment, the climbing school, supplementary training, and exercises in climbing. → `references/conditioning-and-climbing.md`

## 5. Mountain Stream Crossings

**5.1. The hazard.** Sudden rains or thaws can change placid streams into roaring torrents, which may have to be crossed without special equipment or the assistance of specialized troops. All mountain soldiers should be trained in crossing mountain streams.

**5.2. When and where.** The best time for crossing is **early morning, when the water is low**: as glaciers, snow, or ice melt during the day, rivers rise, reaching maximum height between midafternoon and late evening, depending on distance from the source. Reconnoiter possible crossings before the main body arrives. Choose a crossing point, if possible, at the **widest point** on the stream, or where it branches into several smaller streams; cover should be available on the banks; select a point with few, if any, large stones in the river bed — water is turbulent over large stones and flows smoothly over small ones, and large stones increase the difficulty of maintaining footing. Mark the crossing place clearly on both banks. **Shoes should be worn to prevent foot injuries, but socks should be removed and kept dry.**

**5.3. Fording a shallow stream.** A shallow stream with a moderate current can be forded without ropes or logs. Men cross in a **line four abreast without linking arms or affording each other support**, the strongest and most able men at the ends of the line. Cross at an angle, downstream. Feet wide apart, kept flat with the bed of the stream, always set down on the upper side of any obstruction; **drag the legs through the water, not lifted**, so the current will not throw the individual off balance and drag him under.

**5.4. Group crossings.** For swifter and deeper rivers, groups of men assist each other. In the **ring method**, a squad forms in a ring, locking hands with each other and placing the arms behind their backs, the body bent well forward. In the **chain crossing**, a squad forms in line, locks arms with each other, then crosses their own arms and locks hands in front for added support; the line moves across diagonally downstream. Wherever possible and when experience permits, torrential streams should be forded individually for a speedier crossing — but only experienced soldiers should attempt an individual crossing of a strong, deep current. A balance pole may be used, worked ahead of the individual, always on the upstream side, the weight of the body evenly distributed between pole and feet to maintain two points of support. → `references/weather-snow-and-streams.md`

## 6. Weather — Read It, Forecast It

**6.1. General.** Mountain weather is characterized by inclemency, large day-night temperature differences, and sudden localized disturbances. The manual covers weather determinants, storms and weather types, and weather-prediction methods.

**6.2. Causes of bad weather.** Most bad weather in mountain regions is caused by: local storms (thunderstorms, with or without showers); traveling storms, accompanied by radical and severe weather changes over a broad area, usually identifiable by their associated clouds; and seasonal moisture-bearing monsoon-type winds, which bring consistently bad weather to some mountain ranges for weeks at a time.

**6.3. Cloud types.** Local and traveling storms can be differentiated, and their probable occurrence estimated, by cloud recognition: **cirrus** (ice crystals, 4–7 miles high, detached white clouds with fibrous structure or extensive thin veils); **alto stratus** (dark, continuous uniform sheet, 8,000–16,000 feet, covering the sky completely); **cumulus** (great patches of cotton or giant cauliflower heads, 1,500–7,000 feet, flat bases, brilliant white in direct sunlight, dark on the shaded side); **alto cumulus** (similar but smaller, in a uniform layer or deck at 8,000–16,000 feet); **cumulo nimbus** (thunderheads — overgrown, darkened cumulus yielding or likely to yield precipitation).

**6.4. Forecasting.** The portable aneroid barometer, thermometer, and hygrometer can be of great assistance in making local weather forecasts; reports from other localities and from the Air Forces' weather service are also of great value. The forecast must get down to the small-unit leaders who are expected to make use of favorable weather for specific missions. → `references/weather-snow-and-streams.md`

## 7. Winter Characteristics — Snow, Avalanches, Glaciers

**7.1. Snow classification.** Chapter 7 classifies snow and covers movement over snow and ice, the dangers of glacial regions, and movement over glaciers, with special clothing and equipment for winter mountain operations.

**7.2. Avalanche causes.** Avalanches occur when the weight and pressure of the snow overcome the resistance and friction of the under layer, or when the tension in the snow cover is disturbed. The chief influences are the composition and slope of the under layer, the composition of the snow, and the thickness of the snow cover. Boulders, shrubs, trees, and especially woods tend to keep avalanches from forming; bushes hold snow back only when not completely buried, and their elastic branches later favor avalanches by counterpressure. The exact angle at which snow may slide is hard to determine: **there is doubtless avalanche danger on a 24° slope under certain conditions** (smooth ground, grass, hard snow). A **convex surface is much more likely to avalanche than a concave one**.

**7.3. Types of avalanches.** Avalanches are classified by the type of snow: new snow (dry, powder snow; wet, new snow) and old snow or wet spring snow. **Dry snow avalanches are the most dangerous**: the snow is powdery, fine-grained, loose, fluffy, and dry; it does not pack well; the farther it slides, the more snow it picks up; a very strong gust of wind rushes ahead of the falling avalanche, usually with such power that houses are swept away and woods laid flat. Powdery snow avalanches occur most often in winter after a snowfall and a rapid drop in temperature, and in high mountains must be considered likely during cold weather at every season of the year. **Snow-cushion and wind-slab avalanches** belong to the powdery class: wind drifts snowflakes across ridges, and on the leeward side huge masses of loose snow come to rest; wind-slab avalanches occur where wind drifts fine powdery snow onto an old, firm underlayer and hardens it — the most dangerous feature is the **false impression of solidarity** from the hard surface overlying uncompacted snow or air pockets. A wind slab settles suddenly with a dull crack as soon as it is weighted, or even as soon as it is cut by the sharp edge of a ski; the whole slope breaks off on a definite line, more or less straight across the slope, and the snow breaks into cracks and angular blocks that slide, shoved beside and over each other, rushing toward the valley. **Slopes with snow cushions and wind slabs must be crossed with the greatest care**: if the ice-ax can be forced through the crust into deep, soft snow below, it is a snow cushion; breaking into small blocks with a definite cleavage across the slope indicates a wind slab. **No slope that appears to be a wind slab should be approached from below; traversing such a slope is even more dangerous.** When the dull sound of the settling snow mass is heard, it is usually too late to withdraw. Wet or damp new-snow avalanches, which pack well because of their moisture, settle fast, and start sliding because of their weight, occur when wet snow falls and is worked on by sun and wind.

**7.4. Cornices and glaciers.** Cornices — overhanging masses of snow on ridge crests — are a companion hazard to avalanches and are covered with them in Section III. Glacial regions present their own dangers (crevasses, ice falls, meltwater), and movement over glaciers is covered in Section VI of chapter 7. → `references/weather-snow-and-streams.md`

## 8. Administration and Communications

**8.1. Administration.** Chapter 8 covers motor transportation, animal transport, supply, and evacuation in mountains — all constrained by the same facts as §1.5: time measured in distance, vertical distance exceeding horizontal, and routes limited in extent and quality. Supply moves by pack animal and man-carry where vehicles cannot go; evacuation is slow and laborious, and medical installations must be divided to serve isolated compartments.

**8.2. Communications.** Chapter 9 covers communications in mountains, where terrain compartmentation, weather, and the isolation of echelons make wire, radio, and visual signaling difficult and intermittent — a further reason for the decentralization of command in §1.3. → `references/mountain-operations-general.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **The mountain decides the plan.** Terrain, weather, and altitude set the limits; small units, initiative, and decentralized command work inside them.
2. **Feet carry the weight; hands balance.** Three points of support, small intermediate holds, never the spread-eagle.
3. **Acclimatize or pay for it.** Ten to fourteen days above 8,000 feet; water and salt balance is imperative.
4. **Read the snow before you trust it.** Convex slopes, wind slabs, and the dull crack of settling snow are warnings, not curiosities.
5. **Cross streams in the morning, at the widest point, downstream at an angle.** Never lift the legs in a current.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** TC 3-97.61 (2025), ATP 3-90.97, and MCTP 12-10A govern modern mountain operations; FM 70-10 is the deep historical field reference.
