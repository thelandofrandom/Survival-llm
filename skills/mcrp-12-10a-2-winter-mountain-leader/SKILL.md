# Winter Mountain Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare

## Purpose

MCRP 12-10A.2, *Winter Mountain Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare* (U.S. Marine Corps, 11 May 2026, 116 pages), is the reference for winter mountain leaders operating in snow-covered terrain or a cold-weather environment. It covers tactics, techniques, and procedures for snowshoe and ski mobility, movement techniques, firing positions, avalanche hazard assessment and mitigation, ice reconnaissance and crossing frozen waterways, snow tracking and deception, ski-borne patrolling, and over-the-snow vehicle employment. It is used alongside MCTP 12-10A (*Mountain Warfare*), MCTP 12-10E (*Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations*), MCRP 12-10A.1 (*Small-Unit Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare*), and MCRP 12-10A.3 (*Mountain Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare Operations*). It supersedes the 2013 *Mountain Leader's Guide to Winter Operations*.

The winter mountain leader is the unit's subject-matter expert on over-the-snow mobility and avalanche hazard assessment, an advisor to the commander, and the person who conducts pre-environmental and mobility training for the unit. The governing idea of the whole manual: entire units can be killed in a single avalanche, so route selection, preparation, and decision making — not boldness — are what keep a unit alive in winter mountains. It is possible to travel at times of high snow instability by choosing safe routes, and it is equally possible to be caught in an avalanche when the snow is stable because of poor route selection and poor stability evaluation.

## 1. Operating in Avalanche-Prone Terrain

**The decision framework.** Avalanche work starts before anyone steps on snow: data collection and analysis from local maps, slope angles and aspects, seasonal patterns, and weather history, then field observation on the ground. The leader's job is to combine the two — the forecast and the snowpack in front of them — into a go/no-go route decision, and to keep re-evaluating as conditions change through the day.

**Slope angle is the first filter.** Avalanche-prone slopes run from **25 to 60 degrees**, and the most common avalanche slope is approximately **38 degrees**. Below 25 degrees avalanches are rare; above 60 degrees snow tends to slough off before it can build into a slab. Measure slope angle with a compass fitted with an inclinometer or a clinometer: the instrument reads the angle from horizontal, and the leader subtracts the reading from 90 degrees — a dial reading of 60 degrees means a slope angle of 30 degrees. A field-expedient method uses a ski pole and protractor: place the protractor on the pole with 0 degrees down and 90 degrees facing down the slope. Aspect matters as much as angle: the direction a slope faces determines sun and wind loading, which determines where weak layers form and persist.

**Avalanche triggers.** A trigger is any disturbance that initiates a slide — a skier, a snowmobile, a falling cornice, a rock, a rifle shot's vibration, or simply new snow loading a weak layer past its limit. Triggers are what turn a loaded, unstable snowpack into a moving avalanche, which is why the leader's control measures focus on where people travel and how much load they put on a slope at once: one at a time across suspect slopes, spaced out, with the rest of the unit in a safe zone.

**Signs of instability and stability.** Learn to read both lists in the field:
- *Instability:* recent avalanches on similar slopes and aspects; "whumpfing" (a collapsing weak layer that makes a hollow sound and sometimes drops the snow surface); shooting cracks running out from skis or boots; heavy snowfall or rapid warming; wind loading that builds deep slabs on lee slopes; a hollow, drum-like sound underfoot.
- *Stability:* no recent avalanche activity; a snowpack that has settled and bonded over days; consistent, rounded snow grains; no cracking or collapsing; cold temperatures holding the pack together.
No single sign decides anything. The leader weighs the whole set, and when the signs conflict, the conservative reading wins.

**Field-expedient stability tests.** Three tests dominate, all done on a slope with the same angle and aspect as the slope to be crossed, and all interpreted with the manual's stability levels:
- *Rutschblock test:* cut a block roughly 2 meters wide across the fall line and 1.5 meters up the slope, isolate it, then load it progressively — standing on it, flexing the knees, jumping once on skis, repeated jumps on skis, repeated jumps with skis off. The earlier it fails, the more unstable the snow. Failure while excavating or approaching the block means extremely unstable; failure while standing on it means extremely unstable; failure on knee-flex means unstable; failure on one jump means unstable; failure only after repeated jumps means relatively stable; no failure after repeated jumps with skis off means stable.
- *Shovel- and ski-shear test:* isolate a column about 12 inches (30.5 cm) wide and deep in the uphill pit wall, keep it vertical and smooth, insert a shovel or ski behind it, and push steadily downward, watching for weak layers separating. Quick, minimal digging, and it pinpoints *where* in the snowpack the weak layer sits.
- *Shred-block test:* like the rutschblock but using snowshoes — a block about 5.75 feet (1.75 m) wide across the fall line, 5 feet (1.5 m) on each side up the fall line, and up to 5 feet (1.5 m) deep, deeper than the suspected failure layer.
- *Compression test:* a column is dynamically loaded with the standard loading steps; the number of taps to failure and the character of the fracture (clean, sudden collapse vs. progressive) tell the leader how reactive the weak layer is.

**Avalanche checklists.** Appendix A is the avalanche decision-making checklist and Appendix B the avalanche data-observation checklist — use them as written, every time, rather than from memory. The data-observation checklist is the record the leader keeps so the next leader, and the commander, can see what was observed, where, and when.

**Avalanche transceivers.** Every member of a party in avalanche terrain wears a transceiver, switched to transmit, worn under the outer layer against the body, and checked with an operating test before movement. Know the transceiver's nomenclature and functions — transmit/receive switch, range dial, volume, and battery indicator — and its specified operating range, inside which it can effectively receive. Before entering avalanche terrain the whole party does a transceiver check: one person in transmit, the others confirming reception at a set distance.

**The bracketing search.** When someone is buried, speed is everything, and the bracketing search is the fast, accurate method:
1. Record the last point where the victim was seen. If further avalanche danger exists, post an avalanche guard, ready to switch back to transmit.
2. Set all searchers' transceivers to receive, so no other transmissions mislead the search.
3. Turn the range dial to its highest setting and the volume up.
4. Deploy searchers at a maximum of **20 meters apart** in the last-seen area and move down the slope.
5. Slowly rotate the transceiver **120 degrees** side to side to find the direction of strongest signal.
6. Mark where the first signal was received and proceed in a straight line; the signal will get stronger, then weaker. Mark the weakest point.
7. Find the midpoint between the two weak points, mark it, and turn the range dial and volume down so the signal is low.
8. Proceed 90 degrees to the first line: in one direction the signal weakens, in the other it strengthens — move toward the stronger signal, narrowing down until the signal is strongest directly below, then probe and dig.

Practice this until it is mechanical. A buried victim's survival clock is measured in minutes, and a practiced bracketing search is the difference between a rescue and a recovery.

## 2. Tracking in Snow-Covered Terrain

Snow is the tracker's best and worst medium: it preserves sign beautifully and betrays the tracker just as clearly.

**Determining a track's age.** Every area's climate ages tracks differently, so the leader learns the local aging pattern rather than memorizing a universal table. The factors that deteriorate a track: temperature (freeze-thaw cycles round and soften edges), wind (fills and erases), new snowfall (buries), sun (melts and glazes the edges), and time itself. A fresh track has sharp edges, crisp detail, and undisturbed snow thrown clear; an old track is rounded, glazed, partially filled, and its edges are soft. Compare tracks of known age — your own unit's tracks from hours earlier — against the unknown track to calibrate the estimate. Age determines everything downstream: how far ahead the enemy is, whether pursuit is worthwhile, and whether the track is a fresh trail or a days-old decoy.

**Tracking.** The tracking team reads the track for size, direction, and proficiency of the enemy unit — numbers from the width of the trail and the number of distinct footprints, direction from the orientation of the prints and the snow kicked forward, proficiency from spacing, discipline, and whether the trail is straight and purposeful or wandering. Track in teams: a point tracker reads the sign at the front while the rest provide security and carry the load. Keep the team small, quiet, and spread so one burst of fire does not catch everyone.

**Sign cutting.** When the trail is lost or the area is wide, two tracking teams crisscross in arcs to intersect the track — sign cutting. Teams leapfrog each other, each cutting a new arc further along the expected line of movement, covering far more ground than a single team following one trail. It is the standard technique for finding a track across open snow where the enemy's line of travel is uncertain.

**Lost spoor procedures.** When a team operating alone loses the track, stop, mark the last definite sign, and work outward from it in a systematic search — widening arcs, back along the trail to re-read the last confirmed sign, and forward along the most likely line of movement. Do not wander: the last known point is the anchor, and every search leg returns to it.

**Antitracking techniques.** The manual lists the techniques a unit uses to defeat a tracker, and more than one can be used at once: walking in streams or on hard ground that takes no print, brushing out tracks, walking in single file to reduce the number of trails, backtracking, using false trails and decoy routes, and splitting the party. The counter to antitracking is the same discipline as tracking itself — slow down, read the ground wider than the trail, and look for the sign the enemy could not hide: broken twigs, disturbed snow on branches, a scuff where a boot slipped.

## 3. Ice Reconnaissance

Crossing frozen water is a reconnaissance problem first and an engineering problem second. Never assume ice is safe because it looks solid.

**Ice classification.** Three general types:
- *Saltwater (sea) ice* is weaker than freshwater ice. It begins as crystals in the salt-water layer affected by convection, giving it an oily or greasy appearance. Young sea ice must be about **1.66 times thicker** than old sea ice to carry the same load. Pack ice is perennial in the Arctic basin, and telescoping and piling of floes can build thicknesses of many feet.
- *Freshwater ice* forms on lakes and rivers three to five weeks after daily temperatures drop below freezing, and is stronger and has fewer defects than sea ice — the standard against which the manual's load tables are written.
- *Land ice* (glaciers, ice caps) is a different problem again, covered in MCRP 12-10A.3.

**Ice formation and its traps.** Know the dangerous forms: blue ice (clear, dense, strong when thick, but deceptive where thin); chandelier ice (crystals hanging below the surface, weak and unreliable); rotten ice (honeycombed by melt, looks thick, holds nothing); unsupported ice (water level has dropped beneath it, leaving a void — it cannot support heavy loads even at full thickness). Formation rates depend on temperature, wind, snow cover (insulating snow slows freezing), and current. Moving water — rivers, inlets, outlets, springs — freezes later and thinner than still water; always recon those spots separately.

**Determining ice thickness and load capacity.** Measure thickness by drilling or cutting test holes at intervals along the route, and recon the whole route, not one spot. The manual's tables give the working numbers for solid, clear, freshwater ice:
- *Individuals:* a person on snowshoes or skis needs **1.5 inches (4 cm)** for one-time use and **2 inches (5 cm)** for repeated use; a person on foot needs **3 inches (8 cm)** one-time and **4 inches (11 cm)** repeated. Keep **17 feet (5 m)** between individuals.
- *Vehicles:* required thickness follows the rule of thumb that thickness in inches is approximately **4 times the square root of the vehicle weight in tons** — 1 ton needs 4 inches, 2 tons 6 inches, 4 tons 8 inches, 10 tons 13 inches, 20 tons 18 inches, 40 tons 26 inches, 100 tons 40 inches. Spacing between vehicles is about **100 times the ice thickness** (a 10-inch crossing means roughly 82 feet between vehicles).
- *The armored-vehicle rule:* **16 inches of water-supported ice supports 16 tons, and each additional inch supports one additional ton.** This rule does not apply below 16 inches — 3 inches of ice does not support three tons.
- *Temperature correction:* in temperatures above **14°F, add 25 percent** to all required ice thicknesses.
- *Gold's Formula* for aircraft and landing zones: H = √(W/A), or W = A × H², where H is ice thickness in inches, W is gross weight in pounds, and A is the flexural strength of the ice in psi. Average flexural strength is about 150 psi; lower A-values are used as safety factors for higher risk tolerance, and Table 3-3 tabulates required thicknesses from very low risk (A = 50 psi) to extreme risk (A = 250 psi) across aircraft weights.

**Crossing-site selection and movement.** Choose the crossing where the ice is thickest and most uniform, the banks give easy entry and exit, and the route is as short and straight as practical. Cross in single file at the prescribed spacing, at steady speed — never stop on the ice, never accelerate hard, and keep the route marked. For vehicles, cross one at a time at the table spacing. If the water level has dropped under the ice, do not attempt heavy crossings at all.

**Ice breaching for countermobility.** The manual also covers the reverse problem: breaching ice to deny the enemy a crossing, organizing an ice-breaching party, maintaining the water obstacle once opened, and setting ice ambushes where the enemy's crossing route is predictable. An open water obstacle in winter is as effective as a minefield and far harder to bypass.

## 4. Military Ski Equipment

**Nomenclature.** Know the parts of a military ski — tip, shovel, waist, tail, edges, base, camber, and the climbing-skin attachment points — because every care and maintenance instruction refers to them by name. The military ski is wider and heavier than a civilian touring ski, built to carry a loaded Marine over unbroken snow.

**Bindings.** Military bindings must accept the issue boot, allow a free heel for touring and climbing, and lock down for descents. Check them before every movement: heel and toe pieces secure, release function working, no ice packed into the mechanism, and no cracked or bent parts. Ice in a binding is the most common failure — clear it with a ski pole tip or a gloved hand, never by forcing the mechanism.

**Poles.** Military ski poles are adjustable and long enough to plant firmly in deep snow; the baskets are wide so the pole does not sink. Check straps, locks, and baskets before movement, and carry a spare basket — a lost basket turns a pole into a spike that punches through the snow instead of supporting weight.

**Care, maintenance, and serviceability.** Inspect skis before and after every use: base for gouges and delamination, edges for burrs and cracks, bindings for function, skins for glue and adhesion. Clean snow and ice off after movement, dry the skis before storage, and store them base-to-base or flat, away from heat. A ski that is not maintained becomes a liability on the first descent.

**Waxing.** Wax controls glide and grip, and the wrong wax is worse than no wax. The manual covers wax types and their effects on snow at different temperatures, and the application sequence: clean the base, warm the wax, apply evenly, let it cool, scrape, and brush. In the field, carry a basic wax kit with a range of temperatures and a scraper; a ski that will not glide exhausts the skier, and a ski that will not grip cannot climb.

**Climbing skins.** Skins are the engine of uphill movement — the directional nap grips the snow going up and slides going down. Military skis use scales for traction on slopes up to about 15 degrees; skins extend that to modest grades of 10 to 15 degrees and beyond, aiding novice skiers and loaded movement. Keep skins dry, keep the glue clean, and never fold them glue-to-glue without a protective sheet.

## 5. Over-the-Snow Movement

**Skiing.** The manual teaches ski techniques as a system: the basic positions, turning, stopping, traversing, and falling safely. Mitigating falls and injuries is a skill in itself — fall uphill and to the side, keep the hands up, release the poles, and never fight a fall with a locked knee. A broken leg in deep snow far from a road is a casualty evacuation, not an inconvenience.

**Skijoring.** Towing skiers behind a vehicle — over-the-snow vehicle or other prime mover — lets a unit move fast over flat or rolling snow. The manual covers the tow rope, the skier's position behind the vehicle, spacing, hand signals, and the critical safety rule: the skier must be able to release instantly, and the driver must be able to see the skier. Skijoring is fast but unforgiving of inattention; a fallen skier dragged behind a vehicle is an emergency.

**Over-the-snow vehicles.** Appendix E covers employment of over-the-snow vehicles (snowmobiles and tracked carriers): capabilities, limitations, maintenance in extreme cold, and tactical employment — reconnaissance, resupply, and casualty evacuation. Vehicles extend the unit's reach but create their own signature (tracks and noise), need fuel and maintenance, and can be immobilized by deep powder or overflow ice. Plan for the vehicle to fail and the unit to continue on foot.

## 6. Over-the-Snow Casualty Evacuation

Casualty evacuation in snow is a planning problem, not an improvisation. A CASEVAC in deep snow typically requires **4 to 12 Marines** — pullers, a security element, and a medic — which means the evacuation strips combat power from the unit and must be planned into the mission, with routes, rally points, and a landing zone reconnoitered in advance. The manual covers over-the-snow litters and improvised drags, the use of skis and snowshoes as a litter platform, and the protection of the casualty from cold during a long drag: insulation from the snow beneath, wind protection, and the hypothermia wrap. Move the casualty steadily and smoothly — every jolt in deep snow is both painful and dangerous — and rotate the pullers on a schedule so the team does not exhaust itself halfway to the pickup point.

## 7. Weapons Carries, Firing, and Employment in Deep Snow

Deep snow changes how a weapon is carried, fired, and maintained. Snow gets into actions, freezes bolts, fogs optics, and swallows a dropped magazine.

**Rifle carries.** Two standard carries are taught: Carry 1 and Carry 2, each suited to different movement conditions — one for hands-free movement on skis, the other for rapid employment. The principle is the same in both: the weapon is protected from snow, accessible enough to bring into action quickly, and slung so it does not interfere with the ski poles or the skier's balance.

**Ski crawls.** When the unit must advance under fire on skis, the manual teaches four methods: working forward on skis, advancing by sliding, advancing in a crouching form, and advancing with trailing skis. Each trades speed for cover; the leader picks the method by the terrain and the threat, and the unit practices them until the movement is automatic — because a unit that cannot move under fire on skis is fixed in place by the snow.

**Firing positions and employment.** Deep snow will not support a stable firing position without preparation — pack the snow, build a platform, or fire from the skis. Cold degrades lubricants and batteries, fogs optics when a warm weapon meets cold air, and changes ballistics slightly at long range. Keep the weapon as dry as possible, keep the action clear of snow, and keep spare batteries warm against the body.

## 8. Snow Shelters

A snow shelter is a life-support system: it trades the wind and the cold outside for still air and body heat inside. The manual's rule is to build the shelter *before* you need it, in daylight, with dry hands.

**Basic characteristics.** A good snow shelter is out of the wind, has a low entrance (cold air sinks, warm air stays up), a ventilation hole that can never be allowed to block, and a sleeping platform raised above the entrance level so the coldest air pools at the door, not under the sleeper. The manual's comparison table gives the trade-offs: a snow cave needs 6 feet of compacted snow and shelters many for days; a trench needs less snow and shelters fewer for shorter; a tree-pit needs almost no snow preparation and shelters one to three for a night.

**Snow cave.** Shelters **1 to 16 Marines** for extended durations. It needs a base of **6 feet of well-compacted snow**. Dig the entrance low, dig up into the sleeping chamber, and cut a ventilation hole through the roof at a **45-degree angle** above the entrance, leaving a ski pole or branch in the hole to mark it and to clear it if it blocks. Wear avalanche transceivers inside a snow cave in case of collapse. A single candle raises the inside temperature about **2°F** and, more importantly, serves as a warning: if the candle goes out, the ventilation is blocked and oxygen deprivation is a risk — clear the vent immediately.

**Tree-pit snow shelter.** For **one to three Marines**, short duration. Dig down around the trunk of a large conifer where the snow is naturally shallow, using the tree's branches as a roof. Fast to build, warm enough for a night, but limited in space and vulnerable to snow falling from the branches.

**Snow trench.** A quick, simple shelter: dig a trench, roof it with skis or branches and a tarp or snow blocks, and sleep in it. Faster than a cave, colder than a cave, and the right answer when time or snow depth is short.

**Snow wall, snow coffin, fallen-tree bivouac.** The wall is a windbreak for a short halt, not a shelter. The snow coffin is a body-sized trench roofed over — the minimum viable shelter for one person in an emergency. The fallen-tree bivouac uses a downed tree's trunk and branches as structure and roof, filled in with snow and boughs.

**The shelter decision.** Pick by the variables the manual keeps returning to: how many people, how long, how much snow, how much time, and how much energy the party has left. A tired party that tries to build a snow cave in the dark with wet gloves is worse off than a party that digs a trench in twenty minutes and gets inside. The shelter that gets built is always better than the shelter that was planned.

## Output Contract

When asked about winter mountain operations, answer from this doctrine: lead with the decision (go/no-go, which route, which shelter, which test), give the numbers that support it (slope angle, ice thickness, spacing, test results), and name the checklists and appendices that govern it. Quote the manual's load tables and test interpretations exactly — a wrong ice thickness or a misread stability test is a fatal error, not a rounding error. Distinguish what the manual states as doctrine from what is field judgment: the tables are doctrine; the choice between two safe routes is judgment.

## Operating Rules

1. **Avalanche terrain is a decision problem, not a courage problem.** Route selection and stability evaluation keep units alive; boldness does not.
2. **Measure the slope, test the snow, then decide.** Never cross a suspect slope on appearance alone.
3. **Everyone wears a transceiver, and everyone practices the bracketing search.** The search is a perishable skill.
4. **Never trust ice you have not measured.** Drill it, table it, space the crossing — and add 25 percent above 14°F.
5. **Build the shelter before you need it.** In daylight, with dry hands, while the party still has energy.
6. **A casualty evacuation in snow is a mission, not an afterthought.** Plan the pullers, the route, and the pickup before the casualty happens.
7. **The shelter that gets built beats the shelter that was planned.** Pick the shelter the party can actually finish.
