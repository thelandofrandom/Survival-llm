# Avalanche Hazard and Transceiver Search

Distilled from MCRP 12-10A.2, Chapter 1 and Appendices A–B (U.S. Marine Corps, May 2026).

## Slope Angle

- Avalanche-prone slopes: **25–60 degrees**. Most common avalanche slope: **~38 degrees**.
- Measure with a compass inclinometer or clinometer: subtract the dial reading from 90° (a 60° reading = 30° slope).
- Field-expedient: ski pole + protractor, 0° down, 90° facing down the slope.
- Aspect matters as much as angle: sun and wind loading decide where weak layers form and persist.

## Avalanche Triggers

A trigger is any disturbance that initiates a slide: a skier, snowmobile, falling cornice, rockfall, vibration, or new snow loading a weak layer past its limit. Control measures: cross suspect slopes one at a time, spaced out, with the rest of the unit in a safe zone.

## Signs of Instability

- Recent avalanches on similar slopes/aspects
- "Whumpfing" — a collapsing weak layer, hollow sound, snow surface dropping
- Shooting cracks running out from skis or boots
- Heavy snowfall or rapid warming
- Wind loading building deep slabs on lee slopes
- Hollow, drum-like sound underfoot

## Signs of Stability

- No recent avalanche activity
- Snowpack settled and bonded over days
- Consistent, rounded snow grains
- No cracking or collapsing
- Cold temperatures holding the pack together

No single sign decides. Weigh the whole set; when signs conflict, take the conservative reading.

## Field-Expedient Stability Tests

All tests are done on a slope with the **same angle and aspect** as the slope to be crossed.

### Rutschblock test

Cut a block ~2 m wide across the fall line, 1.5 m up the slope, isolate it, and load progressively. Interpretation (Table 1-1):

| Reaction | Result |
|---|---|
| Fails while excavating test site | Extremely unstable |
| Fails while approaching test site | Extremely unstable |
| Fails while standing on shear block | Extremely unstable |
| Fails while flexing knees | Unstable |
| Fails with one jump while wearing skis | Unstable |
| Fails after repeated jumps with skis on | Relatively stable |
| Doesn't fail after repeated jumps with skis off | Stable |

### Shovel- and ski-shear test

Isolate a column ~12 in (30.5 cm) wide and deep in the uphill pit wall, vertical and smooth. Insert a shovel or ski behind it and push steadily downward; watch for weak layers separating. Quick, minimal digging; pinpoints where the weak layer sits in the pack.

### Shred-block test

Like the rutschblock but using snowshoes. Block ~5.75 ft (1.75 m) wide across the fall line, 5 ft (1.5 m) on each side up the fall line, up to 5 ft (1.5 m) deep — deeper than the suspected failure layer.

### Compression test

A column is dynamically loaded with standard loading steps. The number of taps to failure and the fracture character (clean sudden collapse vs. progressive) show how reactive the weak layer is.

## Avalanche Transceivers

- Worn under the outer layer, against the body, switched to **transmit**.
- Know the nomenclature: transmit/receive switch, range dial, volume, battery indicator, and the specified operating range.
- Whole-party operating test before entering avalanche terrain.
- Inside a snow cave, wear transceivers in case of collapse.

## Bracketing Search Method

1. Record the last point where the victim was seen. If further avalanche danger exists, post an avalanche guard, ready to switch back to transmit.
2. Set all searchers' transceivers to **receive**.
3. Range dial to highest setting, volume up.
4. Searchers at a maximum of **20 m apart** in the last-seen area, moving down the slope.
5. Rotate the transceiver **120°** side to side to find the direction of strongest signal.
6. Mark the first signal point; proceed in a straight line. Signal strengthens, then weakens — mark the weakest point.
7. Find the midpoint between the two weak points, mark it, turn range dial and volume down so the signal is low.
8. Proceed **90°** to the first line: one direction weakens, the other strengthens — move toward the stronger signal, narrowing until the signal is strongest directly below, then probe and dig.
