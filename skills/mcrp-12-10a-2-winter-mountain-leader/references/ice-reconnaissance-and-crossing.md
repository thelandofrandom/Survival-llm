# Ice Reconnaissance and Frozen-Waterway Crossing

Distilled from MCRP 12-10A.2, Chapter 3 (U.S. Marine Corps, May 2026).

## Ice Classification

- **Saltwater (sea) ice** — weaker than freshwater ice. Begins as crystals in the salt-water layer affected by convection; oily or greasy appearance. Young sea ice must be **~1.66× thicker** than old sea ice to carry the same load. Pack ice is perennial in the Arctic basin; telescoping and piling of floes can build great thicknesses.
- **Freshwater ice** — forms on lakes and rivers 3–5 weeks after daily temperatures drop below freezing. Stronger, fewer defects than sea ice. The load tables below are written for solid, clear freshwater ice.
- **Land ice** — glaciers and ice caps; a separate problem (see MCRP 12-10A.3).

## Dangerous Ice Forms

- **Blue ice** — clear, dense, strong when thick, deceptive where thin.
- **Chandelier ice** — crystals hanging below the surface; weak and unreliable.
- **Rotten ice** — honeycombed by melt; looks thick, holds nothing.
- **Unsupported ice** — water level has dropped beneath it, leaving a void; cannot support heavy loads even at full thickness.

Formation rates depend on temperature, wind, snow cover (insulating snow slows freezing), and current. Moving water — rivers, inlets, outlets, springs — freezes later and thinner than still water; recon those spots separately.

- **Armored-vehicle rule:** 16 in of water-supported ice supports 16 tons; each additional inch supports one additional ton. **Does not apply below 16 in** — 3 in of ice does not support three tons.
- **Temperature correction:** above **14°F, add 25%** to all required thicknesses.
- When moving two loads on the ice, use the spacing for the larger load.

## Gold's Formula (Landing Zones)

H = √(W/A), or W = A × H², where H = ice thickness in inches, W = gross weight in pounds, A = flexural strength in psi.

- Average flexural strength of ice ≈ **150 psi** (A = 150 → safety factor 1, very low risk in the manual's framing).
- Table 3-3 tabulates required thicknesses from very low risk (A = 50 psi) to extreme risk (A = 250 psi) across aircraft weights. Example: a 20,000-lb aircraft needs 20.00 in at very low risk, 14.14 in at tolerable risk (A = 100), and 8.94 in at extreme risk (A = 250).

## Determining Ice Thickness

Drill or cut test holes at intervals along the whole route — never one spot. Recon the route, not a point.

## Crossing-Site Selection and Movement

- Choose where ice is thickest and most uniform, banks give easy entry and exit, and the route is short and straight.
- Cross single file at prescribed spacing, steady speed. Never stop on the ice; never accelerate hard; keep the route marked.
- Vehicles cross one at a time at table spacing.
- If the water level has dropped under the ice, do not attempt heavy crossings.

## Ice Breaching for Countermobility

- Organize an ice-breaching party, open the water obstacle, maintain it, and set ice ambushes where the enemy's crossing route is predictable.
- An open water obstacle in winter is as effective as a minefield and far harder to bypass.
