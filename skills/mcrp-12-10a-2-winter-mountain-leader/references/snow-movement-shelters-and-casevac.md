# Snow Movement, Shelters, Tracking, and CASEVAC

Distilled from MCRP 12-10A.2, Chapters 2, 4–8 and Appendices C–F (U.S. Marine Corps, May 2026).

## Ski Equipment

- **Parts:** tip, shovel, waist, tail, edges, base, camber, climbing-skin attachment points.
- **Bindings:** must accept the issue boot, free heel for touring/climbing, lock down for descents. Check before every movement: heel/toe secure, release working, no ice packed in the mechanism, no cracked or bent parts. Ice in a binding is the most common failure — clear it with a ski-pole tip or gloved hand, never by forcing the mechanism.
- **Poles:** adjustable, long enough to plant in deep snow; wide baskets so the pole does not sink. Check straps, locks, baskets; carry a spare basket.
- **Care:** inspect base (gouges, delamination), edges (burrs, cracks), bindings, skins (glue, adhesion) before and after every use. Clean snow and ice off, dry before storage, store base-to-base or flat, away from heat.
- **Waxing:** wax controls glide and grip; wrong wax is worse than no wax. Clean base → warm wax → apply evenly → cool → scrape → brush. Carry a basic wax kit covering a range of temperatures plus a scraper.
- **Climbing skins:** directional nap grips going up, slides going down. Ski scales give traction on slopes up to ~15°; skins extend uphill capability on modest grades (10–15°) and beyond. Keep skins dry, glue clean, never fold glue-to-glue without a protective sheet.

## Over-the-Snow Movement

- **Skiing:** basic positions, turning, stopping, traversing, falling safely. Fall uphill and to the side, hands up, release poles, never fight a fall with a locked knee.
- **Skijoring:** towing skiers behind a vehicle for fast movement over flat or rolling snow. Tow rope, skier's position, spacing, hand signals. The skier must be able to release instantly and the driver must be able to see the skier.
- **Over-the-snow vehicles (Appendix E):** snowmobiles and tracked carriers — reconnaissance, resupply, CASEVAC. They extend reach but create tracks and noise, need fuel and maintenance, and can be immobilized by deep powder or overflow ice. Plan for the vehicle to fail and the unit to continue on foot.

## Tracking in Snow (Chapter 2)

- **Track age:** temperature (freeze-thaw rounds edges), wind (fills/erases), new snowfall (buries), sun (melts/glazes edges), time. Fresh tracks: sharp edges, crisp detail. Old tracks: rounded, glazed, partially filled. Calibrate against your own unit's tracks of known age.
- **Reading a track:** size (trail width, distinct footprints), direction (print orientation, snow kicked forward), proficiency (spacing, discipline, straight vs. wandering).
- **Sign cutting:** two teams crisscross in arcs to intersect a track, leapfrogging to cover ground when the line of travel is uncertain.
- **Lost spoor:** stop, mark the last definite sign, work outward in widening arcs, back along the trail to re-read the last confirmed sign, forward along the most likely line. The last known point is the anchor.
- **Antitracking:** walking in streams or on hard ground, brushing out tracks, single file, backtracking, false trails and decoys, splitting the party. Counter: slow down, read the ground wider than the trail, look for sign that could not be hidden.

## Weapons in Deep Snow (Chapter 7)

- **Two standard rifle carries** (Carry 1 and Carry 2): one for hands-free movement on skis, one for rapid employment. Protect the weapon from snow, keep it accessible, keep it clear of poles and balance.
- **Ski crawls** (advance under fire on skis): working forward on skis; advancing by sliding; advancing in a crouching form; advancing with trailing skis. Trade speed for cover by terrain and threat.
- **Firing positions:** deep snow will not support a stable position without preparation — pack the snow, build a platform, or fire from the skis. Keep the weapon dry, the action clear of snow, spare batteries warm against the body; cold degrades lubricants and batteries and fogs optics.

## Snow Shelters (Chapter 8)

Principles: out of the wind, low entrance (cold air sinks), a ventilation hole that must never block, sleeping platform raised above entrance level so the coldest air pools at the door.

| Shelter | Occupants | Duration | Snow needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snow cave | 1–16 | Extended | 6 ft compacted snow |
| Snow trench | Few | Short | Less than cave |
| Tree-pit | 1–3 | Short (one night) | Almost none (uses tree) |
| Snow coffin | 1 | Emergency | Minimal |
| Snow wall | — | Short halt only | Windbreak, not a shelter |

- **Snow cave:** dig entrance low, dig up into the sleeping chamber. Ventilation hole through the roof at a **45° angle** above the entrance; leave a ski pole or branch in the hole to mark it and clear it. Wear transceivers inside in case of collapse. One candle raises inside temperature ~**2°F** and is a warning device: if it goes out, ventilation is blocked — clear the vent immediately.
- **Tree-pit:** dig down around the trunk of a large conifer where snow is naturally shallow; branches form the roof. Fast, warm enough for a night.
- **Snow trench:** dig a trench, roof with skis/branches/tarp/snow blocks. Faster than a cave, colder than a cave — the right answer when time or snow depth is short.
- **Fallen-tree bivouac:** downed trunk and branches as structure and roof, filled in with snow and boughs.

Decision rule: pick by headcount, duration, snow depth, time, and remaining energy. The shelter that gets built beats the shelter that was planned.

## Over-the-Snow CASEVAC (Chapter 6)

- Typically requires **4–12 Marines** (pullers, security, medic) — it strips combat power and must be planned into the mission, with routes, rally points, and a landing zone reconnoitered in advance.
- Use over-the-snow litters and improvised drags; skis and snowshoes as a litter platform.
- Protect the casualty from cold during a long drag: insulation from the snow beneath, wind protection, hypothermia wrap.
- Move steadily and smoothly; rotate pullers on a schedule so the team does not exhaust itself halfway to the pickup point.
