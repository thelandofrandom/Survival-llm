# FM 31-72 Mountain Operations

## Purpose

FM 31-72, *Mountain Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, Washington, D.C., 19 May 1964, 160 pages) — the Army's 1964 mountain operations manual, written for commanders, staffs, and subordinate leaders for operations in mountainous areas, primarily below division level. It covers the mission of the infantry brigade and supporting arms and services, plus the specialized techniques and training required for combat in mountainous areas, and is applicable without modification to both nuclear and non-nuclear warfare. Its chapters are general considerations, operations, employment of the arms and services, military mountaineering, snow and glaciers, fixed alpine paths, and training, with three appendices. It is the most technically detailed mountaineering manual in this batch — the only one of the five to teach rock climbing, rope work, glacier travel, and fixed alpine path construction in full.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 31-72 is a 1964 manual and its weapons, equipment, communications, and organizational details are obsolete. For current Army doctrine, mountain operations are governed by **TC 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (2025)** and **ATP 3-90.97** — follow the current manuals where they disagree with FM 31-72. FM 31-72 remains valuable because its mountaineering technique is still sound and unusually complete: balance climbing, holds, pitons and anchors, rappels, unit movement on ropes, stream crossings, evacuation, snow and avalanche doctrine, glacier travel and crevasse rescue, and fixed alpine path construction are taught here in a detail no current single manual matches. The durable core is the climbing technique, the avalanche safety rules and rescue procedure, and the fixed alpine path doctrine — the ropes, pitons, and hardware are period equipment, and modern gear and current safety standards supersede the manual's specifics.

## 1. General Considerations

**1.1. Purpose and scope.** The manual provides doctrinal guidance to commanders, staffs, and subordinate leaders for operations in mountainous areas, for use below division level; operations at division level and above are essentially the same as in other areas. It covers the mission of the infantry brigade and supporting arms and services, plus specialized techniques and training for combat in mountains.

**1.2. Tactics.** The same tactical principles govern warfare in mountainous terrain as on any other terrain. In rugged mountains there are limits to the employment of large forces: deployment is greatly hindered and restricted, adjacent units often cannot provide mutual support, and rapid employment and shifting of reserves are difficult in the more restricted positions. Small units have many occasions for sudden and bold action, and opportunities to deceive the enemy are many. Small forces can prevent, impede, harass, or canalize the movement of the main enemy force so that when the decisive battle takes place, enemy troop strength is dissipated and he is compelled to fight under unfavorable conditions. Troops must be prepared to advance over narrow and twisting roads and paths and trackless terrain.

**1.3. Air support.** Although lift efficiency decreases markedly with increase of density altitude, maximum utilization of available Army air vehicles must be effected to obtain tactical mobility and accomplish resupply and evacuation.

## 2. Operations

**2.1. Terrain and weather, marching.** Terrain and weather dominate every movement calculation. March technique in mountains, effects of marching at altitude, march discipline, pace-making and halts, night marches, column length, and selection of routes are all covered — routes must be reconnoitered in advance, and night marches in mountains should be attempted only when absolutely necessary. Listening posts, antimechanized security, bivouac precautions (in snow and extreme cold, see FM 31-70), formations, and the influence of terrain on control measures are treated. The manual's rule — "never be lost" — is especially applicable in mountains: dominant terrain features serve as intermediate objectives and check points.

**2.2. The attack and the defense.** Initiative, resourcefulness, and judgement are the qualities the mountain demands of every leader. Approach march, supporting fires, use of smoke, exploitation and pursuit, attack against a fortified position — all with mountain modifications. The attacker's problem is that the defender on dominating ground can see and shoot down on every approach; the attacker's answer is envelopment, infiltration, and the seizure of heights the enemy considers impassable. Detached missions and small-unit initiative matter more in mountains than anywhere else.

**2.3. Employment of the arms and services.** Chapter 3 treats the infantry, artillery, armor, engineers, signal, and services in mountain operations — each arm's capabilities and limitations in steep, compartmented terrain, and the coordination required to make them work together where mutual support is difficult.

## 3. Military Mountaineering

**3.1. Importance and use.** Mountaineering skill is not a sport in this manual — it is the means by which a unit moves, fights, and evacuates casualties in ground that would otherwise stop it. Climbing experts should be selected with care; not less than one per company or battery is the manual's planning figure, and they must be used as specialists, not as ordinary riflemen.

**3.2. Mountain walking.** The foundation skill: walking on steep, broken, and slippery ground with a load, using the whole foot, keeping the body in balance, and moving with a slow, rhythmic motion. Small irregularities in the ground, however small, help the climber keep his footing when pushed down upon.

**3.3. Rock climbing.** Balance climbing is the type of movement used on steep slopes — a combination of the balanced movement of a tight-rope walker and the unbalanced climbing of a man ascending a tree or ladder. The soldier must climb with the body in balance, the weight in poise over the feet; the feet, not the hands, should usually carry the weight except on the steepest cliffs, and the hands are for balance. Feet will not hold well when the climber leans in toward the rock. Three points of support (two feet and one hand, for example) are used whenever possible; hand holds waist to shoulder high are preferable; relaxation is necessary because tense muscles tire quickly; when resting, the arms are kept low where circulation is not impaired. Use of small intermediate holds is preferable to stretching and clinging to widely separated big holds. A spread-eagle position, in which a man stretches so far he cannot let go, should be avoided. In descents, the climber faces out where the going is easy, sidewise where it is steeper.

**3.4. Holds.** Push holds (pushed down upon, help keep the arms low, but more difficult to hold in a slip — often used with pull holds); pull holds (pulled down upon, easiest to use, but most likely to break out); friction holds (dependent solely on friction of hands or feet against a relatively smooth surface with very shallow holds — difficult, giving a feeling of insecurity the inexperienced climber tries to correct by leaning close to the rock, thereby increasing insecurity); jam holds (jamming any part of the body or extremity into a crack).

**3.5. Pitons, snaplinks, and the piton hammer.** Pitons are driven into cracks to create anchors and protection points; snaplinks (carabiners) connect the rope to the piton. The manual teaches selection of cracks, driving technique, and testing — a piton that will not hold is worse than no piton, because it invites reliance on it.

**3.6. Rappels.** The rappel is the controlled descent on a fixed rope. The manual teaches the body rappel and the use of the rope around the body, with the rope crossing the hip on the side opposite the direction of movement. Rappels are used for descending cliffs, for evacuating casualties, and for moving equipment down ground that cannot be walked.

**3.7. Free climbing.** Climbing without artificial aids for upward progress (as opposed to aid climbing, where pitons and slings are used to gain height). Free climbing demands the most skill and the most judgement, and leaders should not take long leads, particularly where a fall would be serious.

**3.8. Unit movement.** Basic aids for unit movement: fixed ropes, handlines, and the techniques for moving a squad or platoon across steep ground, including hauling lines for equipment and casualties. Mountain stream crossings and bridge and stream crossing expedients (including the rope suspension crossing, in which the crosser hangs below the rope) are covered in detail — shoes should be worn to prevent foot injury in stream crossings, and crossings are made at the safest point, not the shortest.

**3.9. Evacuation.** Principles of evacuation in mountains, special training for evacuation teams, preparation for evacuation, ascending and descending slopes with casualties, field expedients, and evacuation from trees. Evacuation stations must be staffed and equipped before the operation, and the belay position must be changed with care — the casualty is never left unsecured. → `references/mountaineering.md`

## 4. Snow and Glaciers

**4.1. Snow.** General characteristics, classification of snow, and snow formations — the manual classifies snow by its structure and behavior, because the type of snow determines both the avalanche danger and the movement technique. Windslab, depth hoar, and crust each behave differently under load.

**4.2. Avalanches — causes.** Avalanches are one of the principal dangers of mountaineering. Basic causes: large amounts of snow and steep slopes, acting together. The manual's treatment parallels FM 90-6's Appendix F (minimum angle 25 degrees, critical zone above 35 degrees) and adds tactical detail: stabilization by explosives (charges of approximately 5 pounds thrown over the crest to land 5 to 6 meters below it, detonated from behind the crest; 5 to 10 pounds of TNT controls about 39 meters of slope width), stabilization by artillery and infantry weapons (recoilless rifles are the handiest tool, since artillery pieces are hard to move off roads), stabilization by aircraft (guns, rockets, or sonic boom), and use of barriers (diversion walls and piers) to protect lines of communication and fixed installations.

**4.3. Avalanche safety rules.** The manual's rules of conduct for negotiating snow-covered terrain that threatens to avalanche:

- **Adjust equipment:** remove the safety strap on the ice axe from the wrist; loosen ski bindings for quick removal and remove ski pole straps from wrists — skis tangle, restrict movement, and get dragged under sliding snow. Where snow permits, negotiate danger zones on foot, carrying the skis. While skiing, avoid falling and execute all movements with caution.
- **Select routes:** learn the avalanche paths and detour around hazardous slopes. The crest of a ridge is a safe route, but never move on the overhang of a cornice. Avoid the bottom of narrow, V-shaped valleys, where a disturbance could cause a double avalanche from both sides and fill the valley floor instantly. Movement along the middle of wide, U-shaped valleys is less dangerous. Do not move above natural barriers such as a moraine on the valley floor, since the slide collides with the barrier.
- **Weather:** obtain the latest forecast before starting; during the move be alert for any change — any sudden change can be dangerous.
- **Ascent and descent:** climb avalanche slopes as straight up or as straight down as possible, preferably along a line of protruding rocks, ledges, and trees where the snow is less likely to slide. Traversing back and forth is avoided, since the snow cover is dissected and disturbed. Ravines, gullies, and low terrain features where snow deposits collect are avoided.
- **Observation and testing:** constantly observe the area for signs of danger and test whenever the tactical situation permits. Before entering a dangerous slope, test a small area of the same grade and exposure. Avoid windslab formations — they give a false impression of solidity. Watch the shadows: when they point at a slope like warning fingers, sun action is at its maximum. Seek heavy timber, windbeaten slopes, and terrain barriers.
- **Timing and spacing:** greatly increase the distance between individuals; expose only one or the least possible number of individuals to the danger at one time. If a dangerous slope, hollow, or gully must be traversed, let one man test the slope, belayed with the climbing rope by other members. Maintain visual contact and post observers at safe locations.
- **Avalanche cord:** on reconnaissance in avalanche areas, tie one end of a brightly colored cord (15 to 20 meters long) around the body and trail it behind — the cord ordinarily remains on or near the surface of the slide and facilitates location and rescue.
- **If caught:** DON'T PANIC. Maintain self-control, attempt to stay on the surface and get out of the main slide path. Execute "swimming" motions to combat the tendency of sliding snow to pull one underneath. Grab any obstruction. Kick off the skis. After being completely covered, hold both arms in front of the face and push against the covering snow to provide an airspace before the slide hardens.
- **Tactical locations:** exercise great care in selecting battle positions, command posts, outposts, security elements, battery positions, bivouac sites, and administrative support bases in avalanche areas.

**4.4. Avalanche rescue.** Prompt and organized rescue offers the only hope of getting the victim out alive. There are records of persons who lived as long as 72 hours buried, but ordinarily victims are killed instantly by crushing or die within a short period from exposure, shock, and suffocation — suffocation is the most important item. **Two hours is the average survival limit**: snow is porous and ordinarily contains enough air to support life, though not consciousness, but in about two hours an ice mask, caused by condensation of the victim's breath, forms an air-proof seal around his face and he dies of suffocation. Rescue operations are designed to get the victim out within the two-hour limit, and should not be discontinued for 24 hours at least. The rescue operation has three phases — immediate action, followup, and evacuation. Immediate action: surviving members at the site organize into the first rescue team and start at once — their well-organized and immediate action is generally the main factor in successful rescue. The followup rescue party should be on its way **within 15 minutes** after receiving the alarm, moving out lightly equipped — speed is the first consideration. The first team posts an avalanche guard, locates and marks the "last seen point" so it cannot be obliterated by wind or snowfall (eyewitnesses, even in poor physical condition, should return to the scene to point out where victims were last seen), makes a hasty search of the slide surface downhill from the last seen point, and probes where indications are found — or in likely locations such as obstructions in the slide — if none are found. Digging is organized, not haphazard: searchers should be prevented from wasting energy digging in unlikely spots.

**4.5. Movement over snow and ice; glaciers.** Equipment for glacier movement, movement techniques, principal dangers, and use of the rope — the rope is the lifeline on a glacier, and the manual stresses that it only supplements, and does not replace, route selection and judgement. Crevasses that gape wide open are obvious and an inconvenience rather than a danger; narrow cracks can be jumped if takeoff and landing are firm; wider cracks must be circumvented unless a solid ice bridge strong enough to support at least one member of the party joins the sides. **Snow bridges** are formed of windblown snow building a cornice over the empty interior of the crevasse; they can also form without wind, especially during heavy falls of dry snow — and since dry snow has little cohesion, such bridges are unreliable and must be tested. Routes across glaciers are chosen to avoid crevasse fields, and the party moves roped, with spacing, so that a fall into a crevasse can be held by the others. → `references/snow-glaciers-avalanche.md`

## 5. Fixed Alpine Paths

**5.1. Purpose.** Fixed alpine paths are prepared routes — fixed ropes, handlines, ladders, and cableways — that let ordinary troops cross ground they could not climb. They are the means by which a whole unit, not just its climbers, moves through steep terrain. Site conditions, transportation of materials, and the steps of construction are covered in detail.

**5.2. Components.** Handlines (ropes fixed along a route for hand-over-hand movement); ladders (for short vertical steps, with anchors top and bottom — an anchor is usually sufficient at the bottom); the three-rope suspended walkway (for crossing gaps); cableways (for moving supplies and casualties across ravines and rivers). Inspection and repair are standing requirements — ropes and cables lose strength with use and weather, and a fixed path that is not inspected becomes a trap.

**5.3. Construction and use.** The manual gives the steps for building each component, the anchors required, and the supervision needed. Installation teams build the paths; guides supervise their use; every soldier using a fixed path must be briefed on the technique and the safety rules. A fixed alpine path is a force multiplier — it converts impassable ground into a usable route — but it is also a fixed target and a maintenance burden, and its location must be chosen with both in mind. → `references/fixed-alpine-paths.md`

## 6. Training

**6.1. General.** Training is the heart of the manual: selection of students, supervisors, instructors, class organization, and training aids. Instructors are responsible for safety, for grades and records, and for ensuring that training is realistic without being reckless.

**6.2. The training progression.** Basic movement skills first, then rock climbing, then rope work, then snow and glacier work, then fixed alpine paths, then unit movement and evacuation under realistic conditions. A soldier who has not been trained in mountaineering cannot be expected to perform it under fire; a unit that has not trained together on ropes cannot be expected to move together on a mountain.

## Output Contract

When answering a mountain operations question from FM 31-72, give the technique first (climbing, rope work, avalanche procedure, fixed path), then the numbers (25-degree avalanche minimum, 2-hour survival limit, 15-minute followup, 15–20 meter avalanche cord, 5–10 pounds of TNT per 39 meters of slope), then the cautions. State the 1964 vintage and the period equipment, and where current doctrine (TC 3-97.61, ATP 3-90.97) differs, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Feet carry the weight, hands are for balance.** Three points of support, small intermediate holds, never a spread-eagle.
2. **Test before you trust.** Pitons, snow bridges, avalanche slopes, fixed ropes — everything is tested before it is relied on.
3. **Two hours is the limit.** Avalanche rescue is a race against the ice mask; the followup party moves within 15 minutes.
4. **One man tests the slope, roped.** Never expose the whole party to avalanche danger at once.
5. **Fixed paths multiply the force.** A prepared route turns impassable ground into a usable route — but it must be inspected and maintained.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** TC 3-97.61 (2025) and ATP 3-90.97 supersede FM 31-72 where they conflict; FM 31-72 is the deep mountaineering-technique reference, not the authority.
