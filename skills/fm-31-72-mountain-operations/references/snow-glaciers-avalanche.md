# Snow, Glaciers, Avalanche — FM 31-72

## Snow

General characteristics, classification of snow, and snow formations. The manual classifies snow by its structure and behavior, because the type of snow determines both the avalanche danger and the movement technique. Windslab, depth hoar, and crust each behave differently under load: windslab gives a false impression of solidity; depth hoar is a weak layer of large, loose crystals at the bottom of the snowpack; crust is a hard layer formed by sun or rain that can support weight until it breaks.

## Avalanches — Causes and Stabilization

Avalanches are one of the principal dangers of mountaineering. Basic causes: large amounts of snow and steep slopes, acting together. The manual's treatment parallels FM 90-6's Appendix F (minimum angle 25 degrees, critical zone above 35 degrees) and adds tactical detail on stabilization:

- **Stabilization by explosives.** Throw charges of approximately 5 pounds over the crest, with the electric wire coiled so the charge lands on the slope 5 to 6 meters below the crest, then detonate safely from behind the crest. Under certain snow conditions more powerful charges may be required; as a rule of thumb, approximately 5 to 10 pounds of TNT controls 39 meters of slope width. Huge cornices are blasted by digging charges into the snow along the probable line of fracture; individuals digging holes and placing charges must be belayed from the crest while working.
- **Stabilization by artillery and infantry weapons.** Carrying explosives and demolition equipment up mountain slopes in deep snow is hard work; an easy way to cause the snow to slide is to use artillery pieces within their range. Because artillery pieces are difficult to move off roads or over secondary mountain trails, recoilless rifles become the handiest tool in campaigns against avalanches.
- **Stabilization by aircraft.** Where artillery and recoilless rifles are limited by range, avalanches may be set off against lines of communication deep in enemy territory by aircraft; the pilot selects suitable slopes and causes the snow to slide by using guns, rockets, or sonic boom.
- **Use of barriers.** Lines of communication and fixed installations under avalanche threat can be protected by diversion walls and piers.

## Avalanche Safety Rules

Rules of conduct for negotiating snow-covered terrain that threatens to avalanche:

- **Adjust equipment:** remove the safety strap on the ice axe from the wrist; loosen ski bindings for quick removal and remove ski pole straps from wrists — skis tangle, restrict movement, and get dragged under sliding snow. Where snow permits, negotiate danger zones on foot, carrying the skis. While skiing, avoid falling and execute all movements with caution.
- **Select routes:** learn the avalanche paths and detour around hazardous slopes. The crest of a ridge is a safe route, but never move on the overhang of a cornice. Avoid the bottom of narrow, V-shaped valleys, where a disturbance could cause a double avalanche from both sides and fill the valley floor instantly. Movement along the middle of wide, U-shaped valleys is less dangerous. Do not move above natural barriers such as a moraine on the valley floor, since the slide collides with the barrier.
- **Weather:** obtain the latest forecast before starting; during the move be alert for any change — any sudden change can be dangerous.
- **Ascent and descent:** climb avalanche slopes as straight up or as straight down as possible, preferably along a line of protruding rocks, ledges, and trees where the snow is less likely to slide. Traversing back and forth is avoided, since the snow cover is dissected and disturbed. Ravines, gullies, and low terrain features where snow deposits collect are avoided.
- **Observation and testing:** constantly observe the area for signs of danger and test whenever the tactical situation permits. Before entering a dangerous slope, test a small area of the same grade and exposure. Avoid windslab formations — they give a false impression of solidity. Watch the shadows: when they point at a slope like warning fingers, sun action is at its maximum. Seek heavy timber, windbeaten slopes, and terrain barriers.
- **Timing and spacing:** greatly increase the distance between individuals; expose only one or the least possible number of individuals to the danger at one time. If a dangerous slope, hollow, or gully must be traversed, let one man test the slope, belayed with the climbing rope by other members. Maintain visual contact and post observers at safe locations.
- **Avalanche cord:** on reconnaissance in avalanche areas, tie one end of a brightly colored cord (15 to 20 meters long) around the body and trail it behind — the cord ordinarily remains on or near the surface of the slide and facilitates location and rescue.
- **If caught:** DON'T PANIC. Maintain self-control, attempt to stay on the surface and get out of the main slide path. Execute "swimming" motions to combat the tendency of sliding snow to pull one underneath. Grab any obstruction. Kick off the skis. After being completely covered, hold both arms in front of the face and push against the covering snow to provide an airspace before the slide hardens.
- **Tactical locations:** exercise great care in selecting battle positions, command posts, outposts, security elements, battery positions, bivouac sites, and administrative support bases in avalanche areas.

## Avalanche Rescue

Prompt and organized rescue offers the only hope of getting the victim out alive. There are records of persons who lived as long as 72 hours buried, but ordinarily victims are killed instantly by crushing or die within a short period from exposure, shock, and suffocation — suffocation is the most important item. **Two hours is the average survival limit**: snow is porous and ordinarily contains enough air to support life, though not consciousness, but in about two hours an ice mask, caused by condensation of the victim's breath, forms an air-proof seal around his face and he dies of suffocation. Rescue operations are designed to get the victim out within the two-hour limit, and should not be discontinued for 24 hours at least.

**Phases:** immediate action, followup, and evacuation. Successful rescue depends on decisive leadership, proficiency of the search party, and availability of special equipment.

- **Immediate action.** Surviving members at the site organize into the first rescue team and start at once — their well-organized and immediate action is generally the main factor in successful rescue. The leader decides whether a followup rescue party and additional help are needed; if so, it is sent for by the most expeditious means.
- **Followup.** The rescue party on duty, on being alerted, is issued special equipment and provided transportation, dispatched under the most experienced officer or NCO available, with strength of at least one squad. It should be on its way **within 15 minutes** after receiving the alarm, moving out lightly equipped — speed is the first consideration.
- **Search.** The first rescue team posts an avalanche guard for protection of the search parties; locates and marks the "last seen point" so it cannot be obliterated by wind or snowfall — eyewitnesses, even in poor physical condition, should return to the scene to point out where victims were last seen. From the last seen point downhill, the team makes a hasty search of the slide surface for victims or any part of their equipment. If any indication of the victim's location is found, probing starts in that vicinity; if none is found, probing starts in likely locations such as obstructions in the slide. Digging is organized, not haphazard: searchers should be prevented from wasting energy digging in unlikely spots.

## Glaciers

**Equipment and movement.** Equipment for glacier movement, movement techniques, principal dangers, and use of the rope — the rope is the lifeline on a glacier, and it only supplements, and does not replace, route selection and judgement. The party moves roped, with spacing, so that a fall into a crevasse can be held by the others. Routes are chosen to avoid crevasse fields; walking sticks and frequent checks of the snow surface are used to detect hidden crevasses.

**Crevasses.** Crevasses that gape wide open are obvious and an inconvenience rather than a danger. Narrow cracks can be jumped, provided takeoff and landing spots are firm and offer good footing. Wider cracks must be circumvented unless a solid piece of ice joins into an ice bridge strong enough to support at least the weight of one member of the party. **Snow bridges** are formed of windblown snow building a cornice over the empty interior of the crevasse; as the cornice grows from the windward side, a counterpart forms on the leeward side, and the junction occurs over the middle of the crevasse only when contributing winds blow equally from each side. Bridges can also form without wind, especially during heavy falls of dry snow — and since dry snow has little cohesion, such bridges are unreliable and must be tested before anyone commits to them.
