# Fixed Alpine Paths — FM 31-72

## Purpose

Fixed alpine paths are prepared routes — fixed ropes, handlines, ladders, and cableways — that let ordinary troops cross ground they could not climb. They are the means by which a whole unit, not just its climbers, moves through steep terrain. A fixed alpine path converts impassable ground into a usable route — but it is also a fixed target and a maintenance burden, and its location must be chosen with both in mind.

## Site Conditions

Site selection considers the ground, the exposure, the availability of anchors, the route the path must serve, and the tactical situation. The path must serve the movement it is built for — a path that does not connect useful positions is wasted effort. Transportation of materials (ropes, ladders, cable, anchors, tools) to the site is itself a mountaineering problem and must be planned as part of the construction.

## Components

**Handlines.** Ropes fixed along a route for hand-over-hand movement. Handlines let troops move across steep or exposed ground with a handhold at all times; they are the simplest fixed installation and the most widely used.

**Ladders.** For short vertical steps where a rope alone is not enough. Ladders require anchors at the top and usually at the bottom — an anchor is usually sufficient at the bottom. Ladders must be secured against shifting and tested before use.

**Three-rope suspended walkway.** For crossing gaps — ravines, streams, and crevasses — where no bridge can be built. Two ropes serve as handrails and one as the walkway, or the equivalent arrangement, with anchors at either end. The walkway must be tensioned and tested before anyone crosses.

**Cableways.** For moving supplies and casualties across ravines and rivers where a walkway is impractical. A cableway carries a load suspended from a cable, moved by rope or by gravity; it requires strong anchors at both ends and careful attention to the load limits of the cable and the anchors.

## Construction Steps

The manual gives the steps for building each component: selection of the route, selection and preparation of anchors, installation of the ropes or cables, tensioning, testing, and marking. Installation teams build the paths; guides supervise their use; every soldier using a fixed path must be briefed on the technique and the safety rules. Construction is done by trained mountaineers, roped and belayed, and the path is not opened to general use until it has been tested under load.

## Inspection and Repair

Inspection and repair are standing requirements — ropes and cables lose strength with use and weather, and a fixed path that is not inspected becomes a trap. Inspect anchors, ropes, cables, ladders, and walkways regularly and after every storm, every heavy use, and every enemy action in the area. Repair or replace any component that shows wear, fraying, corrosion, or loss of tension. A path that cannot be maintained should be closed rather than left in use.

## Training

Training for fixed alpine paths is part of the mountaineering training progression: basic movement skills first, then rock climbing, then rope work, then snow and glacier work, then fixed alpine paths, then unit movement and evacuation under realistic conditions. Instructors are responsible for safety, for grades and records, and for ensuring that training is realistic without being reckless. A soldier who has not been trained in mountaineering cannot be expected to perform it under fire; a unit that has not trained together on ropes cannot be expected to move together on a mountain.
