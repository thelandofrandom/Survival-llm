# Mountaineering — FM 31-72

## Importance and Use

Mountaineering skill is not a sport in this manual — it is the means by which a unit moves, fights, and evacuates casualties in ground that would otherwise stop it. Climbing experts should be selected with care; not less than one per company or battery is the manual's planning figure, and they must be used as specialists, not as ordinary riflemen. The manual's stratification: basic movement skills for all, advanced techniques for specialists, expert qualification for the few who lead on technical ground.

## Mountain Walking

The foundation skill: walking on steep, broken, and slippery ground with a load, using the whole foot, keeping the body in balance, and moving with a slow, rhythmic motion. Small irregularities in the ground, however small, help the climber keep his footing when pushed down upon. Relaxation is necessary because tense muscles tire quickly; when resting, the arms are kept low where circulation is not impaired.

## Rock Climbing

**Balance climbing** is the type of movement used on steep slopes — a combination of the balanced movement of a tight-rope walker and the unbalanced climbing of a man ascending a tree or ladder. The soldier must climb with the body in balance, the weight in poise over the feet; the feet, not the hands, should usually carry the weight except on the steepest cliffs, and the hands are for balance. Feet will not hold well when the climber leans in toward the rock. **Three points of support** (two feet and one hand, for example) are used whenever possible; hand holds waist to shoulder high are preferable. Use of small intermediate holds is preferable to stretching and clinging to widely separated big holds. A **spread-eagle position**, in which a man stretches so far he cannot let go, should be avoided. In descents, the climber faces out where the going is easy, sidewise where it is steeper.

**Holds:**
- **Push holds** — pushed down upon; help the climber keep his arms desirably low, but are more difficult to hold to in case of a slip. Often used to advantage in combination with a pull hold.
- **Pull holds** — pulled down upon; the easiest holds to use, but also the most likely to break out.
- **Friction holds** — dependent solely on the friction of hands or feet against a relatively smooth surface with very shallow holds. Difficult to use because they give a feeling of insecurity which the inexperienced climber tries to correct by leaning close to the rock, thereby increasing his insecurity. They often serve well as intermediate holds, some of which will give needed support while the climber moves over them but would not hold him were he to stop.
- **Jam holds** — jamming any part of the body or extremity into a crack.

## Pitons, Snaplinks, and the Piton Hammer

Pitons are driven into cracks to create anchors and protection points; snaplinks (carabiners) connect the rope to the piton. The manual teaches selection of cracks, driving technique, and testing — a piton that will not hold is worse than no piton, because it invites reliance on it. Anchors must be tested before they are loaded; multiple anchors share the load where a single anchor is doubtful.

## Rappels

The rappel is the controlled descent on a fixed rope. The manual teaches the body rappel and the use of the rope around the body, with the rope crossing the hip on the side opposite the direction of movement. Rappels are used for descending cliffs, for evacuating casualties, and for moving equipment down ground that cannot be walked. The rappel must be practiced on easy ground before it is used on a cliff, and the anchor must be tested before anyone commits to the rope.

## Free Climbing

Climbing without artificial aids for upward progress (as opposed to aid climbing, where pitons and slings are used to gain height). Free climbing demands the most skill and the most judgement, and leaders should not take long leads, particularly where a fall would be serious. The leader's protection — pitons placed on the way up — is what makes a long lead survivable; a leader who runs out of protection is climbing without a net.

## Unit Movement

Basic aids for unit movement: fixed ropes, handlines, and the techniques for moving a squad or platoon across steep ground, including hauling lines for equipment and casualties. The party moves with spacing, with the rope as the lifeline, and with a belay wherever a fall is possible.

**Mountain stream crossings.** Crossings are made at the safest point, not the shortest. Shoes should be worn to prevent foot injury. Bridge and stream crossing expedients include the rope suspension crossing, in which the crosser hangs below the rope and is pulled across. The current, the depth, the bottom, and the availability of a belay all determine the crossing method.

## Evacuation

Principles of evacuation in mountains: evacuation stations must be established and staffed before the operation; special training is required for evacuation teams; preparation includes ropes, litters, and anchors at the evacuation site. Ascending and descending slopes with casualties requires belays at every stage — the casualty is never left unsecured, and the belay position must be changed with care, one anchor at a time. Field expedients (improvised litters, rope harnesses) and evacuation from trees (casualties caught in trees after a fall or a parachute landing) are covered. The manual's rule: evacuation is planned before the operation, not improvised after casualties occur.
