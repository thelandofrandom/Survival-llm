# FM 21-76 Survival

## Purpose

FM 21-76, *Survival* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 5 June 1992, 646 pages) — the classic Army survival manual and the foundation of modern U.S. military survival doctrine. It is written for any soldier who may be sent to any area of the world — temperate, tropical, arctic, or subarctic — and find themselves cut off from their unit, without their equipment, and on their own. Its premise is stated in the preface: survival is not luck, it is preparation plus the refusal to give up. The manual covers the whole arc of staying alive in isolation: the psychology that decides whether you live, the planning and kit that precede the event, medicine, shelter, water, fire, food, and then the environment-specific chapters (desert, tropical, cold, sea) and the hostile-area chapters (direction finding, signaling, evasion movement, camouflage, contact with people, man-made hazards).

**Supersession note — read this first.** For current Army doctrine, FM 21-76 is largely superseded by **ATP 3-50.21, *Survival* (September 2018)**, which is already a skill in this vault. ATP 3-50.21 is the current, shorter, authoritative Army survival manual; where the two disagree on a procedure or a number, follow ATP 3-50.21. FM 21-76 remains valuable for three reasons: it is far more detailed (646 pages against ATP 3-50.21's ~200), its environment chapters (desert, tropical, cold weather, sea) and its hostile-area chapters (evasion movement, camouflage, contact with people, man-made hazards) have no equivalent depth in the newer manual, and its field-expedient weapons, tools, and equipment chapter is unique. Use ATP 3-50.21 as the authority and FM 21-76 as the deep field reference.

The manual's core organizing idea is the keyword **SURVIVAL** — each letter a standing action (see §1). Everything else in the book hangs off that word.

## 1. The Psychology of Survival — Will First

**1.1. Survival is a mental problem before it is a physical one.** The manual is blunt: "the will to survive" can also be considered "the refusal to give up." Stress is the body's reaction to pressure — fight, flight, or freeze — and in moderate doses it sharpens performance. In excess it becomes distress: panic, forgetfulness, carelessness, angry outbursts, withdrawal, thoughts of death. The survivor's job is to manage stress, not eliminate it. Techniques the manual names: relaxation skills, time management, assertiveness, and cognitive restructuring — controlling how you view the situation, because you can control your response even when you cannot control your circumstances.

**1.2. The SURVIVAL acronym.** The memory tool for sound decisions under pressure:

- **S — Size up the situation.** Surroundings, physical condition, equipment. Think before acting.
- **U — Undue haste makes waste.** Panic-driven rushing wastes energy and creates mistakes.
- **R — Remember where you are.** Orient yourself; know your location relative to safety, water, and danger.
- **V — Vanquish fear and panic.** Fear is normal. Acknowledge it, then act on training.
- **I — Improvise.** Everything is a potential tool. Ordinary objects have second uses.
- **V — Value living.** The will to live is a decision. People who give up psychologically die long before their bodies fail.
- **A — Act like the natives.** Local people have already solved the problems of this environment — watch what they eat, wear, and avoid.
- **L — Learn basic skills.** Skills practiced in training become confidence in isolation.

**1.3. Common survival stressors.** Fear, anxiety, anger, frustration, guilt, depression, and loneliness are all normal reactions — expect them and do not mistake them for failure. Countermeasures are routine and purpose: set small achievable goals, keep a schedule, stay busy with survival tasks, maintain hope through a realistic plan for recovery. A survivor working a plan is far harder to break than one waiting passively. → `references/survival-pattern-and-kits.md`

## 2. Survival Planning and Survival Kits

**2.1. Planning is preparation.** Survival planning is nothing more than realizing something could happen that would put you in a survival situation and taking steps to increase your chances. Detailed prior planning is essential: include survival considerations in mission planning, know your route, know the area, keep dental problems fixed and immunizations current — a toothache or a preventable disease in isolation can end a survival effort. All Army aircraft normally carry survival kits for the type of area they fly over (over-water, hot-climate, and aviator survival vest kits, described in Appendix A); if you are not an aviator, knowing what those kits contain helps you build your own.

**2.2. Layer your kit.** The environment decides what you need; how you carry it decides how much. Keep the most important items on your body (map and compass always on the body), less important items on load-bearing equipment, bulky items in the rucksack. Select items usable for more than one purpose; do not duplicate functions — duplication only adds size and weight. The case should be water-repellent or waterproof, easy to carry or attach to the body, able to accept variously sized components, and durable — a Band-Aid box, first aid case, or ammunition pouch all work.

**2.3. Kit contents.** A functional kit holds: first aid items; water purification tablets or drops; fire-starting equipment; signaling items; food-procurement items; shelter items. Examples the manual lists: lighter, metal match, waterproof matches; snare wire; signaling mirror; wrist compass; fish and snare line; fishhooks; candle; small hand lens; oxytetracycline tablets (diarrhea or infection); solar blanket; surgical blades; butterfly sutures; condoms for water storage; Chap Stick; needle and thread; knife. Include a weapon only if the situation dictates. Read about and practice the techniques before you need them. → `references/survival-pattern-and-kits.md`

## 3. Basic Survival Medicine

**3.1. Health maintenance comes first.** Foremost among the problems that compromise a survivor are medical problems from parachute descent and landing, extreme climates, ground combat, evasion, and illnesses contracted in captivity. Survivors have reported that the inability to treat themselves produced apathy and helplessness that led to capture or surrender; the ability to treat themselves raised morale and aided return. One person with a fair amount of basic medical knowledge can make the difference for many.

**3.2. Water and hygiene.** At 20°C (68°F) with average daily exertion, the average adult loses — and therefore requires — **2 to 3 liters of water daily**. Heat, cold, intense activity, high altitude, burns, or illness increase losses. Dehydration decreases efficiency and, if injured, increases susceptibility to shock. Maintain high personal hygiene standards: wash hands, keep feet dry and sound, protect against insects and parasites.

**3.3. Lifesaving priorities.** Open the airway and restore breathing, stop severe bleeding with direct pressure and pressure dressings, then treat for shock (keep the casualty warm, lying flat with legs elevated unless head, chest, or abdominal injury forbids it). Splint fractures as found — do not straighten a deformed limb in the field. Treat heat and cold injuries early: cool a heat casualty in shade with water and airflow; warm a cold casualty gradually with insulation and warm liquids; never rub frostbitten tissue and never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze.

**3.4. Field remedies and cautions.** The manual catalogs improvised treatments — chlorine bleach, alcohol, vinegar, concentrated salt water, and iodine for fungal infections, with varying success — and warns to use any unorthodox method with caution. For rashes: if it is moist, keep it dry; if it is dry, keep it moist; do not scratch; use a compress of vinegar or tannic acid from tea or from boiling acorns or hardwood bark to dry weeping rashes. → `references/survival-pattern-and-kits.md`

## 4. Shelters

**4.1. Shelter is protection.** A shelter protects against weather, cold, heat, insects, and observation. Site selection matters more than construction: pick high, dry ground away from standing water and animal trails, near water and materials but not in a drainage or avalanche path, with concealment from the enemy in hostile areas. Build with what is at hand — poncho, parachute material, branches, leaves, snow.

**4.2. Types.** The manual covers the poncho lean-to, poncho tent, and field-expedient shelters built from natural materials; in cold weather, snow shelters (snow cave, snow trench) use snow's insulating property; in the desert, shade and a below-ground shelter beat a sun-baked above-ground one. Whatever the type: insulate yourself from the ground (ground contact steals heat faster than air), keep the entrance low and away from the wind, and make the shelter just big enough — a shelter that is too large is a shelter you cannot warm.

## 5. Water Procurement and Purification

**5.1. How much and where.** The 2–3 liter daily baseline (§3.2) is the planning figure; in high heat, sweat loss can reach several quarts per hour. Find water by following animal trails downhill, watching birds circling at dawn and dusk, noting green vegetation in dry country, and watching insects — bees and mosquitoes rarely range far from water. Collect from rain, dew, transpiration bags over leafy branches, solar stills, green bamboo sections, vines (cut high first, then low, let it drip — avoid milky or bitter sap), and banana stumps.

**5.2. Purification — the numbers that matter.**

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Boiling (safest) | Rolling boil **1 minute at sea level, adding 1 minute for each additional 300 meters above sea level** — or boil 10 minutes no matter where you are |
| Iodine, 2% tincture | **5 drops per quart of clear water; 10 drops if cloudy or cold**; wait **30 minutes** |
| Purification tablets | Follow the directions provided |
| Filtration | Settle muddy water **12 hours**, or filter through sand, crushed rock, charcoal, or cloth in bamboo, a hollow log, or clothing — this only *clears* water; you must still purify it |
| Deodorizing | Add charcoal from your fire; let stand **45 minutes** |

Rainwater collected in clean containers or from plants is usually safe; purify water from lakes, ponds, swamps, springs, or streams — especially near human settlements or in the tropics. Never drink urine or seawater. Untreated water risks dysentery (severe, prolonged diarrhea with bloody stools, fever, weakness), cholera and typhoid (inoculations do not guarantee protection), blood flukes in stagnant tropical water, and leeches that can attach in the throat or nose. → `references/water-fire-shelter.md`

## 6. Firecraft

**6.1. What fire is for.** Fire provides warmth, light, a means to purify water and cook food, a way to dry clothing, a signal, and a psychological anchor. You need three types of material: **tinder** (dry material that ignites with little heat — a spark starts it; it must be absolutely dry), **kindling** (small sticks that catch from the tinder), and **fuel** (larger wood that sustains the fire). Prepare all three before you strike the first spark.

**6.2. Fire lays.** The tepee fire (tinder in the middle, kindling arranged in a cone above) lights easily and burns hot; the lean-to fire (tinder under a leaning stick, kindling leaned against it) works well in wind; the cross-ditch fire (tinder in the middle of a cross, kindling pyramid above, a shallow ditch letting air sweep under the tinder) ventilates well. Keep a fire small and controlled — a small fire you can feed is better than a large one you cannot.

**6.3. Ignition methods.** The manual ranks methods by reliability. **Flint and steel** is the most reliable direct-spark method: strike a flint or other hard, sharp-edged rock with a piece of steel so sparks fall on the tinder. **Convex lens** (hand lens, camera lens, binoculars) works only on bright, sunny days — angle it to concentrate the sun's rays on the tinder until it smolders, then blow gently into flame. **Battery and wire**: touch bare wires together next to the tinder so the sparks ignite it. **Gunpowder**: a spark will ignite powder — be extremely careful. Friction methods (bow drill, fire plow, hand drill) work but demand dry wood, correct technique, and practice; learn them in training, not in an emergency. Always carry at least two ignition methods — a lighter and waterproof matches, plus a metal match or flint. → `references/water-fire-shelter.md`

## 7. Food Procurement and Plants

**7.1. Food priorities.** Food is the lowest immediate priority — a healthy person can survive weeks without it — but it sustains energy, warmth, and morale for movement and work. Procure food by fishing, trapping, and snaring (snares work while unattended — set them on trails to water, feeding, and bedding areas and at den mouths), and by gathering plants. A simple loop snare uses a figure-8 locking loop when wire is used: once tightened, the wire locks and the animal cannot escape.

**7.2. The Universal Edibility Test (UET).** Test only one part of one plant at a time, and only if the plant is abundant — each part takes more than 24 hours to test. Avoid plants with milky sap (dandelion is a safe, recognizable exception), spines, fine hairs, or thorns (prickly pear and thistles are exceptions), mushrooms and fungus (avoid entirely — identification is too unreliable and the penalty too high), and umbrella-shaped flower clusters (hemlock). Even after a food tests safe, eat in moderation — large portions of plant food on an empty stomach can cause diarrhea, nausea, or cramps. The full step-by-step test (skin contact, lip, tongue, chew-and-spit, small swallow, then a measured portion with waiting periods between each step) is in `references/food-plants-animals.md`.

**7.3. Poisonous plants.** The danger signs: milky sap, almond scent, spines or fine hairs, umbrella-shaped flower clusters, beans or bulbs, seeds inside pods. Never eat a plant you cannot positively identify, and never test an unknown plant by eating a full portion. Learn the edible plants of your area of operations before you deploy — identification in the field is a training task, not an improvisation.

**7.4. Dangerous animals.** Know the dangerous animals of the area: venomous snakes, large predators, insects, and marine hazards (Chapter 16 covers sharks). Prevention beats treatment — watch where you put hands and feet, shake out clothing and bedding, avoid carrion and animal dens, and do not handle unfamiliar animals. For snakebite: keep the casualty calm and still, keep the bitten limb below heart level, remove constricting items, and evacuate — do not cut, suck, or apply a tourniquet.

**7.5. Field-expedient weapons, tools, and equipment.** Improvise clubs, spears, slings, and traps from available material; fashion cutting and digging tools from stone, bone, and metal scrap. A knife is the single most valuable field tool — protect it, keep it sharp, and use it for everything from shelter construction to food preparation. → `references/food-plants-animals.md`

## 8. The Environments — Desert, Tropical, Cold, Sea

**8.1. Desert survival.** In the desert, water is the decisive factor and shade is life. Travel at night and rest in shade during the day; cover the body to reduce sweat loss — long sleeves and a head covering beat bare skin. Conserve water by limiting activity, breathing through the nose, and avoiding alcohol. Desert water sources: follow animal trails and birds, dig in dry streambeds at the outside of bends, and collect dew. Heat injuries (heat cramps, heat exhaustion, heatstroke) kill fast — recognize confusion and hot, dry skin as an emergency and cool the casualty immediately.

**8.2. Tropical survival.** Heat, humidity, insects, and disease dominate. The jungle provides abundant water and food but hides hazards: leeches, venomous snakes, and waterborne disease. Keep the body dry when possible (fungal infections thrive in constant damp), treat all water, protect food from insects, and move slowly — the jungle punishes haste with injury and exhaustion. Build shelters off the ground to avoid flooding and ground insects.

**8.3. Cold-weather survival.** Cold kills through hypothermia and frostbite, and it dehydrates — the thirst response weakens in cold, so drink on schedule, not on thirst. Priorities reverse: fire and shelter come before water and food. Dress in layers, keep dry (sweat is the enemy — ventilate before you sweat), insulate from the ground, and build snow shelters that exploit snow's insulating value. Eat more — the body burns extra fuel to stay warm. Never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze; never rub frostbitten tissue.

**8.4. Sea survival.** After abandoning ship or ditching, get out of the water and into a raft or onto flotsam — immersion in cold water kills in minutes to hours. Protect against sun, salt-water sores, and dehydration; do not drink seawater. Collect rainwater, use the solar still, and fish. Signal with mirror, dye marker, and flares. Sharks: avoid splashing and blood in the water; if attacked, strike the eyes, gills, or nose. → `references/environments-and-signaling.md`

## 9. Direction Finding, Signaling, and Evasion

**9.1. Field-expedient direction finding.** Without a compass: the stick-and-shadow method (mark the tip of a shadow, wait 15–30 minutes, mark the new tip — the line between marks runs roughly west to east in the Northern Hemisphere), the watch method (point the hour hand at the sun; in the Northern Hemisphere the line halfway between the hour hand and 12 o'clock points south), and the stars (Polaris in the north; the Southern Cross in the south). Moss does not reliably grow on the north side of trees — do not rely on folklore.

**9.2. Signaling.** Signal only when you are ready to be found — in hostile areas, signaling invites the enemy as well as rescue. The signaling mirror is the most effective daytime visual signal; use smoke by day (dark smoke against light background, light smoke against dark) and fire by night; ground-to-air signals (large, contrasting symbols laid out in open ground) communicate specific messages to aircraft. Sound signals (whistle blasts in groups of three, gunshots) carry farther than shouting and save energy. Carry at least one signaling device on your body at all times.

**9.3. Survival movement in hostile areas.** In hostile territory, security takes priority over comfort and speed. Move at night when possible, use concealment, avoid trails and open ground, cross danger areas quickly, and establish hole-up sites where you can rest, reassess, treat injuries, and plan. Camouflage breaks up your outline — use natural materials, mud, and charcoal on skin and equipment, and avoid shiny objects and strong smells. Move with a purpose: toward friendly lines, a recovery site, or a populated area where contact is safe.

**9.4. Contact with people.** Contact with local people is a risk and a resource. Observe before approaching: watch what they eat, wear, and avoid. Approach openly but cautiously, show respect for customs, and never take food or shelter by force — goodwill may be the difference between recovery and capture. In occupied territory, assume some locals may report you; balance the need for help against the risk of betrayal.

**9.5. Survival in man-made hazards.** Nuclear, biological, and chemical hazards demand immediate protective action: find shelter (an existing structure improved against fallout, or a trench/foxhole), limit exposure time, maximize distance from the source, and use shielding — protection depends on time, distance, and shielding. Avoid detection and capture while sheltering; decontaminate by washing with water and removing contaminated clothing; treat contaminated water as undrinkable until purified. → `references/environments-and-signaling.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a survival question from FM 21-76, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (water quantities, purification doses and wait times, boil times), then the cautions. State the priority order explicitly — what kills first in this environment, right now — and name the environment the advice belongs to, since desert, tropical, cold, and sea priorities differ. Where current Army doctrine (ATP 3-50.21) differs from FM 21-76, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Will first.** The refusal to give up is the decisive factor; skills practiced in training become confidence in isolation.
2. **Priority by what kills fastest.** Medicine first, then protection (fire, shelter, clothing), then sustenance (water, food), then navigation — reassessed constantly as the environment changes.
3. **Purify all water.** Assume every surface source is contaminated; boiling is the safest method.
4. **Never eat an unknown plant whole.** One part, one plant, more than 24 hours to test; mushrooms never.
5. **Signal only when ready to be found.** In hostile areas, a signal is as visible to the enemy as to rescue.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-50.21 (2018) supersedes FM 21-76 where they conflict; FM 21-76 is the deep field reference, not the authority.
