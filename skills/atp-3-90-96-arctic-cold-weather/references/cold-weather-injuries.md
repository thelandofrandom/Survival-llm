# Cold-Weather Injuries — ATP 3-90.96 Appendix D

Distilled from ATP 3-90.96, Appendix D and Chapter 1 (24 February 2025). Paraphrase with short quotes only; full text in `full-text.md`.

## The injuries (Table D-1)

### Hypothermia — core temperature below 95°F

| Stage | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| Mild | Shivering; lack of sound judgment; confusion, apathy, mild mental impairment; pale, cool skin; increased heart and respiratory rates; the "umbles" — fumbles, stumbles, tumbles, grumbles, mumbles |
| Moderate | Uncontrollable shivering; worsening of the "umbles"; increased confusion; increased heart and respiratory rates; cold and pale skin |
| Severe | Cessation of shivering; muscle rigidity; stupor progressing to unconsciousness; slow or nonpalpable pulse and respiration; cold, bluish skin |

### Frostbite — freezing of skin

- Numbness, pale/waxy skin, loss of feeling. Time to onset per Tables 1-4/1-5 (see `arctic-environment.md`).
- Removing gloves in subzero temperatures can cause frostbite in minutes or even seconds.
- Contact frostbite: bare skin on cold metal or fuel. Fuel handlers always wear gloves designed for handling petroleum products.

### Trench foot / immersion foot

- Nonfreezing cold injury from prolonged wet, cold feet. Can occur at temperatures at or below **50°F**.
- May occur at any point on the windchill chart, even when frostbite cannot.

### Chilblains

- Nonfreezing cold injury; red, itchy, swollen skin from repeated cold exposure.

### Snow blindness (solar keratitis)

- Scratchy, sandy feeling under the eyelids; red and watering eyes; headache.
- Prevention: issued sunglasses from the approved protective eyewear list. If sunglasses are unavailable, make glasses with slits from cardboard, thin wood, tree bark, or similar material.
- Treatment: blindfold with a dark cloth, reassure the patient, evacuate to a medical treatment facility.

## Treatment principles

### Hypothermia

- Change the environment from cold and wet to warm and dry.
- Replace damp clothing with dry clothing.
- Add a windproof or waterproof layer and/or place the casualty in shelter.
- Add extra insulation under and around the casualty.
- Provide food and warm liquids.
- Exercise mildly hypothermic patients.
- Package a moderately hypothermic casualty in a hypothermia wrap.
- **All cases of hypothermia are evacuated to a medical facility for treatment.**
- Preventing casualty hypothermia starts at the point of injury: insulation to treat and prevent hypothermia during tactical casualty combat care and advanced trauma care. Cold can induce hypothermia and exacerbate the treatment timeline.

### Frostbite

- Never rub frostbitten tissue. Never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze.
- Medical personnel can treat mild hypothermia and superficial frostbite effectively; deeper cases need evacuation.

## Prevention — the program (D-2 to D-4)

- Cold-weather injuries are always a possibility in cold environments. If allowed to develop, they can be debilitating, diminishing a unit's combat power. Prevention is practiced 24 hours a day.
- Preventive measures: adequate clothing; regular meals, hot drinks, and rest; dry clothing.
- **Attentive leadership is essential** to ensure troops have, and are using, available cold-weather clothing and equipment. Leaders continually check fitness and clothing/equipment condition and frequently rotate troops into warming tents/areas for relief from the cold.
- **Buddy system:** buddies monitor each other — eating properly, drinking adequate water, dressing warmly, equipment maintained and functional.
- **Rapid evacuation** of cold-weather casualties is essential to minimize long-term effects and save lives. Helicopter evacuation is preferred; ground evacuation always planned. Risks to patient, unit, and mission assessed.

## Layer management (Figure D-1)

- Insulation layers trap air; the windproof/waterproof outer layer blocks wind and wet.
- Add or remove layers to manage sweat — wet clothing loses insulation and accelerates heat loss.
- Sweat management is the core individual discipline: work hard enough to stay warm, not so hard you soak your layers.

## The historical warning

- **World War II, winter 1944–45:** total cold-weather injury cases on the European western front were more than **23,000 troops** — about an infantry division and a half of personnel. General Omar Bradley, commanding the 12th Army, assessed that cold-weather injuries had seriously crippled U.S. fighting strength in Europe.
- The injuries caught the force unprepared. The manual's prevention program exists so that does not happen again.
