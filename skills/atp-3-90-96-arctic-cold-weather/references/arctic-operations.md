# Arctic Operations — ATP 3-90.96

Distilled from ATP 3-90.96, Chapters 2–10 and Appendixes A–B (24 February 2025). Paraphrase with short quotes only; full text in `full-text.md`.

## Principles of Arctic operations (Chapter 2)

1. **Campaign mentality** — Arctic operations cannot be a single battle or short deployment. Distances, weather windows, and sustainment timelines force sequenced operations, pre-positioned supplies, and patience. Think in weeks and months.
2. **Cold-weather capability** — a unit is either cold-weather capable or it is not; the difference is training, equipment, and leadership. Units with superior cold-weather clothing sustain fewer injuries. Non-capable units need significant time and training before operating in the Arctic.
3. **Isolated endurance** — forces must operate cut off from resupply, communications, and support for extended periods. Built by carrying more, caching supplies, and training to function without the temperate logistics tail.
4. **Arctic task organizing** — forces task-organized specifically for Arctic operations: infantry, armor, aviation, engineers, sustainment, specialized cold-weather elements, with over-snow vehicles, skis, snowshoes, and cold-weather shelters.

## Planning and execution (Chapter 3)

- Environmental considerations first: weather, terrain, ice, darkness, season shape every course of action.
- COA development must use Arctic movement rates, sustainment consumption, and casualty rates — all worse than temperate planning factors.
- Offensive operations exploit mobility corridors (frozen rivers, ice, snow-packed routes) and the enemy's cold-weather limitations. Defensive operations exploit prepared positions, warming shelters, and knowledge of the ground.
- Maritime domain: amphibious operations, supporting arms, supply by water, riverine operations. Seaport access can be limited by sea ice.
- Air domain: rotary-wing, fixed-wing, UAS. Ice runways have thickness and condition requirements; ski landing areas need control elements.

## Command, control, information (Chapter 4)

- Leadership is hands-on: continual checks of troop fitness and clothing/equipment, warming rotations, buddy system enforcement.
- Mission command matters more because communications fail more often — subordinates act on intent when the radio does not work.
- Risk management is continuous: windchill, ice thickness, carbon monoxide, injury rates, vehicle reliability tracked as risks.
- Information systems: cold degrades radios, batteries, electronics; headquarters layout accounts for heating, power, shelter.

## Movement and maneuver (Chapter 5)

- **Infantry:** terrain and snow depth govern movement; cold/ice/snow affect weapons (lubrication, icing, brittle parts). Shorter bounds, warming halts, bulky clothing reduces mobility and increases fatigue. Many items are less efficient or even harmful if used improperly.
- **Armor:** deep snow, ice crossing, spring thaw, freeze-up, summer movement each impose different limits. Winter preparation, observation of fire, ammunition handling (ammunition tends to freeze in the wooden fuse protective ring), maintenance in extreme cold. Constant supervision to prevent frostbite; tank movement generates windchill.
- **Rotary-wing aviation:** attack, reconnaissance, security, air assault, air movement, aeromedical evacuation, C2 support, sustainment — all with cold-weather limits. Door gunners and crew chiefs face severe frostbite risk; crews check each other for frostbite after the aircraft departs.
- **Special operations:** civil affairs, direct action, special reconnaissance, unconventional warfare under the same cold-weather constraints, often in small isolated teams.

## Intelligence (Chapter 6) and fires (Chapter 7)

- Intelligence: plan/direct, collect/process, produce, disseminate/integrate — degraded collection (weather, darkness, sparse infrastructure), slower dissemination, premium on human and local knowledge.
- Fires: artillery movement, position areas, observation, survey, delivery of fire, smoke — frozen ground changes survey and position preparation; snow changes observation; cold affects ammunition and fuzes. Air defense faces the same constraints plus tracking icing aircraft in poor visibility.

## Sustainment (Chapter 8)

- Longer lines of communication, fewer routes, weather closures, higher consumption. Site selection considers shelter, heating, snow load, drainage, defense.
- Maintenance takes longer, needs heated facilities; expect substantial delays.
- **Food:** caloric requirement in extreme cold is **4,600 calories/day average for men, 3,150 for women**, depending on workload. Altitude/cold-weather pack adds ~**1,100 calories** of eat-on-the-move items (caffeinated gum, dried fruits, powdered carbohydrate beverages, energy bars). Unitized Group Ration arctic supplement adds ~**900 calories**. The body consumes food to counteract heat loss; fats and carbohydrates are quick energy.
- **Water:** requirement **3.5 to 5 quarts per day**. Bulk water in 5-gallon cans filled only three-quarters full (freezing expansion). Individuals can melt snow or ice for cooking/drinking water. Distribution priority: hot drink first, then personal canteens, then 5-gallon cans.
- Field services, distribution, contract support, general engineering — all harder and slower in the cold.
- Health service support: medical treatment, hospitalization, medical evacuation (helicopter preferred, ground always planned), medical logistics.

## Protection (Chapter 9)

- Survivability positions in snow/frozen ground (digging hard; snow and ice construction substitute); camouflage in a white environment — tracks in snow betray movement, dark objects stand out for miles.
- CBRN hazards behave differently in cold (persistency, detection, decontamination); electromagnetic protection; EOD support.
- Personnel recovery in the Arctic — same PR problem, harder by cold, distance, darkness. Detainee operations under Arctic constraints.
- Force health protection: combat and operational stress control, operational public health services.

## Training (Chapter 10)

- Sequenced: training objectives → preliminary training → winter training → summer training.
- Fundamentals first: clothing layer management, cold-weather bivouacking, equipment care.
- Emphasis: physical readiness for cold-weather demands; specialized training (over-snow movement, skiing, ice operations, cold-weather medical); officer and NCO training — Arctic operations punish units whose leaders do not understand cold-weather risk management.
- Units can reduce risks by conducting routine training on cold-weather fundamentals before a crisis occurs.
