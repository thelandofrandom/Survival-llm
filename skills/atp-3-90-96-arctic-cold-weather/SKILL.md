# ATP 3-90.96 Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations

## Purpose

ATP 3-90.96, *Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations* (24 February 2025, including Change 2), is the U.S. Army's doctrine for operating and fighting in the Arctic — published jointly with the Marine Corps as MCTP 12-10E. It supersedes MCRP 12-10A.4 (January 2011) and is approved for public release, distribution unlimited. The manual covers the whole problem in ten chapters: the Arctic environment, operational considerations, planning and executing Arctic operations, command and control, movement and maneuver, intelligence, fires, sustainment, protection, and training — plus appendixes on units of measure, mobility, ice, cold-weather injury identification and prevention, and unit measures.

The manual's governing idea is stated in its principles of Arctic operations (Chapter 2): Arctic operations demand a **campaign mentality**, a genuine **cold-weather capability**, **isolated endurance**, and deliberate **Arctic task organizing**. The Arctic is not temperate warfare with heavier coats. Cold, darkness, distance, and ice change what weapons, vehicles, aircraft, radios, and human beings can do — and a force that treats the Arctic as an inconvenience will lose more people to the environment than to the enemy. The historical warning is in Appendix D: in the winter of 1944–45, cold-weather injuries on the European western front totaled more than **23,000 troops** — about an infantry division and a half — and General Omar Bradley assessed that cold-weather injuries had seriously crippled U.S. fighting strength in Europe. The manual exists so that does not happen again. → `references/arctic-environment.md`

## 1. The Arctic Environment — What You Are Fighting

**1.1. Defining Arctic operations.** The Arctic is defined by latitude, climate, and conditions: extreme cold, prolonged darkness and prolonged daylight, permafrost, sea ice, snow and ice terrain, and vast distances with thin infrastructure. The manual distinguishes Arctic operations from ordinary cold-weather operations by the combination of all of these at once. The Arctic's significance is strategic — sea routes, resources, and great-power competition — which is why the Army must be able to operate there rather than merely survive there.

**1.2. Polar climates and terrain.** Terrain in the Arctic includes tundra, taiga (boreal forest), mountains, glaciers, muskeg, rivers, lakes, and sea ice. Each behaves differently under cold: permafrost makes digging difficult and drainage poor; muskeg swallows vehicles in thaw; rivers and lakes become highways when frozen and obstacles when thawing. Snow depth and density are the most critical parameters for mobility, followed by strength and layering of the snowpack. Terrain analysis in the Arctic must account for season — the same ground is a different battlefield in January, April, and July.

**1.3. Weather.** Arctic weather is characterized by extreme cold, high winds, blowing and drifting snow, ground blizzards, whiteout, and icing. A **ground blizzard** creates blizzard-like conditions without any precipitation: strong winds move unpacked snow forcefully through the air, severely limit visibility, can last for days, and can lead to whiteout. An attacker or defender with the wind at their back gains an advantage in a ground blizzard; the force facing the wind fights blind. Weather changes rapidly and forecasting is limited by sparse observation networks — commanders must plan for weather they cannot predict precisely.

**1.4. Seasons.** The Arctic year divides into winter (dark, cold, frozen), spring thaw and freeze-up transitions, summer (long daylight, thawed ground, insects, wet conditions), and autumn freeze-up. The transition seasons — freeze-up and spring thaw — are the most dangerous for movement: ice is unreliable, ground is impassable, and conditions change daily. **Ice thickness is a commander's critical information requirement**: it determines safe travel over frozen water, and leaders must pay particular attention during thaw and freeze. Failure to determine safe ice thickness for travel can result in vehicles and personnel breaking through.

**1.5. Wildlife.** Arctic wildlife — bears, moose, wolves, and others — is a real planning factor: a threat to personnel, a hazard to camps and food storage, and a consideration for movement and bivouac site selection.

**1.6. Windchill — the numbers.** Windchill dramatically amplifies cold. Movement in a vehicle, free-fall parachuting, and operations around helicopters all create wind, and any wind can exponentially increase windchill. The manual's Table 1-4 is the windchill chart (air temperature vs. wind speed in mph); Table 1-5 gives time to cheek frostbite in minutes:

| Frostbite danger | Time to frostbite (dry, exposed cheek skin) |
|---|---|
| Slight danger | Greater than 120 minutes |
| Increased danger | Less than 45 minutes |
| Great danger | Less than 5 minutes |

Frostbite does not occur when ambient air is above freezing — but trench foot and immersion foot may occur at any point on the chart. The Table 1-5 data are based on dry cheek skin of the most susceptible five percent of personnel; wet skin or exposed extremities such as fingers can significantly decrease the time. A concrete example from the table: at −20°F with a 25 mph wind, cheek frostbite can occur in about **7 minutes**; at −50°F with a 40 mph wind, in about **2 minutes**. Commanders must consider windchill for both safety and operations, and exposed skin checks are a leadership duty, not a personal preference.

## 2. Environmental Hazards — People, Equipment, Operations

**2.1. Hazards to personnel.** Cold-weather injuries dominate: hypothermia (cooling of the body's internal temperature below **95°F**), frostbite (freezing of skin), trench foot and immersion foot (which can occur at temperatures at or below **50°F**), chilblains, and snow blindness (solar keratitis). Cold affects electronics and batteries, impairs dexterity — removing gloves in subzero temperatures can cause frostbite in minutes or even seconds — and degrades judgment: the "umbles" (fumbles, stumbles, tumbles, grumbles, mumbles) are the early signs of hypothermia. Carbon monoxide poisoning is a killer in enclosed, heated shelters and vehicles. Dehydration is common because the thirst response weakens in cold weather.

**2.2. Hazards to equipment.** Cold thickens lubricants, embrittles metals and plastics, drains batteries, freezes fuel and ammunition (ammunition tends to freeze in the wooden fuse protective ring), ices optics and weapons, and makes vehicles hard to start and keep running. Contact frostbite occurs when bare skin touches cold metal or fuel — fuel handlers must always wear gloves designed for handling petroleum products.

**2.3. Hazards to operations.** Reduced mobility, reduced visibility, reduced communications range, longer task times for every job, and greater sustainment demands. Everything takes longer in the cold; plans that assume temperate timelines fail.

## 3. Operational Considerations — The Principles (Chapter 2)

**3.1. PMESII-PT considerations.** The manual works through political, military, economic, social, information, infrastructure, physical environment, and time considerations for the Arctic, then the competition continuum: competition, crisis, and armed conflict in the Arctic each pose different demands and constraints.

**3.2. Campaign mentality.** Arctic operations cannot be conducted as a single battle or a short deployment. Distances, weather windows, and sustainment timelines force a campaign approach: sequenced operations, pre-positioned supplies, and patience. Units must think in weeks and months, not hours and days.

**3.3. Cold-weather capability.** A unit is either cold-weather capable or it is not, and the difference is training, equipment, and leadership — not willpower. Units with superior cold-weather clothing sustain fewer injuries. Non-cold-weather-capable units need significant time and training before they can operate in the Arctic at all. Fundamental training includes clothing layer management, cold-weather bivouacking, and equipment care.

**3.4. Isolated endurance.** Arctic forces must be able to operate isolated — cut off from resupply, communications, and support — for extended periods. Endurance is built by carrying more, caching supplies, and training to function without the logistics tail temperate operations assume.

**3.5. Arctic task organizing.** Forces are task-organized specifically for Arctic operations: the right mix of infantry, armor, aviation, engineers, sustainment, and specialized cold-weather elements, with the right equipment (over-snow vehicles, skis, snowshoes, cold-weather shelters) for the mission and season.

## 4. Planning and Executing Arctic Operations (Chapter 3)

**4.1. Planning.** Environmental considerations come first: weather, terrain, ice, darkness, and season shape every course of action. Course-of-action development must account for the movement rates, sustainment consumption, and casualty rates the Arctic imposes — all worse than temperate planning factors.

**4.2. Forms of maneuver and offensive/defensive operations.** Offensive operations in the Arctic exploit mobility corridors (frozen rivers, ice, snow-packed routes) and the enemy's own cold-weather limitations; defensive operations exploit prepared positions, warming shelters, and the defender's knowledge of the ground. Enabling operations, stability operations, and defense support of civil authorities are all addressed, as are operations in the maritime domain (amphibious, supporting arms, supply by water, riverine) and the air domain (rotary-wing, fixed-wing, unmanned aircraft systems).

**4.3. Air domain.** Rotary-wing operations face icing, reduced lift in cold dense air (a mixed blessing — cold air helps lift but icing and visibility hurt), and maintenance burdens. Fixed-wing operations need prepared surfaces — ice runways have thickness and condition requirements — and ski landing areas need control elements. UAS operations are degraded by cold batteries and icing.

## 5. Command, Control, and Information (Chapter 4)

**5.1. Leadership and mission command.** Arctic leadership is hands-on: leaders continually check the fitness of troops and the adequacy of clothing and equipment, rotate troops into warming tents, and enforce the buddy system. Mission command matters more in the Arctic because communications fail more often — subordinates must be able to act on intent when the radio does not work.

**5.2. Risk management.** Cold-weather risk management is continuous: windchill, ice thickness, carbon monoxide, cold-weather injury rates, and vehicle reliability are all tracked as risks, not assumed away.

**5.3. Information systems.** Cold degrades radios, batteries, and electronics; headquarters layout must account for heating, power, and shelter. Informing domestic and international audiences and influencing foreign relevant actors are part of the information fight in a strategically visible region.

## 6. Movement and Maneuver (Chapter 5)

**6.1. Infantry.** Terrain and snow depth govern infantry movement; cold, ice, and snow affect weapons (lubrication, icing, brittle parts, reduced rates of fire if not managed). Tactical considerations include shorter bounds, warming halts, and the burden of cold-weather clothing and equipment — bulky clothing reduces mobility and increases fatigue, and many items are less efficient or even harmful if used improperly.

**6.2. Armor.** Deep snow, ice crossing, spring thaw, freeze-up, and summer movement each impose different limits on armor. Preparation for winter operations, observation of fire, ammunition handling, and maintenance in extreme cold are all specified. Constant supervision is necessary to prevent frostbite among crews, and the wind generated by tank movement adds windchill.

**6.3. Rotary-wing aviation.** Attack, reconnaissance, security, air assault, air movement, aeromedical evacuation, C2 support, and aviation sustainment are all addressed, with cold-weather limits on each. Door gunners and crew chiefs face severe frostbite risk; individuals check each other for frostbite after the aircraft departs.

**6.4. Special operations.** Civil affairs, direct action, special reconnaissance, and unconventional warfare in the Arctic environment, with the same cold-weather constraints applied to small, often isolated teams.

## 7. Intelligence and Fires (Chapters 6–7)

**7.1. Intelligence.** The intelligence process — plan and direct, collect and process, produce, disseminate and integrate — operates in the Arctic with degraded collection (weather, darkness, sparse infrastructure), slower dissemination, and a premium on human and local knowledge. Planners must account for the fact that Arctic intelligence takes longer and is less certain.

**7.2. Fires.** Artillery movement, position areas, observation, survey, delivery of fire, and smoke are all affected by cold, snow, and ice: frozen ground changes survey and position preparation, snow changes observation, and cold affects ammunition and fuzes. Air defense artillery faces the same constraints plus the difficulty of tracking low, slow, or icing aircraft in poor visibility.

## 8. Sustainment (Chapter 8) — The Arctic Eats Logistics

**8.1. Sustainment planning and sites.** Sustainment planning must account for longer lines of communication, fewer routes, weather closures, and higher consumption. Sustainment site selection and layout must consider shelter, heating, snow load, drainage, and defense.

**8.2. Maintenance and transportation.** Cold-weather maintenance takes longer and needs heated facilities or shelters; planners should expect substantial delays common with cold-weather maintenance. Transportation over snow and ice needs over-snow vehicles, and movement rates are far below temperate norms. Appendix B covers mobility considerations in detail.

**8.3. Supply — food and water.** The caloric requirement in extreme cold is **4,600 calories per day on average for men and 3,150 for women**, depending on workload. The altitude/cold-weather pack supplements rations with about **1,100 calories** of eat-on-the-move items (caffeinated gum, dried fruits, powdered carbohydrate beverages, energy bars); the Unitized Group Ration series has an arctic supplement adding about **900 calories**. The body consumes food to counteract heat loss, and requires increased calories during cold exposure; fats and carbohydrates are quick energy-producing foods. Water requirement is **3.5 to 5 quarts per day**. Bulk water is transported in 5-gallon cans filled only three-quarters full (to allow for freezing expansion); individuals can furnish cooking and drinking water by melting snow or ice. Priority in water distribution: a hot drink first, then personal canteens, then the 5-gallon cans.

**8.4. Field services, distribution, contract support, engineering.** Field services (laundry, bath, mortuary, food service) are harder and slower in the cold; distribution is constrained by weather and routes; operational contract support and general engineering support must be planned for Arctic conditions.

**8.5. Health service support.** Medical treatment, hospitalization, medical evacuation, and medical logistics in the Arctic. Helicopter evacuation is the preferred method of casualty transport, but ground evacuation must always be planned. Preventing casualty hypothermia starts at the point of injury: insulation to treat and prevent hypothermia during tactical casualty combat care and advanced trauma care. Medical personnel can treat mild hypothermia and superficial frostbite effectively — but evacuation of all hypothermia cases to a medical facility is required, and cold can induce hypothermia that exacerbates treatment timelines.

## 9. Protection and Force Health (Chapter 9)

**9.1. Survivability and camouflage.** Survivability positions in snow and frozen ground (digging is hard; snow positions and ice construction substitute); camouflage and concealment in a white environment, where tracks in snow betray movement and dark objects stand out for miles.

**9.2. CBRN, electromagnetic protection, EOD.** Chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear hazards behave differently in cold (persistency, detection, decontamination); electromagnetic protection and explosive ordnance disposal support are addressed.

**9.3. Personnel recovery and detainee operations.** Personnel recovery in the Arctic — the same PR problem as elsewhere, made harder by cold, distance, and darkness — and detainee operations under Arctic constraints.

**9.4. Force health protection.** Combat and operational stress control, operational public health services, and cold-weather injury prevention as a command responsibility (see §10).

## 10. Cold-Weather Injuries — Identification, Prevention, Treatment (Appendix D)

**10.1. The injuries (Table D-1).** The common injuries and their recognition:

| Injury | Signs and symptoms |
|---|---|
| Hypothermia (core below 95°F) | Mild: shivering, lack of sound judgment, confusion, apathy, mild mental impairment, pale cool skin, increased heart/respiratory rates, the "umbles" (fumbles, stumbles, tumbles, grumbles, mumbles). Moderate: uncontrollable shivering, worsening "umbles," increased confusion, cold pale skin. Severe: cessation of shivering, muscle rigidity, stupor progressing to unconsciousness, slow or nonpalpable pulse and respiration, cold bluish skin |
| Frostbite | Freezing of skin; numbness, pale/waxy skin, loss of feeling; time to onset per Tables 1-4/1-5 |
| Trench foot / immersion foot | Can occur at or below 50°F; prolonged wet, cold feet; numbness, swelling, pain |
| Chilblains | Nonfreezing cold injury; red, itchy, swollen skin |
| Snow blindness (solar keratitis) | Scratchy, sandy feeling under the eyelids; red, watering eyes; headache |

**10.2. Treatment principles.** Hypothermia: change the environment from cold and wet to warm and dry; replace damp clothing with dry clothing; add a windproof or waterproof layer and/or place the casualty in shelter; add extra insulation under and around the casualty; provide food and warm liquids; exercise mildly hypothermic patients; package a moderately hypothermic casualty in a hypothermia wrap. **All cases of hypothermia are evacuated to a medical facility.** Snow blindness: blindfold with a dark cloth, reassure the patient, evacuate to a medical treatment facility. Never rub frostbitten tissue; never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze.

**10.3. Prevention — the leader's job.** Cold-weather injuries are always a possibility in cold environments, and if allowed to develop they can be debilitating, diminishing a unit's combat power. Prevention is 24 hours a day: adequate clothing, regular meals, hot drinks, rest, dry clothing. Attentive leadership is essential — leaders ensure troops have and are using available cold-weather clothing and equipment, frequently rotate troops into warming tents or areas, and continually check fitness and clothing. The **buddy system** reinforces leadership: buddies monitor each other for eating properly, drinking adequate water, dressing warmly, and equipment condition. Rapid evacuation of cold-weather casualties is essential to minimize long-term effects; helicopter evacuation is preferred, ground evacuation always planned, and risks to patient, unit, and mission assessed.

**10.4. Layer management.** Figure D-1 (Appendix D) gives layer management of cold-weather clothing: insulation layers trap air, the windproof/waterproof outer layer blocks wind and wet, and layers are added or removed to manage sweat — because wet clothing loses insulation and accelerates heat loss. Sweat management is the core discipline: work hard enough to stay warm, not so hard you soak your layers.

## 11. Training for Arctic Operations (Chapter 10)

**11.1. Developing Arctic training plans.** Training objectives, preliminary training, winter training, and summer training are sequenced so units build competence before they face the Arctic for real. Preliminary training covers the fundamentals — clothing layer management, cold-weather bivouacking, equipment care — before field training.

**11.2. Other training emphasis.** Physical readiness training for cold-weather demands; specialized training (over-snow movement, skiing, ice operations, cold-weather medical); officer and NCO training, because Arctic operations punish units whose leaders do not understand cold-weather risk management. The manual is blunt that units without superior cold-weather training will take casualties from the environment before they ever meet the enemy.

## 12. Appendixes — The Working Tools

- **Appendix A — Unit of Measure.** Conversion tables for Arctic operations (temperature, distance, weight) so joint and multinational forces use common figures.
- **Appendix B — Mobility Considerations.** How snow depth, density, strength, and layering affect movement; vehicle, ski, and snowshoe mobility; route selection.
- **Appendix C — Ice Considerations.** Calculating safe ice thickness for travel and for aircraft (including ice runways), ice quality and temperature factors, and the bearing-capacity limits of ice. Ice thickness is only one factor in bearing capacity — temperature, quality, and loading matter too.
- **Appendix D — Cold Weather Injury Identification and Prevention.** The injury tables, prevention program, and the World War II vignette.
- **Appendix E and following — Unit Measures.** Checklists and measures units use to assess and sustain cold-weather readiness (including the calorie and water figures in §8.3).

## Output Contract

When asked about Arctic or extreme cold weather operations, answer from this publication's structure: (1) describe the environment — terrain, weather, seasons, windchill, and ice — with the numbers (frostbite times, 95°F hypothermia threshold, 50°F trench-foot threshold); (2) apply the principles — campaign mentality, cold-weather capability, isolated endurance, Arctic task organizing; (3) walk the warfighting functions as the manual does (maneuver, fires, sustainment, protection, C2, intelligence); (4) give the sustainment numbers (4,600/3,150 calories, 3.5–5 quarts of water); (5) give the cold-weather injury recognition, prevention, and treatment from Appendix D, with the buddy system and warming rotations as leadership duties; (6) close with training. Distinguish this Army publication from its Marine Corps twin (MCTP 12-10E) — same joint text, different Service framing — and note that the two skills in this vault share the source text and should be cross-checked against each other.

## Operating Rules

1. **The environment is the first enemy.** Plan for cold, wind, darkness, and ice before planning for the human enemy.
2. **Know the windchill and the frostbite clock.** At −20°F with a 25 mph wind, exposed cheek skin can frostbite in ~7 minutes; at −50°F with 40 mph wind, ~2 minutes. Check exposed skin constantly.
3. **Ice thickness is a commander's critical information requirement.** Never cross frozen water on assumption; verify thickness, temperature, and quality — especially in freeze-up and thaw.
4. **Manage sweat like ammunition.** Layer management is the core individual discipline; wet clothing kills.
5. **Feed and water the force like a weapon system.** 4,600 calories (men) / 3,150 (women) per day; 3.5–5 quarts of water; hot drinks first.
6. **The buddy system is a control measure, not a suggestion.** Buddies check eating, drinking, dressing, and equipment — and leaders check the buddies.
7. **Evacuate every hypothermia case.** Mild hypothermia gets first aid and still goes to a medical facility; never thaw a frozen limb that may refreeze.
8. **Train before you deploy.** Units without cold-weather fundamentals take casualties from the environment before contact.
