# FM 21-76-1 Survival, Evasion, and Recovery

## Purpose

FM 21-76-1, *Survival, Evasion, and Recovery — Multiservice Procedures for Survival, Evasion, and Recovery* (Air Land Sea Application Center, June 1999, 107 pages), issued jointly as FM 21-76-1, MCRP 3-02H, NWP 3-50.3, and AFTTP(I) 3-2.26. It is the pocket procedures manual for all four services — the condensed, do-this-now companion to the big survival manuals (FM 21-76 and its Marine twin MCRP 3-02F). The manual itself recommends inclusion in the aviator's survival vest: it is meant to be carried, not shelved.

Where FM 21-76 teaches survival in depth, FM 21-76-1 gives procedures in checklist form across nine chapters: evasion, navigation, radio communications and signaling, recovery, medical, personal protection, water, food, and induced conditions (nuclear, biological, chemical). It is the manual to reach for when someone is down, isolated, and needs the next action — not the theory behind it. It does not cover resistance or escape in captivity in depth; for the full SERE system, see the SERE skill (AFH 10-644) and the survival-evasion-recovery skill (MCRP 3-02H — note that MCRP 3-02H is the Marine Corps designation of this same multiservice publication, so the two skills cover the same source from different angles: this one is the chapter-by-chapter procedures reference).

The manual opens with a **Quick Reference Checklist — Decide to Survive!** The SURVIVAL acronym here reads:

- **S — Size up the situation,** surroundings, physical condition, equipment.
- **U — Use all your senses.**
- **R — Remember where you are.**
- **V — Vanquish fear and panic.**
- **I — Improvise.**
- **V — Value living.**
- **A — Act like the natives.**
- **L — Live by your wits, but for now, learn basic skills.**

## 1. Evasion (Chapter I)

**1.1. Immediate actions.** The checklist's first block is survival actions on the ground: find a hole-up site, reassess the situation, treat injuries, inventory equipment, review the plan of action, establish priorities, determine current location, improve camouflage, focus thoughts on the task at hand, execute the plan — and stay flexible. Decisions in the first minutes set the pattern for everything after.

**1.2. The hole-up site.** A hole-up site is where you stop, hide, recover, and plan. On arrival: reassess the situation; treat injuries, then inventory equipment; review the plan of action and establish priorities; determine your current location; improve camouflage; focus thoughts on the task at hand; execute the plan of action — and stay flexible. A good hole-up site gives concealment from ground and air, a safe distance from enemy positions and lines of communication, and room to rest without being found.

**1.3. Concealment.** Select a place of concealment providing adequate concealment from ground and air, and a safe distance from enemy positions and lines of communication. Improve it with natural materials; break up your outline; avoid shiny objects, strong smells, and skyline positions. Movement to and from the site must not leave a visible trail — enter and leave by different routes, and do not disturb vegetation in a way that marks the site.

**1.4. Shelters.** Shelters in evasion are low, small, and inconspicuous — a poncho shelter or a natural-material hide that keeps you dry and warm without advertising your position. Build only what you need, keep the profile low, and be ready to abandon it fast. Camouflage the shelter with local materials; a shelter that can be seen from the air is worse than no shelter.

**1.5. Movement.** Evasion movement is slow, deliberate, and mostly at night. Use evasion movement techniques: move from concealment to concealment, avoid trails, roads, and open ground, cross danger areas quickly, and stop frequently to listen and observe. Never move in a straight line toward your objective for long — vary direction, use terrain for cover, and never silhouette yourself. If compromised, break contact, move away fast, and find a new hole-up site before the enemy can fix your position. → `references/evasion-and-navigation.md`

## 2. Navigation (Chapter II)

**2.1. Stay or move?** The first navigation decision is whether to move at all. Stay if you are injured, if your position is known to friendly forces, if movement would expose you to the enemy, or if you lack the means to navigate. Move if your position is compromised, if water or food is unavailable, if friendly lines are reachable, or if a recovery site is attainable. A wrong move is worse than no move.

**2.2. Navigation and position determination.** Navigate by compass first — the compass is the primary tool and should be carried on the body at all times. Without a compass: the sun (rises roughly east, sets roughly west), the stick-and-shadow method (mark a shadow tip, wait, mark again — the line runs west to east), the watch method (hour hand at the sun, halfway to 12 o'clock is south in the Northern Hemisphere), and the stars (Polaris in the north; the Southern Cross in the south). Determine your position by terrain association, resection, and dead reckoning — and keep a running estimate of where you are even when you are not moving.

**2.3. Travel considerations.** Plan routes that use cover and concealment; avoid built-up areas, roads, and bridges in hostile territory. River travel can be fast and quiet but exposes you and limits escape routes — use it only when the risk is acceptable. Ice and snow travel demands attention to footing, avalanche terrain, and cold injury; mountain hazards include falls, rockfall, and sudden weather; summer hazards include heat and dehydration; dry climates demand water discipline and night movement. → `references/evasion-and-navigation.md`

## 3. Radio Communications and Signaling (Chapter III)

**3.1. Radio procedure.** When you have a radio, use it correctly and sparingly — every transmission can be located. Follow the procedures in your plan and orders: authenticate, report your situation concisely, and follow recovery-force instructions. Keep transmissions short; listen more than you talk. If you have no radio, visual and sound signals are your only means of effecting recovery.

**3.2. Signaling.** The signaling mirror is the most effective daytime visual signal; smoke by day, fire by night; ground-to-air signals laid out in open ground in large, contrasting symbols; whistle blasts in groups of three. Signal only when you are ready to be found and when the risk is acceptable — in hostile areas a signal is as visible to the enemy as to rescue. Prepare signaling devices before you need them, and keep at least one on your body.

**3.3. What to report.** Be prepared to report enemy activity in the recovery area, recovery-site characteristics (slope, obstacles, size), the number in your party and the medical situation, and the signal devices you have available. → `references/radio-signaling-recovery.md`

## 4. Recovery (Chapter IV)

**4.1. Prepare to be recovered.** Recovery is a coordinated act between you and the recovery force. Be prepared to authenticate as directed in applicable plans and orders, and follow recovery-force instructions. Be prepared to report enemy activity in the recovery area, site characteristics, the number in your party and the medical situation, and your signal devices. If you have no radio, a ground-to-air signal may be your only means to effect recovery.

**4.2. Site selection.** Locate an area for landing pick-up if practical — approximately **150 feet in diameter, free of obstructions, flat and level**. Assess evidence of human activity at or near the site (in combat). Locate several concealment sites around the area (in combat). Plan several tactical entry and exit routes (in combat).

**4.3. Site preparation and procedures.** Pack and secure all equipment. Prepare signaling devices (use as directed or as briefed). Mentally review recovery methods — aircraft, ground, boat. Follow the recovery force's instructions exactly at the moment of pickup: approach the aircraft only as directed, keep clear of rotors and propellers, and do not move toward the aircraft until told to. After physical custody, follow the recovery force's directions — the evasion is over; the reintegration process begins.

**4.4. Authentication.** Authentication is how the recovery force confirms you are who you claim to be — and how you confirm they are friendly. Use the authentication procedures in your plan and orders (challenge and reply, codes, or other means as briefed). Never authenticate to an unknown party, and never board an aircraft or vehicle that has not authenticated itself. → `references/radio-signaling-recovery.md`

## 5. Medical (Chapter V)

**5.1. Assess and treat.** Assess your medical condition; treat as necessary. The priorities are the same as in every survival manual: airway and breathing, severe bleeding, shock. Treat yourself first — you cannot help anyone else if you are incapacitated — then treat others. Keep wounds clean and covered; keep the casualty warm; improvise splints and dressings from available material.

**5.2. Prevention is the treatment.** In isolation, a small problem becomes a fatal one. Stay hydrated, stay warm and dry, keep feet sound, eat what you can, avoid injury. Treat diarrhea and dysentery early — they accelerate dehydration and are among the most dangerous field illnesses. Boil all water; treat wounds promptly; protect against insects and parasites.

**5.3. Induced conditions and injury.** Chapter IX covers the medical side of nuclear, biological, and chemical exposure: recognize symptoms, protect yourself, decontaminate, and seek shelter. Radiation protection depends on time of exposure, distance from the source, and shielding. → `references/medical-water-food-nbc.md`

## 6. Personal Protection (Chapter VI)

**6.1. Priorities and shelter.** Establish priorities and work them in order — the plan of action reviewed at the hole-up site sets the sequence. Personal protection means shelter, clothing, and fire: protect against cold, heat, sun, wind, rain, and insects. Improvise goggles against snow blindness and dust; protect the eyes, the head, and the feet first — feet carry you out, and foot injuries end evasion.

**6.2. Cold, heat, and sun.** Prevent cold injury by layering, staying dry, and insulating from the ground. Prevent heat injury by resting in shade during the day, moving at night, covering the body, and drinking on schedule. Prevent sunburn and snow blindness with improvised coverings — cloth, mud, or charcoal on exposed skin, and slit-goggles cut from any opaque material.

**6.3. Insects and parasites.** Insects carry disease and destroy rest. Use clothing as a barrier, improvise repellent from smoke and mud, and keep sleeping areas clear of standing water and animal sign. Check the body daily for ticks and leeches; remove them carefully and treat the bite.

## 7. Water (Chapter VII)

**7.1. Need and sources.** The body needs 2–3 liters a day at moderate temperature and exertion, more in heat, cold, activity, or illness. Find water by following animal trails downhill, watching birds at dawn and dusk, noting green vegetation, and watching insects. Collect rain, dew, transpiration bags, solar stills, green bamboo, vines (cut high first, then low — avoid milky or bitter sap), and banana stumps.

**7.2. Purification.** Purify all water from lakes, ponds, swamps, springs, and streams. Methods: purification tablets (follow directions); 5 drops of 2% tincture of iodine per quart of clear water, 10 drops if cloudy or cold, wait 30 minutes; boiling — rolling boil 1 minute at sea level, longer at altitude. Filter muddy water through sand, charcoal, or cloth first — filtration only clears water; it does not purify it. Never drink urine, alcohol, or seawater. → `references/medical-water-food-nbc.md`

## 8. Food (Chapter VIII)

**8.1. Snares and traps.** Snares work while unattended — that is their advantage. Place them on trails leading to water, feeding, and bedding areas, and at the mouth of dens. Construction: use materials that will not break under the strain of holding an animal; with wire use a figure-8 (locking) loop — once tightened, the wire locks in place, preventing reopening and the animal's escape. Check snares regularly; a dead animal left too long is lost to scavengers and spoilage.

**8.2. Fishing and gathering.** Fish with line and hooks from the kit, improvised hooks, traps, and spears. Gather plants only where identification is certain — and apply the edibility test to anything unknown.

**8.3. The plant edibility test.** Before testing, ensure there are enough plants to make testing worth the time — each part of a plant (roots, leaves, stems, bark) requires more than 24 hours to test. Test only one part of one plant at a time. Eating large portions of plant food on an empty stomach may cause diarrhea, nausea, or cramps — even after testing safe, eat in moderation. Avoid plants with: milky sap (dandelion has milky sap but is safe to eat and easily recognizable); spines, fine hairs, and thorns (prickly pear and thistles are exceptions; bracken fern fiddleheads also violate this guideline); mushrooms and fungus; umbrella-shaped flowers (hemlock is eliminated by this rule). The step-by-step test — skin, lip, tongue, chew-and-spit, small swallow, measured portion, with waiting periods — is in `references/medical-water-food-nbc.md`.

## 9. Induced Conditions (Chapter IX)

**9.1. Nuclear.** Radiation protection depends on **time of exposure, distance from the source, and shielding**. Find protective shelter immediately. Gather all equipment for survival (time permitting). Avoid detection and capture. Seek existing shelter that may be improved; if no shelter is available, dig a trench or foxhole. Stay sheltered until the fallout threat passes or until movement is ordered — the first hours after a detonation are the most dangerous.

**9.2. Biological.** Biological hazards are invisible and slow to show. Avoid areas of obvious contamination, dead animals, and unusual disease; protect the airway and skin; wash with water and soap when available; boil all water; cook all food thoroughly. Report symptoms early — early treatment is the only treatment that works reliably.

**9.3. Chemical.** Chemical agents attack through skin contact and inhalation. Move upwind and uphill from contamination; avoid low ground and depressions where agents settle; remove contaminated clothing and wash exposed skin with water; do not eat, drink, or smoke in a contaminated area. Use any available protective equipment and seek decontamination at the earliest opportunity. → `references/medical-water-food-nbc.md`

## Output Contract

When answering from FM 21-76-1, give the procedure as a checklist, in the order the manual gives it, with the numbers that make it work (150-foot pickup diameter, iodine doses and wait times, the 24-hour test rule). This manual is a procedures reference, not a textbook — answers should read like the quick-reference checklist: short, ordered, actionable. Say which chapter the procedure comes from. Where the deeper manual (FM 21-76) or current doctrine (ATP 3-50.21) adds detail or supersedes a point, say so and point to it.

## Operating Rules

1. **Decide to survive.** The checklist is the first action — size up, use all senses, remember where you are, vanquish fear and panic, improvise, value living, act like the natives, live by your wits.
2. **Hole up before you move.** Reassess, treat injuries, inventory equipment, review the plan, determine location, improve camouflage — then execute, and stay flexible.
3. **Security first in hostile areas.** Concealment from ground and air, safe distance from enemy positions, no visible trail to the hole-up site.
4. **Authenticate before recovery.** Never authenticate to an unknown party; never board an aircraft or vehicle that has not authenticated itself.
5. **One part, one plant, 24 hours.** The edibility test is slow by design; mushrooms never.
6. **Time, distance, shielding.** The three levers against radiation — and the first move is always shelter, immediately.
