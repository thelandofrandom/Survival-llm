# Austere Care — ST 31-91B

> Dated-manual note: recognition, wound care, splinting, burn estimation, environmental-injury care, sanitation, and water purification are durable. Drug doses, CPR, airway procedures, and shock/hemorrhage treatment are 1982 — TCCC (ATP 4-02.11, 2026) governs those.

## Shock — Recognition Only

- Signs: restlessness, pale/cool/clammy skin, rapid weak pulse, falling blood pressure, reduced urine output, altered consciousness.
- Hemorrhagic shock is the field-relevant form. **Treatment follows modern TCCC** — tourniquets, wound packing, junctional control — not this 1982 chapter.

## Wound Care (Chapter 16 Principles)

- Control bleeding (TCCC methods), irrigate clean, remove foreign material, dress and cover.
- **Never close a war wound primarily.** Contaminated wounds are left open for delayed primary closure.
- Debride dead tissue. Tetanus prophylaxis for dirty wounds. Evacuate deep, contaminated, or joint-penetrating wounds.
- Infection signs: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, increasing pain, fever, pus. A wound that gets worse after day 2–3 is infected until proven otherwise.

## Fractures (Chapter 9)

- Diagnose: pain, deformity, crepitus, loss of function, swelling.
- Splint the joint above and below the fracture; pad well; check circulation before and after splinting.
- Splint as found — do not attempt reduction unless trained and authorized. Open fractures and fractures with neurovascular compromise are evacuation priorities.

## Burns and Blast (Chapter 10)

**Rule of Nines:** head 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, perineum 1%.

| Depth | Appearance | Pain |
|---|---|---|
| First-degree | Red, dry | Painful |
| Second-degree | Blistered, moist | Very painful |
| Third-degree | White/charred, dry | Insensate (nerve endings destroyed) |

- Field care: stop the burning, cool with clean water, cover with a clean dry dressing, keep the patient warm (burn patients lose heat fast), oral fluids if conscious and not vomiting.
- Evacuate: face, hands, genitalia, circumferential burns, or >10% body surface in the field setting.
- **Blast injuries:** hidden killers are blast lung and ruptured eardrums plus penetrating fragments. Any blast casualty with breathing difficulty or altered consciousness is an immediate evacuation.

## Heat Injuries (Chapter 11)

| Condition | Signs | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Heat cramps | Painful muscle spasms in the heat | Rest in shade, salt/fluids |
| Heat exhaustion | Weakness, nausea, headache, heavy sweating, near-normal temperature | Rest in shade, cool, fluids |
| Heat stroke | Altered consciousness, high temperature, hot dry (or sweaty) skin | **Cool as rapidly as possible, evacuate immediately** — life-threatening |

- Prevention: curtail activity as temperature/humidity rise (the handbook's heat categories), acclimatize gradually, enforce water discipline.
- Cooling: water, fanning, ice to neck/groin/armpits if available. **Cool first — never delay cooling to transport.**

## Cold Injuries (Chapter 11)

- **Frostbite:** freezing of tissue (fingers, toes, nose, ears). Rewarm slowly in warm (not hot) water; never rub frozen tissue; **never rewarm if refreezing is likely** — thaw–refreeze is worse than staying frozen.
- **Trench/immersion foot:** prolonged wet cold without freezing. Keep feet dry, change socks, rewarm gradually.
- **Hypothermia:** confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination; a patient who stops shivering is in trouble. Keep dry, insulate from the ground, warm inside (warm fluids if conscious) and outside.
- Wind-chill chart in the handbook: wind accelerates heat loss in both wet and cold conditions. Starvation, exhaustion, and drugs affecting circulation/sweating predispose to cold injury.

## Bites and Stings (Chapter 12)

- **Snake bite:** keep victim calm and still; immobilize the limb at heart level; remove rings/watches before swelling; do NOT cut, suck, or tourniquet; evacuate for antivenin (a hospital treatment). Identify the snake only if safe; never delay evacuation to hunt it.
- **Insect stings:** anaphylaxis is the emergency — airway swelling, wheeze, collapse. Treat per current anaphylaxis guidance; evacuate.
- **Animal bites:** rabies risk — wash thoroughly, evacuate for rabies evaluation.

## Dental Emergencies (Chapter 19)

- Dental pain disables a man in the field. Temporary fillings, extraction indications, and dental infections are covered in detail.
- Field rule: relieve pain, control infection, evacuate for definitive care.

## Preventive Medicine and Field Sanitation (Chapter 20)

**Water purification (handbook directions):** iodine tablets — one tablet per quart canteen of clear water, two if cloudy; warning that one tablet may not guarantee complete destruction of Giardia cysts in clear, very cold water. Boiling remains the most reliable method; modern filtration/chlorine dioxide are current alternatives. Principle: treat **all** water from natural sources.

**The four sanitation rules:**
1. Latrines 200 feet from water and away from shelter.
2. Wash hands after the latrine and before eating.
3. Wash face, underarms, groin, and feet when a full bath is impossible.
4. Clean eating utensils after each meal.

**Daily preventive checklist:** water checked and treated; food protected from flies/contamination; latrines sited and maintained; insect control (nets, repellent, clothing); waste/garbage disposed of; unit health monitored daily — the first case of diarrhea, fever, or rash is the warning; the outbreak is the failure.

## Veterinary and Primitive Medicine (Chapters 21–22)

- **Veterinary:** care of pack animals, livestock, and working dogs — the medic may be the only animal care available. Same S.O.A.P. logic, adapted.
- **Primitive medicine:** medicine with local materials when supplies run out — the survivalist's chapter. Its value is the mindset: improvise with what the environment provides, keep the patient clean, warm, hydrated, and fed, and evacuate when possible.
