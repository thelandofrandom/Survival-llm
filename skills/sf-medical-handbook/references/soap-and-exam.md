# S.O.A.P. and the Physical Exam — ST 31-91B

> Dated-manual note: this reference covers the handbook's diagnostic method and recognition patterns — the durable part. Drug doses, CPR, and hemorrhage/shock treatment in ST 31-91B are 1982 and superseded; TCCC (ATP 4-02.11, 2026) governs those.

## The S.O.A.P. Method

| Step | What it is | Field discipline |
|---|---|---|
| **S — Subjective** | Patient's history, in their own words | Let them talk uninterrupted; for many diagnoses the history *is* the diagnosis |
| **O — Objective** | Vital signs, exam findings, lab if available | Head to toe, systems in order, write it down |
| **A — Assessment** | Diagnosis or ranked differential | Name the worst plausible diagnosis, not just the likeliest |
| **P — Plan** | Treatment, evacuation decision, follow-up | Decide early whether the patient stays or goes |

## History-and-Physical Guide (Appendix E)

- Chief complaint, history of present illness, past history, medications, allergies, social/exposure history (travel, water, food, insects, contacts).
- Review of systems, head to toe. Vital signs every time: temperature, pulse, respirations, blood pressure.
- Document: a diagnosis you cannot read later is a diagnosis you never made.

## Normal Values (Appendix D)

Know the normals so abnormal means something: hemoglobin, hematocrit, white-cell count and differential, platelets, blood urea nitrogen, electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium), glucose. A white-cell count of 20,000 with a left shift means bacterial infection until proven otherwise; a falling hematocrit in a trauma patient means ongoing bleeding.

## Body-System Recognition Patterns (Chapter 1)

| System | Red-flag patterns |
|---|---|
| Integumentary (skin) | Rapidly spreading redness + fever = cellulitis, evacuate same day; fluctuant abscess = incise and drain; dirty wounds = tetanus prophylaxis |
| Musculoskeletal | Pain + deformity + crepitus + loss of function = fracture; splint joint above and below; check circulation before and after |
| Respiratory | Fever + productive cough + chest pain + consolidation signs (dullness, crackles) = pneumonia; severe dyspnea/cyanosis = oxygen + evacuate |
| Circulatory | Chest pain reliably shortened by nitroglycerin = angina; prolonged pain + sweating + nausea = infarction, evacuate; breathlessness + edema = heart failure, evacuate |
| Digestive | Blood in stool + fever + cramps = dysentery first; rigid abdomen = surgical abdomen, evacuate; jaundice + fever = hepatitis/malaria workup |
| Genitourinary | Burning urination + flank pain + fever = kidney infection, evacuate; retention = emergency |
| Nervous | Fever + stiff neck + altered consciousness = meningitis, evacuate immediately; new weakness or seizure = evacuate |
| Eye/ENT | Penetrating eye injury: never press, cover both eyes, evacuate; severe ear pain with fever = otitis, treat and watch |

## The Evacuation Decision

Ask three questions: (1) Can this kill or disable the patient if I am wrong? (2) Can I diagnose it with what I have? (3) Can I treat it with what I have? If any answer is no, evacuate. The handbook's preface: many treatments described *"would best be given in a hospital where a laboratory and special equipment are available."*
