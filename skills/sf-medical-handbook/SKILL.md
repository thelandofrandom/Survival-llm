# Special Forces Medical Handbook

## Purpose

ST 31-91B *U.S. Army Special Forces Medical Handbook* (U.S. Army Institute for Military Assistance, March 1982) is the ready reference written for Special Forces medics operating far from hospitals — 22 chapters covering body-system diagnosis, communicable disease, emergency surgery, burns, environmental injury, bites, poisoning, NBC, and preventive medicine, plus appendices of anatomical plates, lab procedures, and a drug-of-choice chart. It is for the austere-care provider: the person who must diagnose with hands, eyes, and a stethoscope, treat with what is in the aid bag, and decide who can wait and who must be evacuated now.

## ⚠️ DATED-MEDICINE WARNING — READ FIRST

**This manual is from March 1982. Much of its medicine is dated or superseded, and it must never be followed where it conflicts with current standards.**

- **TCCC wins on any conflict.** The current Tactical Combat Casualty Care standard is ATP 4-02.11 (2026). Where ST 31-91B and TCCC disagree — hemorrhage control, tourniquet use, airway management, fluid resuscitation, casualty movement — follow TCCC.
- **CPR and airway procedures in Chapter 3 are 1982-era.** Do not use them as your CPR reference; use current AHA/CoTCCC guidance. The handbook's own cricothyroidotomy note is a last-resort procedure, not a first-line skill.
- **Drug doses and drug choices are 1982.** Antibiotics, analgesics, and dosages in this book predate modern resistance patterns, modern formularies, and drugs that did not exist in 1982. Treat the drug-of-choice chart (Appendix G) as a historical artifact — never dose a patient from it.
- **What still holds:** the durable material this skill focuses on — the S.O.A.P. diagnostic method, the physical-exam sequence, recognition of disease patterns, field sanitation and water purification, environmental-injury recognition, wound care principles, splinting and fracture care, burn estimation, and the "know your limitations" ethic. The handbook's own preface states its rule: *"First thou shalt do no harm"* — and *"Know your limitations and do not exceed them."* Personnel with serious injuries or illnesses should be evacuated to a hospital if at all possible.

Use this skill for field diagnosis, recognition, austere care, and prevention. For any life-threatening bleeding, airway, or shock decision, use TCCC (ATP 4-02.11, 2026) instead.

## 1. The Diagnostic Method: S.O.A.P.

**1.1 The framework.** Every patient in this handbook is worked up the same way — S.O.A.P.:
- **S — Subjective:** what the patient tells you. History, in the patient's own words, uninterrupted. The handbook stresses that for many diagnoses (angina is its example), the history *is* the diagnosis — let the patient describe symptoms fully before you examine.
- **O — Objective:** what you find. Vital signs, physical exam, lab if available. Appendix E gives a full history-and-physical examination guide.
- **A — Assessment:** your diagnosis (or differential — the ranked list of what it could be).
- **P — Plan:** treatment, evacuation decision, and follow-up.

**1.2 The exam sequence.** Work head to toe, systems in order, and write it down. The handbook's Chapter 1 is organized by body system precisely so a medic can flip to the system with the symptoms: integumentary (skin), musculoskeletal, respiratory, circulatory, digestive, genitourinary, nervous, endocrine, eye/ear/nose/throat. Know the normal values (Appendix D: cellular components of blood, normal values, significance of blood tests) so abnormal means something when you see it.

**1.3 The evacuation question.** The handbook's preface is blunt: many treatments described *"would best be given in a hospital where a laboratory and special equipment are available."* The medic's hardest decision is not what to give — it is whether the patient stays or goes. Decide early, decide on the worst plausible diagnosis, and do not let treatment delay evacuation of a serious case.

## 2. Body Systems: Recognition Patterns

**2.1 Skin (integumentary).** The skin is the most examined organ in the field and the first to show systemic disease. The handbook covers pruritus, rashes, cellulitis, abscesses, fungal infections, and infestations. Key field rules: systemic antibiotics beat topical for spreading cellulitis (penicillin was the handbook's drug of choice, erythromycin second — see the dated warning before acting on any antibiotic choice); incise and drain a fluctuant abscess; tetanus prophylaxis for dirty wounds; and any rapidly spreading redness with fever is a same-day evacuation, not a "watch it" case.

**2.2 Musculoskeletal and orthopedics (Chapter 9).** Fractures are diagnosed by pain, deformity, crepitus, loss of function, and swelling; open fractures and any fracture with neurovascular compromise are evacuation priorities. Field splinting principles: splint the joint above and below the fracture, pad well, check circulation before and after, and never try to reduce a fracture you cannot manage — splint as found. Dislocations are covered with the same caution: the handbook's era allowed field reduction of some dislocations, but the modern rule is to splint and evacuate unless you are trained and authorized — do not attempt reductions from this 1982 text alone.

**2.3 Respiratory.** Pneumonia recognition: fever, productive cough, chest pain, consolidation signs on exam (dullness, bronchial breath sounds, crackles), and a high white-cell count. The handbook distinguishes lobar pneumonia, staphylococcal pneumonia, and aspiration. Severe pneumonia with cyanosis or marked dyspnea needs oxygen and evacuation — and the handbook's own note is that severe pneumonia needs hospital care, not field improvisation. Asthma, pleurisy, and pneumothorax are covered with the same pattern: recognize, stabilize, evacuate.

**2.4 Circulatory.** Angina versus myocardial infarction is the handbook's worked example of history-driven diagnosis: angina is reliably shortened by nitroglycerin; infarction is not, and comes with prolonged pain, sweating, nausea, and fear. Heart failure management in the handbook is 1982-era (diuretics, bed rest, sodium restriction) — recognize the syndrome (breathlessness, edema, fatigue) and evacuate; do not manage heart failure from this text.

**2.5 Digestive.** Acute gastroenteritis and food poisoning are the commonest field complaints; bacillary or amebic dysentery is the first consideration when there is blood in the stool with fever and cramps. Appendicitis, peptic ulcer, cholecystitis, and hepatitis are covered as "recognize and evacuate" diagnoses — the field role is early recognition and not missing the surgical abdomen.

**2.6 Nervous system, eye/ear/nose/throat.** Headache patterns, seizures, meningitis signs (fever, stiff neck, altered consciousness — evacuate immediately), eye injuries, ear infections, and dental pain. Eye injuries get their own caution: never press on a penetrating eye injury; cover both eyes and evacuate.

## 3. Communicable Disease (Chapter 2)

**3.1 The big picture.** The handbook opens Chapter 2 with a blunt fact: of all diseases afflicting mankind, parasites — especially malaria — cause the highest morbidity and mortality worldwide. For a medic in the tropics, infectious disease is the mission, not a sideshow. The chapter is organized by cause: parasitic, mycotic (fungal), bacterial, viral, rickettsial/spirochetal, venereal.

**3.2 Malaria.** Recurrent bouts of chills and fever are the signature. The handbook covers the parasite's life cycle, the mosquito vector, and the clinical pattern of periodic fever. Field rules that still hold: malaria is a medical emergency in a non-immune patient; fever in the tropics is malaria until proven otherwise; prevention (nets, repellent, chemoprophylaxis per current guidance, not 1982 regimens) beats treatment; any patient with fever, altered consciousness, or jaundice is evacuated immediately. Do not use the handbook's 1982 antimalarial regimens — resistance patterns have changed completely.

**3.3 Dysentery, cholera, and diarrheal disease.** Amebic dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica) and bacillary dysentery present with bloody diarrhea, cramps, and fever. Cholera presents with profuse watery ("rice-water") diarrhea and rapid dehydration — the killer is fluid loss, and the treatment is fluid replacement, not antibiotics. The durable field lesson: in diarrheal disease, rehydration is the treatment; oral rehydration with clean water, salt, and sugar saves lives; any patient who cannot keep fluids down or shows signs of severe dehydration needs IV fluids and evacuation.

**3.4 Other infections.** The chapter covers tuberculosis, typhoid, hepatitis, dengue, yellow fever, leptospirosis, plague, rabies, tetanus, and venereal disease, each in the same S.O.A.P. format. The pattern to absorb is not each organism's 1982 treatment but the recognition: fever pattern, exposure history, incubation, and the physical signs that separate one disease from another. Full disease tables are in `references/communicable-disease.md`.

**3.5 Vector control.** Prevention is covered in Chapter 20 (preventive medicine): insect repellent on skin, clothing treated or tucked in, bed nets, and environmental control. Kerosene applied to neck, wrists, and boot tops was the handbook's field expedient against chiggers and mites; lindane dust or sulfa powder against mites, chiggers, and lice when repellents are unavailable. Modern repellents (DEET, picaridin, permethrin-treated clothing) replace these — the principle (barrier + repellent + net) is unchanged.

## 4. Airway, Shock, and Emergency Surgery

**4.1 Airway obstruction (Chapter 3).** The handbook covers clearing airway obstructions and CPR in 1982 form. The durable principles: an obstructed airway kills in minutes; position, suction, and manual clearing come first; an emergency cricothyroidotomy is described only as the last resort when obstruction cannot be dislodged within a few minutes. **Do not train CPR or airway management from this chapter** — use current AHA and CoTCCC guidance. What to take from it: the urgency hierarchy (airway before everything), and the recognition that a patient who cannot breathe needs an intervention now, not a diagnosis.

**4.2 Shock (Chapter 15).** Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion, and the handbook covers its recognition: restlessness, pale/cool/clammy skin, rapid weak pulse, falling blood pressure, reduced urine output, altered consciousness. Hemorrhagic shock is the field-relevant form — and here the 1982 text is most dangerous if followed literally: **modern TCCC hemorrhage control (tourniquets, wound packing, junctional control, tranexamic acid per current protocol) supersedes everything in this chapter.** Use the handbook for recognition only; use TCCC for treatment.

**4.3 Emergency war surgery (Chapter 16).** The handbook gives the austere surgeon's rules: control hemorrhage, debride dead tissue, leave contaminated wounds open (delayed primary closure), and never close a war wound primarily. Wound care principles that still hold: clean with irrigation, remove foreign material, dress and cover, give tetanus prophylaxis, and evacuate. The handbook's operative procedures (chest tubes, amputations, laparotomy indications) are presented for a trained medic in extremis — they are not a license for an untrained person to operate. The rule is the preface's: know your limitations, do not exceed them.

**4.4 IV therapy (Chapter 18).** The handbook covers IV fluids and electrolyte basics in two pages — deliberately brief, because IV therapy without labs is guesswork. The durable lesson: in the field, IV fluids are for the patient in shock or who cannot take oral fluids; oral rehydration is preferred whenever the gut works. Do not use the handbook's fluid and electrolyte regimens as a prescribing reference.

**4.5 Anesthesia (Chapter 17).** Twenty pages on field anesthesia — ketamine, local and regional blocks, and general anesthesia in austere conditions. Included here for completeness and for the trained provider's awareness; it is not a skill to improvise from a summary. If you are not trained and equipped for anesthesia, you do not do anesthesia.

## 5. Burns, Blast, Heat, and Cold (Chapters 10–11)

**5.1 Burns.** Burn estimation uses the Rule of Nines (head 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, perineum 1%) — still the standard field method. Depth: first-degree (red, painful, no blister), second-degree (blistered, very painful), third-degree (white/charred, insensate). Field care that still holds: stop the burning, cool with clean water, cover with a clean dry dressing, keep the patient warm (burn patients lose heat), give oral fluids if conscious and not vomiting, and evacuate any significant burn — face, hands, genitalia, circumferential, or >10% body surface in the field setting. Blast injuries add the hidden killer: blast lung and ruptured eardrums, plus penetrating fragments — any blast casualty with breathing difficulty or altered consciousness is an immediate evacuation.

**5.2 Heat injuries.** The progression is heat cramps → heat exhaustion → heat stroke, and the handbook's recognition still holds: cramps are painful muscle spasms in the heat; exhaustion is weakness, nausea, headache, and heavy sweating with a near-normal temperature; heat stroke is altered consciousness with a high temperature and hot, dry (or sometimes sweaty) skin — a life-threatening emergency. Treatment: get the patient out of the heat, remove clothing, cool as rapidly as possible (water, fanning, ice to neck/groin/armpits if available), and give fluids if conscious. **Heat stroke: cool first, evacuate immediately.** Prevention is the activity guidelines in the handbook's heat categories: curtail physical activity as temperature and humidity rise, acclimatize gradually, and enforce water discipline.

**5.3 Cold injuries.** Frostbite (freezing of tissue, usually fingers, toes, nose, ears) and trench foot/immersion foot (prolonged wet cold without freezing) are the two patterns. The handbook's wind-chill chart shows how fast wind strips heat: wind velocity accelerates body-heat loss under both wet and cold conditions. Field rules: rewarm frostbite slowly in warm (not hot) water, never rub frozen tissue, never rewarm if refreezing is likely (a thaw-refreeze cycle is worse than staying frozen), and protect the rewarming patient from further cold. Hypothermia — core temperature falling — is the systemic killer: confusion, slurred speech, and loss of coordination are late signs; a patient who stops shivering is in trouble. Keep the patient dry, insulated from the ground, and warm from the inside (warm fluids if conscious) and the outside. Starvation, exhaustion, and drugs affecting circulation or sweating all predispose to cold injury.

## 6. Bites, Poisoning, and Overdose (Chapters 12–13)

**6.1 Snake bite.** The handbook classifies poisonous snakes (pit vipers, coral snakes, sea snakes) and gives the field picture: fang marks, local swelling and pain, systemic symptoms. Durable field rules: keep the victim calm and still, immobilize the bitten limb at heart level, remove rings and watches before swelling, do NOT cut, suck, or apply a tourniquet, and evacuate for antivenin — which is a hospital treatment, not a field one. Identify the snake only if it can be done safely; do not delay evacuation to hunt it.

**6.2 Insect and animal bites.** Insect stings (anaphylaxis is the emergency — airway swelling, wheeze, collapse), scorpion and spider bites, and animal bites (rabies risk — any bite from an unknown animal needs rabies evaluation). Wound cleaning, tetanus prophylaxis, and evacuation are the field response.

**6.3 Overdose and poisoning.** Sixteen pages covering the common poisons and overdoses of the era. The durable framework: identify the agent if possible, support airway and breathing, do not induce vomiting (modern guidance: never), and get the patient to definitive care with a sample or container of the agent if available. Specific antidotes and doses in this chapter are 1982 and must not be used as a prescribing reference.

## 7. NBC, Dental, Pediatrics, Gynecology/Obstetrics, Veterinary, Primitive Medicine

**7.1 NBC (Chapter 14).** Nuclear, biological, and chemical injuries: recognition of nerve-agent, blister-agent, blood-agent, and choking-agent effects; biological agents; radiation sickness. The recognition patterns (miosis and secretions for nerve agents, delayed blistering for mustard, rapid collapse for cyanide) still orient a provider, but treatment and decontamination follow current CBRN doctrine (ATP 4-02.85 and related publications), not this 1982 chapter.

**7.2 Dental emergencies (Chapter 19).** Fifteen pages — unusually thorough, because dental pain disables a man in the field. Temporary fillings, tooth extraction indications, and dental infections are covered; the field rule is to relieve pain, control infection, and evacuate for definitive care.

**7.3 Pediatrics, gynecology, obstetrics (Chapters 6–8).** Included because an SF medic may be the only medical care for a village: childhood illness, pregnancy, and delivery. The handbook covers normal delivery and common complications — for the modern reader, the value is recognition and the humility to seek help; obstetric emergencies are evacuation cases.

**7.4 Veterinary medicine (Chapter 21) and primitive medicine (Chapter 22).** Animal care (pack animals, livestock, working dogs) and medicine with local materials when supplies run out. The primitive-medicine chapter is the survivalist's gem: what to do when the aid bag is empty. Both are summarized in `references/austere-care.md`.

## 8. Preventive Medicine — The Real Force Multiplier (Chapter 20)

**8.1 The doctrine.** Preventive medicine is the chapter that prevents more casualties than all the treatment chapters combined. A medic who keeps the unit healthy has done more than one who treats the sick well.

**8.2 Water purification.** The handbook's directions for iodine water-purification tablets: one tablet per quart canteen of clear water, two tablets if cloudy — and a warning that one tablet may not guarantee complete destruction of Giardia cysts in clear, very cold water. Modern practice adds filtration and chlorine dioxide, and boiling remains the most reliable method; the principle (treat all water from natural sources) is absolute.

**8.3 Field sanitation.** Latrines 200 feet from water and away from shelter; wash hands after using the latrine and before eating; wash face, underarms, groin, and feet when a full bath is impossible; clean eating utensils after each meal. These four rules prevent more disease than any drug in the book.

**8.4 The medic's preventive checklist.** Water checked and treated; food protected from flies and contamination; latrines sited and maintained; insect control (nets, repellent, clothing); waste and garbage disposed of; the unit's health monitored daily for the first cases of diarrhea, fever, or rash — because the first case is the warning, and the outbreak is the failure.

## Output Contract

When asked about austere or field medicine, answer from this handbook's durable material — S.O.A.P. diagnosis, recognition patterns, sanitation, environmental injury, wound and fracture care — and **lead with the dated-medicine warning whenever the question touches treatment, drugs, CPR, airway, shock, or hemorrhage control**. Give the recognition pattern and the evacuation decision first; give treatment only where it is durable and clearly labeled as such. Never quote a 1982 drug dose as current guidance. Never present the handbook's CPR, tourniquet, or shock management as modern practice — always redirect to TCCC (ATP 4-02.11, 2026). If the question is about a life-threatening emergency, say plainly: follow TCCC, call for evacuation, and use this handbook only for recognition and context.

## Operating Rules

1. **TCCC wins on any conflict.** ATP 4-02.11 (2026) supersedes this 1982 handbook for hemorrhage, airway, shock, and casualty care. Say so every time it matters.
2. **1982 drugs are history, not guidance.** Never dose from this book. Never present its antibiotic or analgesic choices as current.
3. **First, do no harm — and know your limitations.** The handbook's own rule. Evacuate serious cases; do not exceed your training.
4. **Diagnosis before treatment.** S.O.A.P. every patient: history first, exam second, assessment third, plan last.
5. **Prevention beats treatment.** Water, sanitation, vector control, and early recognition prevent more casualties than any treatment chapter.
6. **Rehydration is the treatment for diarrheal disease.** Oral fluids when the gut works; IV and evacuation when it does not.
7. **Cool heat stroke first, evacuate immediately.** Never delay cooling to transport a heat-stroke patient.
8. **Never rewarm frostbite if it can refreeze.** A thaw–refreeze cycle is worse than staying frozen.
