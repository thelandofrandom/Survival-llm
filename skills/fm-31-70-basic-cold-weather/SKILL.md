# FM 31-70 Basic Cold Weather Manual

## Purpose

FM 31-70, *Basic Cold Weather Manual* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 12 April 1968, with Change 1, 17 December 1968, 203 pages) — the Army's basic cold weather manual, written for the individual soldier and the small unit operating in cold regions. It is the foundation document for Army cold-weather operations: individual clothing and equipment, small unit living (shelter, heat, food, water, hygiene, first aid), skiing and snowshoeing, movement, combat techniques, and small unit leadership, with appendices on references, the ground/air emergency code, ski drill, effects of cold weather on weapons, weights and type loads, environmental effects, cold weather hints, and measurement conversion factors. This copy is a reprint which includes current pages from Change 1.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 31-70 is a 1968 manual and its equipment, weapons, and organizational details are obsolete. For current Army doctrine, cold-weather survival and operations are governed by **ATP 3-90.96, *Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations* (2025)** and **TC 21-3, *Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations in Cold Weather*** — follow the current manuals where they disagree with FM 31-70. FM 31-70 remains valuable because its core doctrine is still sound and unusually complete: the C-O-L-D clothing principles, the layer principle, the cold-injury prevention and treatment, the snow-shelter and tentcraft, the water-from-snow procedures, and the skiing and snowshoeing instruction have no equivalent in a single current manual. The durable core is the clothing system, the cold-injury medicine, and the small-unit living techniques — the specific issue items (50/50 cotton-wool underwear, OG 107 hood, LAW lubricant) are period equipment and should be read as examples, not as a shopping list.

## 1. Cold Weather Conditions

**1.1. Two conditions.** The use of cold weather clothing is affected by two types of weather conditions: wet and dry, amplified by humidity coupled with temperature and wind velocity.

- **Cold-wet** conditions occur when temperatures are near freezing and variations in day and night temperatures cause alternate freezing and thawing, often accompanied by rain and wet snow, making the ground muddy and slushy. Troops wear a water-repellent, wind-resistant outer layer and inner layers with sufficient insulation for moderately cold weather (above 14°F).
- **Cold-dry** conditions occur when average temperatures are lower than 14°F. The ground is usually frozen and snow is usually dry, in fine crystals. Strong winds cause low temperatures to seem colder and increase the need for protection of the entire body (windchill). Troops need additional insulating layers, particularly when entering static situations after strenuous exercise.

What is termed moderately cold to one person may be extremely cold to another, and the windchill factor must always be considered — a moderate cold can change momentarily to extreme cold with the addition of high winds. Commanders should use the type loads for planning only and adjust them to fit a given situation and temperature condition. Because of differences in individual metabolism, commanders must not be arbitrary in delineating strict uniform requirements, but must allow some personal choice of undergarments.

**1.2. Heat balance.** The body loses heat at variable rates, and heat may flow from the body at a rate equal to or greater than the rate at which it is produced. When heat loss exceeds heat production, the body uses up the heat stored in its tissues, causing a rapid drop in body temperature. Excessive heat loss causes shivering — an important warning to start action to rewarm, by adding clothing, exercising, eating, or entering a warm shelter. In freezing temperatures it is as important to remove and adjust clothing to prevent excessive overheating as it is to add clothing to prevent heat loss.

## 2. Clothing — The C-O-L-D System

**2.1. Purpose and design.** Proper clothing, correctly worn, assists the body in adjusting to extreme cold by holding in body heat, insulating the body against the cold outside air. The principles: **insulation** (dry air is an excellent insulator; woolen cloth contains thousands of tiny pockets that trap air warmed by the body), the **layer principle**, and **ventilation** (perspiration fills the air spaces of clothing with moisture-laden air and reduces insulating qualities; as perspiration evaporates it cools the body).

**2.2. The layer principle.** Several layers of medium-weight clothing provide more warmth than one heavy garment, even if the single heavy garment is as thick as the combined layers — the effect comes from the several thick layers of air trapped between the layers, rather than one or two layers of large volume. The layers are of different design: the winter underwear is most porous with many air pockets; the outer garments are windproof and water-repellent to keep cold outside air from the warmed inside air. The layer principle allows maximum freedom of action and rapid adjustment through a wide range of temperatures and activities.

**2.3. Ventilation.** Cold weather clothing is designed so the neck, waist, hip, sleeve, and ankle fastenings can be opened or closed to provide ventilation. The manual's image: the body is a house and the openings in the clothing are windows — open at opposite ends and cross-draft ventilation results. If ventilation is excessive, open only the neck of the garment to allow warm air to escape without permitting complete circulation.

**2.4. C-O-L-D — the five rules of keeping warm.**

1. **C — Keep clothing Clean.** Dirt and grease mat the fibers, crush the air pockets, and destroy insulation; grease also makes clothing more flammable. Underwear requires the closest attention. Wash winter underwear (Army issue 50/50 cotton/wool) in lukewarm water — hot water injures wool fibers and causes shrinkage; synthetic detergents are more soluble in cool water. Rinse all soap or detergent out of outer garments, since residue lessens water-shedding quality. If washing is not possible, dry rubbing and airing remove some dirt and body oils.
2. **O — Avoid Overheating.** Overheating causes perspiration, which dampens clothing, lessens insulation, and cools the body further as it evaporates. Adjust clothing by ventilating (partially opening parka or jacket), removing an inner layer, removing heavy mittens, throwing back the parka hood, or changing to lighter head cover. The head and hands, richly supplied with blood, act as efficient heat dissipators. In cold temperatures it is better to be slightly chilly than excessively warm.
3. **L — Wear clothing Loose and in layers.** Tight clothing and footgear restrict blood circulation and invite cold injury. Wearing more socks than is correct for the footgear makes the boot fit too tightly; a field jacket that fits snugly over a wool shirt is too tight when a liner is also worn. Tight garments also lessen the volume of trapped air layers.
4. **D — Keep clothing Dry.** Moisture soaks in from two directions: inside (perspiration) and outside (melting snow and frost). Brush or shake snow from uniforms before entering heated shelters — do not rub it off, which works it into the fabric. Damp mittens and socks may be hung on the pack on the march, or placed unfolded near the body where body heat dries them. In bivouac, hang damp clothing inside the tent near the top on drying lines. Clothing and footwear should not be dried close to a heat source; leather articles, especially boots, must be dried slowly — if boots cannot be dried otherwise, place them between the sleeping bag and liner.

**2.5. The uniforms.** The **cold-wet uniform** (water-repellent, wind-resistant outer layer; wool OG shirt intermediate layer; 50/50 cotton-wool underwear inner layer; cushion-sole socks; trigger-finger mitten shells or arctic mitten set; winter hood; poncho) and the **cold-dry uniform** (additional insulating layers, including the parka with nylon quilted liner, for temperatures below 14°F) are described in detail with their issue items in Chapter 2 and Appendix E. Footgear follows the same layer logic: layers of socks inside insulated boots with a release valve, never so many socks that the boot fits tightly. → `references/clothing-and-equipment.md`

## 3. Small Unit Living

**3.1. Shelter.** The success of cold weather operations depends upon adequate shelter. Tents and improvised shelters are both covered: pitching and striking cold weather tents, ventilation (a tent must be ventilated even in extreme cold, or moisture and carbon monoxide accumulate), heating of semipermanent tents with stoves, and fuel economy. Improvised shelters include poncho shelters, lean-tos, tree shelters, wigwams, snow holes, and snow caves — the snow cave's tunnel entrance must give access while keeping wind and spindrift out, and snow's insulating property makes a snow shelter warmer than a tent in still air. Campfires are built first to protect the camp site, and heat discipline and fire prevention are command responsibilities. Bivouac site selection depends on the ground: forests, marshy ground, and mountains each have their own rules, and tents should be pitched where they can be sheltered from wind with the best natural cover available.

**3.2. Food and messing.** Small unit messing in the cold requires more fuel and more time; canned rations, either frozen or thawed, must be handled carefully — frozen food should not be placed near heat and then refrozen, and food refrozen two or three times is tasteless. A hot meal is important for morale and for calories; the body burns extra fuel to stay warm. Animals of cold regions (fish and game) can supplement rations for units that must live off the land.

**3.3. Water — the snow problem.** Water is plentiful in most cold regions in one form or another: streams, lakes and ponds, glaciers, fresh-water ice, and last year's sea ice. Freshly frozen sea ice is salty, but year-old sea ice has had the salt leached out — old sea ice is rounded where broken and pitted with pools; fresh sea ice is milky and angular. If possible, water should be obtained from running streams or lakes rather than by melting ice or snow: melting is slow and consumes large quantities of fuel — **17 cubic inches of uncompacted snow, when melted, yields only 1 cubic inch of water**. Ice is preferable to snow when available; if snow must be used, take the most compact snow, gathered only from areas not contaminated by animals, humans, or toxic agents. **Melting procedure:** "prime" the pot with a small quantity of water and add snow gradually (or melt a small quantity in the bottom first if no water is available), compact the snow and stir occasionally to prevent burning the bottom, keep pots of snow or ice on the stove when not cooking so water is available when needed, and keep snow to be melted just outside the shelter. In an emergency, an inflated air mattress placed in the sun at a slight incline will melt light, fluffy snow thrown on it, the water running down the creases to be caught in a canteen cup. Water obtained by melting snow or ice may be purified with water purification tablets. Boiling or tablets have no effect on radioactive contaminants; water suspected of radiological contamination may be filtered through a 15 cm (6 inch) column of loose dirt and then chlorinated or iodinated. **Storage and transport:** 5-gallon water cans with insulated covers, filled only three-quarters full to allow agitation and help prevent freezing; 5-gallon insulated food containers filled at night hold enough water for about four men the next day, and will keep water from freezing for as long as 40 hours at −20°F if the water was at boiling point when filled. → `references/living-water-shelter.md`

**3.4. Dehydration.** Dehydration in the cold is a problem that cannot be overemphasized — and it can be overcome by well-trained, disciplined troops oriented in its causes, symptoms, and effects. Symptoms resemble heat exhaustion: parched mouth, tongue, and throat, difficulty swallowing, nausea, faintness, extreme dizziness, vomiting, general tiredness and weakness, muscle cramps (especially in the legs), difficulty keeping the eyes in focus, fainting or blacking out. The effect is to incapacitate the individual for a few hours to several days; small patrols beyond immediate help must be extra cautious, since dehydration risks a secondary and more dangerous effect — becoming a cold weather casualty while incapacitated.

**3.5. Hygiene.** In cold weather the care of the body requires special emphasis: washing, eating properly, sufficient liquids and salt. Beards add very little insulation and soil clothing with natural oils; in winter a beard or mustache is a nuisance, serving as a base for ice buildup from breath moisture and masking the presence of frostbite. Shave to detect frostbite early.

## 4. Cold Injury — Prevention and Treatment

**4.1. Frostbite.** Frostbite is the freezing of some part of the body by exposure to temperatures below freezing — a constant hazard, especially when the wind is strong. Usually there is an uncomfortable sensation of coldness followed by numbness; there may be tingling, stinging, aching, even cramping pain. The skin initially turns red, later pale gray or waxy white. For all practical purposes frostbite is classified as **superficial or deep**, and treatment is based solely on this classification. Prevention: sufficient clothing against cold and wind; keep clothing and body as dry as possible and avoid perspiring (remove outer layers for heavy work, replace as soon as work stops; change socks when feet become moist); never interfere with circulation (tight socks, shoes, and handwear are especially dangerous); do not touch cold metal with bare skin in extreme cold; provide adequate clothing and shelter during inactivity; exercise the face, fingers, and toes from time to time; **use the buddy system — pair off and watch each other for signs of frostbite**, thawing any small frozen spots immediately with bare hands or other body heat.

- **Superficial frostbite** (skin only): cover the cheeks with warm hands until pain returns; place frostbitten fingers under the opposing armpits next to the skin; place bared frostbitten feet under clothing against a companion's belly. Do NOT rewarm by massage, open fires, cold water soaks, or rubbing with snow. Be prepared for pain when thawing occurs.
- **Deep frostbite** (freezing below the skin — the part appears yellowish or whitish gray, frequently without pain): **do not attempt to treat it in the field**. Get to a hospital or aid station by the fastest means; if transportation is available, avoid walking. Protect the frozen part from additional injury but do not thaw it by rubbing, bending, or massage; do not rub with snow; do not place in cold or warm water; do not expose to hot air or open fires; do not use ointments or poultices. Thawing in the field increases pain and invites infection, greater damage, and gangrene. There is less danger in walking on frozen feet than after thawing.

**4.2. Trenchfoot.** Thermal injury from exposure to cold short of freezing in a damp or wet environment, arbitrarily said to occur between **32°F and 50°F**. Causes include immobility of the limbs, insufficient clothing, and constriction by boots, socks, and garments. Early stages: feet and toes pale, cold, numb, stiff; walking becomes difficult. If preventive action is not taken, the feet swell and become painful; in extreme cases flesh dies and amputation may be necessary. Because the early stages are not painful, individuals must be constantly alert. Prevention: keep feet dry with waterproof footgear and dry shelter floors; clean and dry socks and boots at every opportunity, preferably daily; dry feet as soon as possible after getting them wet, apply foot powder, put on dry socks; if wet boots and socks must be worn, exercise the feet continually and never wear tight boots. Treatment: handle feet very gently — do not rub or massage; cleanse carefully with plain white soap and water, dry, elevate, and leave exposed; keep feet at room temperature; carry the casualty, do not permit walking on damaged feet.

**4.3. Immersion foot.** Injury following prolonged immersion of the feet in water not cold enough to freeze — observed after exposure in subtropical waters also. Clinically and pathologically indistinguishable from trenchfoot; treatment is the same.

**4.4. Total immersion (hypothermia).** Immersion in near-freezing water for a few minutes, or exposure to severe dry cold while inadequately dressed, causes total body cooling with a marked drop in core temperature. See Appendix F for description and therapy; the casualty needs heated shelter, warm liquids, and rapid medical attention.

**4.5. Shock, sunburn, snow blindness, carbon monoxide.** Shock develops more rapidly and progresses more deeply in extreme cold than in normal temperatures — signs are apprehension, sweating, pallor, rapid faint pulse, cold clammy skin, and thirst; keep the patient warm with blankets and sleeping bags, give warm soup, chocolate, coffee, or tea if conscious and there is no known or suspected abdominal injury, and get medical attention as soon as possible. Sunburn can occur when the air is below freezing: on snow, ice, and water the sun's rays reflect from all angles, and exposure time for a burn is reduced in the clear air of high altitudes — carry sunburn cream and chapstick, and apply to exposed face parts. Snow blindness occurs when the sun shines brightly on snow, from reflected ultraviolet rays, particularly after a fall of new snow and at high altitudes. Carbon monoxide poisoning: any fuel-burning device gives off carbon monoxide, and adequate ventilation must be provided whenever stoves are used for food preparation or heating in tents and shelters. → `references/cold-injury.md`

## 5. Skiing and Snowshoeing

**5.1. Skiing.** Chapter 4 is a complete ski instruction course: snow composition and its effects, preparation of skis, basic movement (walking step, step turn, kick turn), falling technique, straight uphill climbing, sidestep, uphill traverse, sidestep traverse, herringbone, straight downhill running, snowplow, ski pole riding, sideslipping, step turn in motion, snowplow turn, and advanced turns (uphill christiania, snowplow christiania, lifted christiania). Skiing in variable terrain and snow, obstacles, sled pulling, and skijoring (being towed on skis) are covered, with a full ski drill in Appendix C. Improperly adjusted bindings chafe feet or wear out quickly; bindings must be fitted to the individual and the boot.

**5.2. Snowshoes.** Snowshoe technique, purpose and scope, and the circumstances where snowshoes beat skis — dense forest, broken ground, and work around camp where skis are impractical. Individuals using snowshoes may keep them on for most camp tasks; skis are faster on open ground and downhill, snowshoes more maneuverable in close terrain.

## 6. Movement

**6.1. Foot movement.** Basic rules for foot movement in snow and cold; trail marking (marks about 1 meter / 3 feet off the trail, on the ground and in trees); navigation by dead reckoning, with a log mandatory; what to do when lost within known locality. Seasonal changes alter every movement calculation — what is a road in summer may be a snowfield in winter.

**6.2. Vehicles and aircraft.** Track-laying vehicles conserve the energy of troops and move where wheeled vehicles cannot; tanks have their own cold-weather limitations; man-hauled sleds move guns in deep snow and are particularly useful where vehicles cannot go; aircraft are valuable for reconnaissance, resupply, and evacuation in areas where ground movement is impossible.

## 7. Combat Techniques and Leadership

**7.1. Northern warfare.** Two opponents face the soldier in northern warfare — the enemy, who must be defeated, and nature, which must be made an ally. The principles of war remain unchanged and tactics are the same as anywhere else, but successful warfare in extreme cold depends on a great number of techniques, and all individuals and units must be indoctrinated and thoroughly trained in them. In the isolated areas of the North it is most essential that a system of teams be developed: pair men together as "buddies" and insure a higher standard of efficiency, safety, and morale. **Never send one man alone on a mission.**

**7.2. Weapons in the cold.** Effects of cold weather on weapons (Appendix D): lubricants thicken, metals contract, and weapons must be stripped completely and lubricated with LAW (Lubricant, Semi-Fluid, MIL-L-46000A) — the Change 1 correction to the original text. Care, cleaning, and maintenance of weapons and special items; blowing snow and fog (ice fog is very common in extreme cold); effects of snow, ice, and frozen ground on crew-served weapon positions; handling of skis and snowshoes in combat formations. Extreme cold lowers the efficiency of batteries and all electrical equipment.

**7.3. Small unit leaders.** Chapter 7 addresses the small unit leader directly: leadership in the cold is a matter of personal example, constant supervision of the details (feet, clothing, water, food, shelter), and the discipline to enforce the small routines that prevent casualties. The leader who checks feet, enforces drinking, and keeps men paired as buddies prevents more casualties than any medical treatment can cure.

## Output Contract

When answering a cold-weather question from FM 31-70, give the C-O-L-D rule first, then the numbers (14°F wet/dry line, 17:1 snow-to-water ratio, 32–50°F trenchfoot range, 40 hours at −20°F for stored water), then the procedure, then the cautions. State the 1968 vintage and the period equipment, and where current doctrine (ATP 3-90.96, TC 21-3) differs, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter or appendix the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **C-O-L-D.** Clean, avoid Overheating, Loose and in layers, Dry — the whole clothing system in four letters.
2. **Better slightly chilly than excessively warm.** Sweat is the enemy; ventilate before you sweat.
3. **Never send one man alone.** The buddy system is a safety system, not a morale nicety.
4. **Deep frostbite is not a field treatment.** Protect, evacuate, do not thaw in the field.
5. **Snow is mostly air.** 17 cubic inches of snow make 1 cubic inch of water — get water from streams and ice first.
6. **Ventilate every heated shelter.** Stoves kill by carbon monoxide in unventilated tents.
7. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-90.96 (2025) and TC 21-3 supersede FM 31-70 where they conflict; FM 31-70 is the deep cold-weather field reference, not the authority.
