# Movement and Combat — FM 31-70

## Skiing

Chapter 4 is a complete ski instruction course: snow composition and its effects on movement, preparation of skis, basic movement (walking step, step turn, kick turn), falling technique, straight uphill climbing, sidestep, uphill traverse, sidestep traverse, herringbone, straight downhill running, snowplow, ski pole riding, sideslipping, step turn in motion, snowplow turn, and advanced turns (uphill christiania, snowplow christiania, lifted christiania). Skiing in variable terrain and snow, obstacles, sled pulling, and skijoring (being towed on skis) are covered, with a full ski drill in Appendix C.

Key points: improperly adjusted bindings chafe feet or wear out quickly — bindings must be fitted to the individual and the boot. Skiing requires a high degree of training; laying troops (troops on skis) require a high degree of physical conditioning. Skis are faster on open ground and downhill; their advantage disappears in dense forest and broken ground.

## Snowshoeing

Snowshoe technique, purpose and scope, and the circumstances where snowshoes beat skis — dense forest, broken ground, and work around camp where skis are impractical. Individuals using snowshoes may keep them on for most camp tasks. Snowshoes are more maneuverable in close terrain; skis are faster on open ground.

## Foot Movement and Navigation

Basic rules for foot movement in snow and cold; trail marking (marks about 1 meter / 3 feet off the trail, on the ground and in trees, so the trail can be followed back); navigation by dead reckoning, with a log mandatory; what to do when lost within known locality — stop, think, and use the map and compass rather than wandering. Seasonal changes alter every movement calculation: what is a road in summer may be a snowfield in winter, and frozen rivers and lakes become routes in winter that are barriers in summer.

## Vehicles and Aircraft

Track-laying vehicles conserve the energy of troops and move where wheeled vehicles cannot; tanks have their own cold-weather limitations (lubricants, batteries, frozen ground); man-hauled sleds move guns in deep snow and are particularly useful where vehicles cannot go; aircraft are valuable for reconnaissance, resupply, and evacuation in areas where ground movement is impossible. The best way to transport water in cold regions is by track-laying vehicles, which are not dependent on roads.

## Combat Techniques

**Northern warfare.** Two opponents face the soldier in northern warfare — the enemy, who must be defeated, and nature, which must be made an ally. We fight the enemy, but we must accept nature as it is, making nature fight with and for us. The principles of war remain unchanged and tactics are the same as anywhere else, but successful warfare in extreme cold depends on a great number of techniques, and all individuals and units must be indoctrinated and thoroughly trained in them. There is always opportunity for each soldier to display initiative — not only in combat, but in the small things that make life more comfortable in the North. In the isolated areas of the North it is most essential that a system of teams be developed: pair men together as "buddies" and insure a higher standard of efficiency, safety, and morale. **Never send one man alone on a mission.**

**Weapons in the cold (Appendix D).** Lubricants thicken, metals contract, and weapons must be stripped completely and lubricated with LAW (Lubricant, Semi-Fluid, MIL-L-46000A) — the Change 1 correction. Care, cleaning, and maintenance of weapons and special items; blowing snow and fog (ice fog is very common in extreme cold and betrays positions); effects of snow, ice, and frozen ground on crew-served weapon positions (frozen ground cannot be dug; positions must be built up with snow and ice); handling of skis and snowshoes in combat formations. Extreme cold lowers the efficiency of batteries and all electrical equipment.

**Emergency evacuation.** Procedures for emergency evacuation of casualties in cold weather, including improvised litters and sleds, and the use of aircraft where ground evacuation is impossible.

## Small Unit Leadership

Chapter 7 addresses the small unit leader directly: leadership in the cold is a matter of personal example, constant supervision of the details (feet, clothing, water, food, shelter), and the discipline to enforce the small routines that prevent casualties. The leader who checks feet, enforces drinking, and keeps men paired as buddies prevents more casualties than any medical treatment can cure. The leader must also manage morale in isolation and darkness: routine, purpose, and small achievable goals keep a unit effective through a northern winter.
