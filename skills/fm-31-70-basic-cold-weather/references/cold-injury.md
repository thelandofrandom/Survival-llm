# Cold Injury — FM 31-70

## Frostbite

Frostbite is the freezing of some part of the body by exposure to temperatures below freezing — a constant hazard, especially when the wind is strong. Usually there is an uncomfortable sensation of coldness followed by numbness; there may be tingling, stinging, aching, even cramping pain. The skin initially turns red, later pale gray or waxy white. For all practical purposes frostbite is classified as **superficial or deep**, and treatment is based solely on this classification.

**Prevention:**
- Sufficient clothing for protection against cold and wind; the face must be protected in high wind and when exposed to aircraft prop blast.
- Keep clothing and body as dry as possible; avoid perspiring. For heavy work in the cold, remove outer layers as needed and replace them as soon as work stops. Change socks whenever feet become moist.
- Any interference with circulation reduces the heat delivered to the extremities. All clothing and equipment must be properly fitted and worn to avoid interference with circulation. Tight socks, shoes, and handwear are especially dangerous in very cold climates.
- Do not touch cold metal with bare skin in extreme cold — it could mean loss of skin.
- Provide adequate clothing and shelter during periods of inactivity.
- Exercise the face, fingers, and toes from time to time to keep them warm and to detect numb or hard areas; massage them with the hands for the same purpose.
- **Use the buddy system.** Men should pair off and watch each other closely for signs of frostbite and for mutual aid if frostbite occurs. Any small frozen spots should be thawed immediately, using bare hands or other sources of body heat.

**Superficial frostbite** (skin only): cover the cheeks with warm hands until pain returns; place uncovered frostbitten fingers under the opposing armpits, next to the skin; place bared frostbitten feet under clothing against the belly of a companion. Do NOT rewarm by massage, exposure to open fires, cold water soaks, or rubbing with snow. Be prepared for pain when thawing occurs.

**Deep frostbite** (freezing below the skin): the part appears yellowish or whitish gray, frequently without pain. The face can usually be judged by appearance; hands and feet are harder — judge by how long the part has been without sensation: if very short, probably superficial; otherwise assume deep and therefore serious. **Do not attempt to treat deep frostbite in the field.** Get to a hospital or aid station by the fastest means possible; if transportation is available, avoid walking. Protect the frozen part from additional injury but do not attempt to thaw it by rubbing, bending, or massage; do not rub with snow; do not place in cold or warm water; do not expose to hot air or open fires; do not use ointments or poultices. Thawing in the field increases pain and invites infection, greater damage, and gangrene. There is less danger in walking on feet while frozen than after thawing. Thawing may occur spontaneously during transportation — this cannot readily be avoided since the body in general must be kept warm.

## Trenchfoot

Thermal injury from exposure to cold short of freezing in a damp or wet environment, arbitrarily said to occur between **32°F and 50°F**. Partial causes include immobility of the limbs (legs and feet down as in sitting or standing), insufficient clothing, and constriction by boots, socks, and other garments. Almost identical with gradual frostbite, since the primary causes are the same except for differences in the degree of cold. Early stages: feet and toes pale, cold, numb, stiff; walking becomes difficult. If preventive action is not taken, the feet swell and become painful; in extreme cases flesh dies and amputation of the foot or leg may be necessary. Because the early stages are not painful, individuals must be constantly alert.

**Prevention:** keep feet dry with waterproof footgear and dry shelter floors; clean and dry socks and boots at every opportunity, preferably daily; dry feet as soon as possible after getting them wet, apply foot powder, put on dry socks; if wet boots and socks must be worn, exercise feet continually by wriggling toes and bending ankles; never wear tight boots.

**Treatment:** handle feet very gently — do not rub or massage. If necessary, cleanse carefully with plain white soap and water, dry, elevate, and leave exposed. While it is desirable to warm the patient, the feet should always be kept at room temperature. Carry the casualty; do not permit walking on damaged feet.

## Immersion Foot

Injury following prolonged immersion of the feet in water not sufficiently cold to cause freezing or frostbite; observed after exposure in subtropical waters also. Clinically and pathologically indistinguishable from trenchfoot, since the cause is essentially the same — lowering of the temperature of the part involved. Usually associated with dependency (legs and feet down) and immobility of the lower extremities and constriction by clothing and shoes; other factors include body cooling from wind, total immersion, inadequate clothing, sickness, and starvation. Treatment is the same as for trenchfoot.

## Total Immersion (Hypothermia)

Immersion in near-freezing water for a few minutes, or exposure to severe dry cold while inadequately dressed, causes total body cooling with a marked drop in inner body (core) temperature. See Appendix F for description and therapy. The casualty needs heated shelter in which medical personnel can operate, warm liquids, and rapid evacuation.

## Shock

Shock is brought about by a reduction of circulating blood volume — severe injuries, loss of blood, pain, emotional disturbances. The body's normal reaction to severe cold, reduction of blood volume circulating to the extremities, is very similar to the circulatory reaction to shock. Shock develops more rapidly and progresses more deeply in extreme cold than in normal temperature. Signs: apprehension, sweating, pallor, rapid faint pulse, cold clammy skin, thirst. First aid: make the patient comfortable; position the litter so the patient is comfortable and not apt to inhale vomitus; keep warm with blankets and sleeping bags; when conscious, give warm soup, chocolate, coffee, or tea if there is no known or suspected abdominal injury; get medical attention as soon as possible.

## Sunburn and Snow Blindness

An individual may get sunburned when the air temperature is below freezing: on snow, ice, and water the sun's rays reflect from all angles, and in a valley the rays come from every direction. Sunlight reflected upward attacks the skin where it is very sensitive — around the lips, nostrils, and eyelids. Exposure time for a burn is reduced in the clear air of high altitudes. Carry sunburn cream and chapstick in the pocket and apply to exposed face parts. In mild weather, protect the neck and ears by draping a handkerchief over the back of the head, held by the cap like a desert neckcloth. Do not use soap or shaving lotions with high alcoholic content — they remove natural oils that protect the skin from the sun. If blistered, report to an aid station as soon as possible, as blistered areas (especially lips) may become badly infected.

Snow blindness occurs when the sun shines brightly on an expanse of snow, from reflected ultraviolet rays; particularly likely after a fall of new snow, even when the sun is partially obscured by light mist or fog, and the risk is increased at high altitudes.

## Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

Any fuel-burning device gives off carbon monoxide. Adequate ventilation must be provided whenever stoves are used for food preparation or heating in tents and shelters. See the shelter chapter for ventilation requirements — an unventilated heated tent is a carbon monoxide trap.
