# Living, Water, Shelter — FM 31-70

## Shelter

The success of cold weather operations depends upon adequate shelter. Tents and improvised shelters are both covered.

**Tents.** Pitching and striking cold weather tents; ventilation — a tent must be ventilated even in extreme cold, or moisture and carbon monoxide accumulate; heating of semipermanent tents with stoves; fuel economy. Tents should be pitched where they can be sheltered from wind with the best natural cover available. Tools needed for frozen ground include axes and saws; frozen ground cannot be dug with ordinary entrenching tools.

**Improvised shelters.** Poncho shelters (ponchos raised with hood drawstrings), lean-tos (built with snowblocks instead of heavy logs where available), tree shelters (protection from strong winds, built against a tree with boughs), wigwams, snow holes (dug into the side of a drift for one or two men), and snow caves (the tunnel entrance must give access while keeping wind and spindrift out). Snow's insulating property makes a snow shelter warmer than a tent in still air. Campfires are built first to protect the camp site.

**Bivouac.** Location of bivouac sites depends on the ground: forests (shelter from wind, fuel at hand), marshy ground (drainage and dry footing), mountains (shelter from wind and avalanche). Establishing bivouac with skis, snowshoes, or tracked vehicles; heat discipline and fire prevention; drying clothes; sleeping arrangements in bivouac (sleeping bags, insulation from the ground, pairing for warmth); water points and snow areas; field sanitation.

## Food and Messing

Small unit messing in the cold requires more fuel and more time; a hot meal is important for morale and for calories — the body burns extra fuel to stay warm. Canned rations, either frozen or thawed, must be handled carefully: frozen food should not be placed near heat and then refrozen, and food refrozen two or three times is tasteless. Once thawed, certain foods should be eaten promptly. Animals of cold regions (fish and game) can supplement rations for units that must live off the land; fish and game are ready to be butchered and stored in the cold.

## Water

Water is plentiful in most cold regions in one form or another: streams, lakes and ponds, glaciers, fresh-water ice, and last year's sea ice. Freshly frozen sea ice is salty, but year-old sea ice has had the salt leached out — old sea ice is rounded where broken and pitted with pools; fresh sea ice is milky and angular. In some areas with small tidal action, a layer of fresh water up to 50–100 cm (20–40 inches) deep lies on top of the ice.

If possible, obtain water from running streams or lakes rather than by melting ice or snow: melting is slow and consumes large quantities of fuel — **17 cubic inches of uncompacted snow, when melted, yields only 1 cubic inch of water**. Ice is preferable to snow when available; if snow must be used, take the most compact snow, gathered only from areas not contaminated by animals, humans, or toxic agents. In winter a hole may be cut through the ice of a stream or lake; cover it with snowblocks, a poncho, board, or ration box, and pile loose snow on top to insulate and prevent refreezing.

**Melting procedure:** "prime" the pot with a small quantity of water and add snow gradually (or melt a small quantity in the bottom first if no water is available); compact the snow and stir occasionally to prevent burning the bottom; keep pots of snow or ice on the stove when not cooking so water is available when needed; keep snow to be melted just outside the shelter and bring it in as needed. In an emergency, an inflated air mattress placed in the sun at a slight incline will melt light, fluffy snow thrown on it, the water running down the creases to be caught in a canteen cup.

Water obtained by melting snow or ice may be purified with water purification tablets, providing it has not been contaminated by toxic agents. Boiling or tablets have no effect on radioactive contaminants; water suspected of radiological contamination may be filtered through a 15 cm (6 inch) column of loose dirt and then chlorinated or iodinated. During chemical, biological, or nuclear warfare, detection tests should be conducted during or after melting, since frozen contamination may not be detectable and radiological contamination covered with snow or ice may not show on radiac instruments.

**Storage and transport:** 5-gallon water cans with insulated covers, filled only three-quarters full to allow agitation and help prevent freezing while in transit; cans stored off the floor in heated shelters as soon as delivered. Sled-mounted 250- to 300-gallon water tanks with immersion-type heaters have proved satisfactory. For small units of two to four men, the 5-gallon insulated food container is satisfactory — filled at night it holds enough water for the next day's needs for about four men, and will keep water from freezing for as long as **40 hours at −20°F** if the water was at boiling point when filled. The best way to transport water in cold regions is by track-laying vehicles, which are not dependent on roads.

## Dehydration

Dehydration in the cold is a problem that cannot be overemphasized — and it can be overcome by well-trained, disciplined troops oriented in its causes, symptoms, and effects. Symptoms resemble heat exhaustion: parched mouth, tongue, and throat, difficulty swallowing, nausea, faintness, extreme dizziness, vomiting, general tiredness and weakness, muscle cramps (especially in the legs), difficulty keeping the eyes in focus, fainting or blacking out. The effect is to incapacitate the individual for a few hours to several days; small patrols beyond immediate help must be extra cautious, since dehydration risks a secondary and more dangerous effect — becoming a cold weather casualty while incapacitated.

## Hygiene

In cold weather the care of the body requires special emphasis: washing, eating properly, sufficient liquids and salt. Lowered efficiency increases the possibility of casualties, either by cold injury or enemy action. Beards add very little insulation and soil clothing with natural oils; in winter a beard or mustache is a nuisance, serving as a base for ice buildup from breath moisture and masking the presence of frostbite. Shave to detect frostbite early. Personal hygiene, foot care, and sanitation are command responsibilities, not private matters.
