# MCRP 12-10A.1 Small Unit Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare Operations (2013)

## Purpose

MCRP 12-10A.1 (formerly MCRP 3-35.1A), *Small Unit Leader's Guide to Mountain Warfare Operations* (U.S. Marine Corps, 21 May 2013, with Change 1, 4 April 2018, 228 pages) — the reference for all Marine leaders, team through company, conducting operations in mountainous terrain, snow, cold weather, and/or high altitude. It is to be used with MCWP 3-35.1, *Mountain Warfare Operations*, and the companion volumes MCRP 12-10A.2 (winter operations) and MCRP 12-10A.3 (mountain leader's guide). The foreword states its purpose plainly: because the Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center cannot train every Marine or unit, and because personnel turnover and operational tempo are high, Marines in combat may have to learn from doctrinal references augmented by their unit's qualified summer and winter mountain leaders — this publication gives them that capability. It supersedes MCRP 3-35.1A, *Small Unit Leader's Guide to Cold Weather Operations*, dated 16 November 1990.

**Date and currency note — read this first.** The base publication is dated **21 May 2013**. **Change 1, dated 4 April 2018**, edited the publication "to ensure gender neutrality of all applicable and appropriate terms" and states explicitly that "no other content has been affected" — so the doctrine in this skill is the 2013 doctrine, reissued in 2018 with a new cover and a renumbering from MCRP 3-35.1A to MCRP 12-10A.1. That is why the vault lists the entry under a 2018 date while the PDF is the 2013 edition. The **newer edition is MCRP 12-10A.1 W/CH-1 (2026)**, already a skill in this vault as `mcrp-12-10a-1-mountain-warfare-leaders-guide` — use that skill for current doctrine, and this one for the 2013 baseline and its detail. The companion winter volume (MCRP 12-10A.2) and mountain leader's guide (MCRP 12-10A.3) are also skills in this vault.

## 1. Mountain weather (Chapter 1)

**Evaluate constantly.** Whether traveling or in the patrol base, Marines should constantly evaluate: what is the weather currently? What was the weather recently, and when did it change last? When is the next forecasted change coming? A Marine's ability to see the enemy, terrain, and troops is greatly affected by weather, and access to weather information is itself a readiness task.

**Winds.** Warmed air plus the earth's rotation produces the global wind circulation that spreads heat and moisture. Two mountain-specific winds: **anabatic winds** blow up mountain valleys to replace warm rising air and are usually light; **katabatic winds** blow down mountain valley slopes as air cools and are occasionally strong. The **jet stream** — a long, meandering current of high-speed winds near the tropopause, generally from the west and often exceeding 250 miles per hour — shapes the weather above the mid-latitudes. Wind speed is estimated by observation (the Beaufort scale, Table 1-1: 0–1 mph, smoke rises straight up; 2–3 mph, smoke drifts) and combined with air temperature produces the **windchill index** (Table 1-2, National Weather Service) — frostbite occurs in 15 minutes or less under severe windchill conditions.

**Clouds and forecasting.** Cloud types signal coming weather: anvil-shaped clouds produce precipitation; stratus forms when a layer of moist air rises. Close observation of nature helps forecast incoming change. The chapter's weather material is the foundation for the avalanche chapter (Chapter 6), which depends on reading snow, wind, and temperature together. → `references/weather-movement-navigation.md`

## 2. Command and control (Chapter 2)

Leadership is a vital aspect of military operations in all environments, particularly in the extreme conditions of mountain warfare, where small units operate decentralized and the leader's decisions about movement, rest, and risk are the mission. The chapter covers command relationships and control measures for mountain operations, communications in mountainous terrain (where line-of-sight radios, antennas, and equipment all behave differently than on flat ground), and the training pipeline through the Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center (MCMWTC) — including how units request training and how qualified summer and winter mountain leaders are used as force multipliers inside units that cannot all attend the center. → `references/weather-movement-navigation.md`

## 3. Movement and navigation (Chapter 3)

**Individual movement is considerably more strenuous in the mountains than on flat terrain.** The chapter's fundamentals: maintain body weight over the feet at all times, placing the weight on the skeletal system rather than the muscles; avoid walking up and down microterrain (small projections such as fallen trees or boulders that can be stepped over or gone around) because the small extra expenditure compounds on long movements; step over rather than on top of obstacles; and **make use of the rest step** — lock the rear leg out with each step, resting body weight onto the skeletal frame — which can reduce energy expenditure by up to 50 percent.

**Special walking techniques.** The **herringbone** (toes pointed outward, flat feet, heel resting, used ascending — walking on the toes tires the calf muscles), the **half-herringbone** (traversing up or down a slope, ankles rolled away from the hill, uphill foot pointed in the direction of travel), and the **plunge step** (a confident, aggressive move down loose slopes — face downhill, step assertively away from the slope, land solidly on the heel with the leg vertical). Ski poles transfer part of the weight from the lower body to the poles; on a traverse, the uphill pole is held along the shaft rather than the handgrip to accommodate the slope angle.

**Navigation.** The chapter covers walking, navigation, and distance-measuring techniques in mountainous terrain, including the direction method (navigation with a compass) and the problem that a terrain feature with a recognizable shape on the map may not be recognizable on the ground. → `references/weather-movement-navigation.md`

## 4. Route planning and selection (Chapter 4)

Route selection is the logical, systematic approach to determining one course over another; of the infinite routes across a piece of terrain, there is only one most logical route that optimizes the unit's safe and efficient passage. The **eight things to consider** when selecting a route: terrain, weather, avalanche/rock slide potential, snow/melt-off conditions, unit ability, tactical situation/mission, equipment, and time.

**Contour, not azimuth.** In mountainous/cold weather, following a contour line rather than a straight azimuth may be optimal: contouring conserves energy and time, reduces the slope angle of the movement, and avoids severe cross-compartment movement, which can expend energy and time and jeopardize the mission — while moving directly up or down a slope without regard for gradient "could be disastrous."

**Natural lines of drift.** A natural line of drift is a terrain feature that allows ease of movement — the path of least resistance. It saves time and energy, but it also presents an opportunity for a well-trained enemy to establish an ambush; the leader must balance the path of least resistance against the tactical environment, weighing the probability of ambush against the time and energy saved. Altitude and elevation on the route determine the weather, speed of movement, clothing and equipment needed, and the capability of rotary-wing assets for emergency extraction and casualty evacuation. → `references/weather-movement-navigation.md`

## 5. Crossing water obstacles (Chapter 5)

Mountain streams and rivers are military obstacles and danger areas; to reduce time in the danger area, a reconnaissance team precedes the main body and selects the best crossing site. Site selection: look for logjams, rocks, or fallen trees that provide a dry crossing; select a wide, shallow point where the current is slower; avoid sharp bends (deep, with a strong current on the outside); look for a firm, smooth bottom (large rocks give poor footing and turbulence); it may be easier to cross several small channels than one large one; never cross just above rapids, falls, or logjams; cross in the early morning, when the water level is lower after less daylight melting.

**Safety.** There must be a safety line at a minimum of a 45-degree angle downstream, skimming the top of the water — anyone swept downstream can grab it and pull to shore. Safety swimmers are used where Marines can touch bottom, or if someone trips and rolls downstream toward a hazard such as a rock jam or waterfall; in swift water, safety swimmers will not swim to a victim without a belay line, and in training they wear life jackets. A field-expedient throw bag can be made with 550 cord and an isopor mat. Combat safety precautions may differ from training ones — combat safety is just as important, with the added concern of a hostile force. → `references/avalanches-snow-travel-water.md`

## 6. Avalanches (Chapter 6)

**The hazard evaluation process.** Four critical variables determine whether an avalanche is possible — the first three form the "data triangle": (1) analyze the terrain — is it capable of producing avalanches? (2) evaluate the stability of the snowpack — could the snow slide? (3) forecast the weather — is the weather contributing to instability? (4) consider the human factor — without the presence of humans, there is no hazard. Consider the alternatives and their possible consequences.

**Terrain.** Seven factors determine whether a slope can produce an avalanche: slope angle, slope aspect, terrain roughness, slope shape, vegetation, elevation, and path history. **Slope angle is the most important terrain variable.** Slopes under 25 degrees will rarely slide for lack of stress; slab avalanches in cold snow are possible between 25 and 60 degrees; most slab avalanches release on slopes with starting-zone angles between 30 and 45 degrees (the manual gives 35–40 degrees as the most common release range). Thinking avalanches occur only on big slopes is "a very common mistake."

**Snowpack.** The snowpack accumulates layer by layer with each new snow or wind event; different metamorphic types (rounding, faceting, melt-freeze) can occur in different layers at the same time, and weak layers — such as faceted snow formed in cooler temperatures — develop from a lack of solar heat. Slab avalanches occur when one or more layers release; speed ranges from roughly 65 miles per hour for a wet slab to 150 miles per hour for a dry slide. Avalanche danger is worst during spring thaw, especially on certain aspects; avoid V-shaped valleys and travel in wide U-shaped valleys, where avalanches are less channeled. The chapter's full treatment covers avalanche types, snowpack evaluation, and the unit's winter mountain-leader role in hazard assessment and mitigation. → `references/avalanches-snow-travel-water.md`

## 7. Movement by snowshoe (Chapter 7)

Snowshoes provide flotation over snow-covered terrain, conserve valuable energy, and let a Marine move through snow at approximately the same pace as walking on hard ground — though speed varies with the depth and consistency of the snow, and the more compact the snow, the faster the movement. Planning rates are given in Table 7-1 for gentle terrain, with the note to **reduce by one third for mountainous terrain**. Advantages: little training time to gain proficiency, little maintenance, carrying and pulling heavy loads on gentle terrain is relatively easy, and movement in confined areas (bivouac sites, supply dumps) is relatively easy. Disadvantages: movement on moderate to steep slopes is extremely difficult, and movement through thick or cut-off brush is difficult.

The Marine Corps' snowshoe is the **modular snowshoe**, which replaced the old magnesium snowshoe: a detachable 8-inch tail for varying snow conditions and loads, more versatile and maneuverable because of the detachable tail and aggressive steel teeth under the footplate. Parts: tip (front of the frame), tail (detachable 8-inch back portion), and the binding and crampon assembly. Snowshoers take V-shaped steps. → `references/avalanches-snow-travel-water.md`

## 8. Movement by sled (Chapter 8)

Combat operations in snow require Marines to carry increased loads, and sleds are the over-snow load carrier. The chapter covers sled types (including the rectangular basket shape with mesh attached), selection factors drawn from reconnaissance reports or local inhabitants, loading and lashing, and pulling techniques — including the use of a carabiner (if worn or missing, opposing carabiners can be used) and the employment of the sled in unit movement. The sled is a logistics tool as much as a movement tool: it is how a unit moves sustainment loads that no Marine can carry through deep snow. → `references/avalanches-snow-travel-water.md`

## 9. Casualty evacuation in mountain warfare operations (Chapter 9)

**General considerations — "A PASS NGG."** Apply essential first aid (lifesaving steps such as splints or pressure bandages); protect the patient from the elements (insulation from the ground — sleeping bag, field tarpaulin, or bivy cover — warm and dry; move the casualty from natural hazards such as falling rocks or lightning, or ensure he is well protected and helmeted); avoid unnecessary handling; select the easiest route (send scouts ahead to break trails if possible); set up relay points and a warming station (fresh litter teams, a system for raising or lowering, minimum medical personnel to treat shock or hemorrhage and constantly re-evaluate the patient); give litter teams specific, realistic goals — the job is extremely tiring, physically and mentally; gear remains with the casualty throughout the evacuation.

**Expedient litters.** Five types: the sling rope carry, rope coil carry, pole carry, the alpine basket, and the field tarpaulin litter. The **sling rope carry** requires a 15-foot sling rope and two individuals — one bearer, one assistant: the bearer goes on all fours and the assistant places the casualty face down on the bearer's back, armpits even with the bearer's shoulders, the middle of the sling rope between the casualty's shoulders and the ends run to secure him. The chapter details each litter's construction and use, and the raising/lowering systems for steep terrain. → `references/avalanches-snow-travel-water.md`

## 10. Mountain patrolling (Chapter 10)

Mountainous terrain forces decentralized operations by small units. The chapter covers patrol organization, planning, and execution in mountains — including the patrol base, movement discipline, and the individual load: each Marine should carry purification tablets for use with natural water sources. Patrolling in mountains is slower, more exposed, and more dependent on the leader's route and weather judgment than patrolling on flat ground; the chapter's techniques are the application of Chapters 3 and 4 to a tactical patrol. → `references/patrolling-fires-logistics.md`

## 11. Fires (Chapter 11)

Weapons employment in mountains and cold weather: lubricants and other liquids may freeze or become thick or gummy; cold affects weapons, ammunition, and optics; and the chapter covers weapons considerations, fire support, and the effects of altitude and cold on ballistics and equipment — the fires counterpart to the movement and logistics chapters. → `references/patrolling-fires-logistics.md`

## 12. Logistics and sustainment (Chapter 12)

"Logistic planning is good leadership" and "a sound logistic plan is essential to mission success" — failures in logistic planning "can be lethal in a mountainous/cold weather environment." Leaders should constantly ask: what does the unit need, and how do supplies and equipment get to the unit to achieve the mission tomorrow, next week, next month? Supply routes are predetermined; transport modes are air, helicopter, motor vehicles, and animal packing. **Air delivery:** fixed-wing aircraft can airdrop supplies where surface resupply is not feasible, with loads as heavy as 10,000 pounds delivered an average of 150 meters from the target from altitudes as high as 25,000 feet. The most recent technology is the **Joint Precision Airdrop System (JPADS)** — GPS-guided parachute systems in 750-pound, 2,000-pound, and 10,000-pound versions used by the Marine Corps, each a canopy plus an airborne guidance unit. → `references/patrolling-fires-logistics.md`

## 13. Mountain safety and health considerations (Chapter 13)

**Prevention is fitness, nutrition, water, and hygiene.** Physical fitness is the foundation — the more fit the Marine, the easier movement, load carriage, and adaptation to mountain combat stress. Nutrition: at sea level in garrison a Marine typically requires about 2,000 calories a day; at high altitude, in the mountains, and especially in winter, **at least 4,500 calories a day** — four MREs or three MCWs (Meal, Cold Weather) a day provides sufficient vitamins and calories. Increased carbohydrate intake (sugar, bread, rice, pasta) is recommended, since carbohydrates are more easily converted into heat energy and aid acclimatization. **Water:** intake is at least **3 to 6 quarts per day** (including water in food), and may be as high as **6 to 8 quarts per day** during intense physical activity; the body loses liquid exceptionally fast in mountain/cold environments through evaporation, exertion, and low humidity, and through cold- and altitude-increased urination and humidifying cold dry air. Thirst is not a good indicator — if Marines are thirsty, it is too late. Watch urine color: keep it clear; the more yellow, the more they need to drink. Coffee and tea are mild diuretics. To treat dehydration in the field, give at least 6 quarts of water per day; severe cases may require intravenous fluids. **Hygiene:** poor hygiene in the field can have fatal consequences through skin and other infections and intestinal illness, and cold-weather inconvenience makes Marines neglect washing — maintaining hygiene is "paramount."

**Injuries.** The most prevalent: dehydration, heat cramps, heat exhaustion, heat stroke, acute mountain sickness (AMS), high altitude cerebral edema (HACE), high altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE), splenic syndrome, hypothermia, frostbite, trench/immersion foot, snow blindness, and carbon monoxide poisoning. **Heat cramps** are painful skeletal-muscle spasms from excessive salt loss. **AMS** is treated with rest and descent — drugs for AMS are Motrin or aspirin for the headache — and Marines must not ascend any higher, since AMS may progress to HACE; **HACE is fatal if not treated**, and the HACE patient is placed in a portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag) to improve the condition. **Hypothermia** is treated by rewarming — the only absolute cure — and hypothermia can kill faster than starvation. **Frostbite** is a constant danger: check for signs, and remember that frostbite occurs in 15 minutes or less under severe windchill. The chapter's safety principles include tempering confidence with a realistic appraisal of the situation and the unit's capability — overconfidence is itself a hazard. → `references/health-clothing-bivouac-camouflage.md`

## 14. Cold weather clothing and equipment (Chapter 14)

The objective of the clothing system is not comfort but safety. The **Marine Corps Cold Weather Clothing System (M/CWCS)** is based on a layering principle, flexible enough to ventilate excess heat and insulate against the cold. The principles of use are remembered as **COLD**: **C**lean — keep clothing clean (warm air trapped against the body and in the fabric is what keeps a Marine warm); **O**verheating — avoid overheating (remove layers at the first sensation of sweating); **L**oose and Layered — clothes comfortably loose (tight clothing constricts blood flow to the extremities; several thin layers trap more warm air than one thick layer); **D**ry — keep clothing dry (wet clothing is less effective; put on a shell/protective layer in wet conditions or walking through wet snow).

**Design layers.** Three principle layers: the **vapor transmission layer** (also called the sweat transfer layer — draws moisture away from the skin to keep the wearer dry and warm); the **insulating layer** (one or several layers holding warm air around the body — preferably lightweight, very compressible, fast drying); and the **protective layer** (protects the insulating layer from becoming wet or dirty). The chapter reviews each item in the system, plus over-the-snow mobility equipment — skis, snowshoes, sleds, combat boots, vapor barrier boots, and ski/march boots — and cold-weather care of equipment, including communications equipment and the windchill threat to helicopter operations. → `references/health-clothing-bivouac-camouflage.md`

## 15. Defensive positions and field fortifications (Chapter 15)

Defensive positions are inherently linked to bivouac sites. Positions are sited for concealment and protection: use shaded areas to provide concealment and to prevent the sun from melting snow/ice protection; use the leeward sides of mountains, where there is less wind and deeper snow for defensive positions and water supply; stay off the valley floor, where cold air settles during windless periods. → `references/health-clothing-bivouac-camouflage.md`

## 16. Bivouac routine in snow (Chapter 16)

The efficient and tactically sound establishment of a bivouac site is fundamental and critical to success in mountain/cold weather warfighting, and more complex than a customary bivouac. A tactically sound site should be well clear of any suspected avalanche site or runout zone; a good defensive position; large enough to contain the entire unit; and in a forested area (natural cover and concealment). Establishment follows a fixed sequence: **security, track plan, defensive positions, living areas, and specific use areas** — failure to address each may unnecessarily expose Marines to the enemy or the weather. A track plan in snow is established during the unit leader's reconnaissance. The chapter's guidance should be incorporated into unit SOPs and become routine; Appendix D holds the bivouac checklist. → `references/health-clothing-bivouac-camouflage.md`

## 17. Camouflage, cover, and concealment (Chapter 17)

In snow-covered terrain, the stark contrast between light and dark emphasizes anything that does not blend naturally, and every movement by vehicles or dismounted troops leaves readily identifiable tracks in the snow — detailed intelligence for an enemy. Before any movement, **track planning** (balancing a minimal track pattern with a track deception plan) must be instituted with high priority. Backgrounds in snow are not all white: rocks, bushes, trees, and shadows sharply contrast with the snow, and snowy wooded terrain viewed from the air has a high proportion of dark areas. Individual concealment requires knowing the terrain, adjusting clothing to the surroundings, and moving in the least obvious manner. By combining outerwear, **four camouflage combinations** are available: all green, all white, white over green, and green over white — the basics the individual Marine builds on with initiative and imagination for thickly wooded areas and other terrain. Light and noise discipline are covered alongside. → `references/health-clothing-bivouac-camouflage.md`

## 18. Mountain warfare skill sets, capabilities, and training requirements (Chapter 18)

There is currently no secondary military occupational specialty for military mountaineers, but several core-plus courses provide specific mountain warfare instruction, and units can identify trained personnel by school codes. **Summer mountain leaders (M7A)** are the unit's subject matter experts for operations in complex, compartmentalized, and mountainous terrain — including high to very high altitude, alpine, and glaciated terrain: they plan, organize, and lead mountain/cold weather operations; plan and lead cliff assaults; plan and lead long-range patrols on complex ridgelines for picketing, overwatch, and reconnaissance, and control fires; train their units; and advise MAGTF commanders and staffs. They are recommended as assault climber platoon commanders and platoon sergeants for Marine expeditionary units — three summer mountain leaders per reconnaissance platoon is the manual's recommendation. Winter mountain leaders (M7B) are the cold-weather counterparts. The chapter gives the recommended number of trained personnel by unit size, the school codes, and the descriptions of each course, covering tactical considerations, navigation, weather forecasting and route planning, weapons employment, fire support and communications, and operations on or across glaciers, steep earth, and near-vertical to vertical rock. → `references/health-clothing-bivouac-camouflage.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Weather first, always.** Evaluate current, recent, and forecast weather constantly — it decides what the mountain allows.
2. **Contour, don't fight the gradient.** A contour route saves energy and time; a straight azimuth up a slope can be disastrous.
3. **Slope angle is the avalanche variable.** Under 25° rarely slides; most slabs release at 30–45°; the human factor makes it a hazard.
4. **Rest step and 50 percent.** Lock the rear leg out; energy saved is mission saved.
5. **Drink on schedule, eat 4,500.** Thirst is too late in the mountains; calories are heat.
6. **COLD clothing.** Clean, avoid Overheating, Loose and Layered, Dry.
7. **Current doctrine governs.** MCRP 12-10A.1 W/CH-1 (2026) is the newer edition; this 2013 skill is the baseline and the deep reference.
