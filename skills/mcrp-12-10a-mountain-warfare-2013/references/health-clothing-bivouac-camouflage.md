# Health, Clothing, Bivouac, Camouflage — MCRP 12-10A.1 (Chapters 13–18)

## Safety and health (Chapter 13)

**Prevention — fitness, nutrition, water, hygiene.** Fitness is the foundation. Nutrition: ~2,000 calories/day in garrison at sea level; **at least 4,500 calories/day** at altitude in winter — four MREs or three MCWs a day. More carbohydrates (sugar, bread, rice, pasta) — easily converted to heat energy, aiding acclimatization. **Water: at least 3–6 quarts/day** (including water in food), up to **6–8 quarts/day** in intense activity. Thirst is a late indicator — if thirsty, it is too late. Watch urine color (keep it clear). Coffee and tea are mild diuretics. Field treatment of dehydration: at least 6 quarts of water per day; severe cases may need IV fluids. Hygiene is paramount — cold-weather inconvenience invites neglect, and poor hygiene can be fatal through skin and intestinal infections.

**Injuries:** dehydration, heat cramps (painful muscle spasms from salt loss), heat exhaustion, heat stroke, acute mountain sickness (AMS), high altitude cerebral edema (HACE), high altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE), splenic syndrome, hypothermia, frostbite, trench/immersion foot, snow blindness, carbon monoxide poisoning. **AMS:** rest and descent; Motrin or aspirin for headache; do not ascend higher — AMS may progress to HACE. **HACE is fatal if not treated** — place the patient in a portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag). **Hypothermia:** rewarming is the only absolute cure; it can kill faster than starvation. **Frostbite:** constant danger; occurs in 15 minutes or less under severe windchill. Overconfidence, untempered by a realistic appraisal of the situation and the unit's capability, is itself a hazard.

## Cold weather clothing and equipment (Chapter 14)

Objective is safety, not comfort. The **Marine Corps Cold Weather Clothing System (M/CWCS)** is a layering system. Principles remembered as **COLD**: **C**lean (warm air trapped in fabric keeps the Marine warm); **O**verheating — avoid it (remove layers at the first sensation of sweating); **L**oose and Layered (tight clothing constricts blood flow; several thin layers beat one thick layer); **D**ry (wet clothing is less effective; wear a shell in wet conditions or wet snow). Design layers: **vapor transmission layer** (sweat transfer — draws moisture away from skin); **insulating layer** (holds warm air — lightweight, compressible, fast drying); **protective layer** (keeps the insulating layer dry and clean). Also covers over-the-snow mobility equipment (skis, snowshoes, sleds, combat boots, vapor barrier boots, ski/march boots) and cold-weather equipment care.

## Defensive positions (Chapter 15)

Positions are linked to bivouac sites: shaded areas for concealment and to keep the sun from melting snow/ice protection; leeward sides of mountains (less wind, deeper snow for positions and water supply); off the valley floor (cold air settles there in windless periods).

## Bivouac routine in snow (Chapter 16)

Site must be well clear of avalanche sites/runout zones; a good defensive position; large enough for the whole unit; in a forested area (natural cover and concealment). Establishment sequence: **security, track plan, defensive positions, living areas, specific use areas**. Track plan established during the leader's reconnaissance. Incorporate into unit SOPs; Appendix D holds the bivouac checklist.

## Camouflage, cover, concealment (Chapter 17)

Snow's light-dark contrast emphasizes anything that does not blend; every movement leaves tracks — detailed intelligence for an enemy. **Track planning** (minimal track pattern plus track deception plan) is high priority before any movement. Backgrounds are not all white: rocks, bushes, trees, shadows contrast sharply. Four outerwear combinations: **all green, all white, white over green, green over white**. Light and noise discipline included.

## Mountain warfare skill sets (Chapter 18)

No secondary MOS for mountaineers, but core-plus courses exist, identified by school codes. **Summer mountain leaders (M7A):** unit subject-matter experts for complex, compartmentalized, mountainous terrain (high to very high altitude, alpine, glaciated) — plan, organize, and lead mountain/cold weather operations; plan/lead cliff assaults; plan/lead long-range patrols on complex ridgelines (picketing, overwatch, reconnaissance, control of fires); train their units; advise MAGTF commanders and staffs. Recommended as assault climber platoon commanders/sergeants for MEUs — three per reconnaissance platoon. Winter mountain leaders (M7B) are the cold-weather counterparts. The chapter gives recommended numbers by unit size, school codes, and course descriptions (tactics, navigation, weather forecasting, route planning, weapons, fire support, communications, glacier and vertical-rock operations).
