# Weather, Movement, Navigation — MCRP 12-10A.1 (Chapters 1–4)

## Mountain weather (Chapter 1)

Constantly evaluate: current weather, recent weather and when it last changed, next forecasted change. **Anabatic winds** blow up valleys (usually light); **katabatic winds** blow down valley slopes as air cools (occasionally strong). **Jet stream:** high-speed winds near the tropopause, generally from the west, often exceeding 250 mph. Wind speed by observation (Beaufort scale: 0–1 mph smoke rises straight up; 2–3 mph smoke drifts) combined with temperature gives the **windchill index** (National Weather Service table) — frostbite in 15 minutes or less under severe windchill. Cloud types signal weather: anvil-shaped clouds produce precipitation; stratus forms from rising moist air.

## Command and control (Chapter 2)

Leadership is vital in all environments, particularly in extreme mountain conditions, where small units operate decentralized. Covers command relationships and control measures, communications in mountainous terrain, and the MCMWTC training pipeline — how units request training and use qualified summer and winter mountain leaders as force multipliers.

## Movement and navigation (Chapter 3)

Individual movement in mountains is considerably more strenuous than on flat terrain. Fundamentals: body weight over the feet at all times (weight on the skeleton, not the muscles); avoid up-and-down microterrain; step over rather than on top of obstacles; **rest step** — lock the rear leg out each step, resting weight on the skeletal frame, reducing energy expenditure by up to **50 percent**.

Special techniques: **herringbone** (toes outward, flat feet, ascending — walking on toes tires calves); **half-herringbone** (traversing up/down, ankles rolled away from the hill, uphill foot in direction of travel); **plunge step** (down loose slopes — face downhill, step assertively away, land solidly on the heel with the leg vertical). Ski poles transfer weight from the lower body to the poles; on a traverse, hold the uphill pole along the shaft, not the grip. Navigation: compass direction method; a feature recognizable on the map may not be recognizable on the ground.

## Route planning and selection (Chapter 4)

Eight considerations: terrain, weather, avalanche/rock slide potential, snow/melt-off conditions, unit ability, tactical situation/mission, equipment, time. **Contour, not azimuth:** following a contour line conserves energy and time and reduces slope angle; severe cross-compartment movement can jeopardize the mission, and moving directly up or down a slope without regard for gradient "could be disastrous." **Natural lines of drift** (path of least resistance) save time and energy but invite ambush — weigh ambush probability against time and energy saved. Altitude/elevation determines weather, speed, clothing/equipment, and rotary-wing CASEVAC capability.
