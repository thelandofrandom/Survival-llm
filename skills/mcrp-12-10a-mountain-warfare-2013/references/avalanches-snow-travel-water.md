# Avalanches, Snow Travel, Water, CASEVAC — MCRP 12-10A.1 (Chapters 5–9)

## Crossing water obstacles (Chapter 5)

Reconnaissance team precedes the main body to select the crossing site. Prefer logjams, rocks, or fallen trees for a dry crossing; otherwise a wide, shallow point with slower current; avoid sharp bends (deep, strong current outside); firm, smooth bottom; several small channels may beat one large one; never cross just above rapids, falls, or logjams; cross in the early morning when the water level is lower.

**Safety line:** minimum 45-degree angle downstream, skimming the water — anyone swept downstream grabs it and pulls to shore. Safety swimmers where Marines can touch bottom or if someone rolls toward a hazard; in swift water they will not swim to a victim without a belay line; in training they wear life jackets. Field-expedient throw bag: 550 cord and an isopor mat.

## Avalanches (Chapter 6)

**Hazard evaluation — four variables:** (1) terrain — can it produce avalanches? (2) snowpack stability — could it slide? (3) weather — is it contributing to instability? (4) human factor — without humans present, there is no hazard. Terrain factors: slope angle, aspect, roughness, shape, vegetation, elevation, path history. **Slope angle is the most important variable:** under 25° rarely slides; slab avalanches in cold snow possible 25–60°; most release at 30–45° (commonly 35–40°). Snowpack accumulates layer by layer; weak layers (e.g., faceted snow) form without solar heat. Slab avalanche speed: ~65 mph (wet slab) to 150 mph (dry slide). Worst danger in spring thaw; avoid V-shaped valleys, travel in wide U-shaped valleys.

## Movement by snowshoe (Chapter 7)

Flotation over snow; pace about the same as walking on hard ground, varying with snow depth and consistency. Planning rates in Table 7-1 for gentle terrain — **reduce by one third for mountainous terrain**. Advantages: little training, little maintenance, heavy loads easy on gentle terrain, good in confined areas. Disadvantages: moderate-to-steep slopes extremely difficult; thick brush difficult. The **modular snowshoe** replaced the magnesium snowshoe: detachable 8-inch tail, aggressive steel teeth under the footplate. Snowshoers take V-shaped steps.

## Movement by sled (Chapter 8)

Sleds carry the increased loads snow operations require. Covers sled types (including the rectangular basket with mesh), selection factors from reconnaissance or local inhabitants, loading and lashing, and pulling techniques (carabiner use; opposing carabiners if one is worn or missing).

## Casualty evacuation (Chapter 9)

**"A PASS NGG":** Apply essential first aid; Protect the patient from the elements (insulation from the ground — sleeping bag, field tarpaulin, bivy cover — warm and dry; move from falling rocks/lightning or helmet the patient); Avoid unnecessary handling; Select the easiest route (scouts ahead to break trails); Set up relay points and a warming station (fresh litter teams, raising/lowering systems, minimum medical personnel to treat shock/hemorrhage and re-evaluate); give litter teams specific, realistic goals; Gear remains with the casualty.

**Five expedient litters:** sling rope carry, rope coil carry, pole carry, alpine basket, field tarpaulin litter. **Sling rope carry:** 15-foot sling rope, two people — bearer on all fours, casualty face down on the bearer's back, armpits even with the bearer's shoulders, sling rope middle between the casualty's shoulders.
