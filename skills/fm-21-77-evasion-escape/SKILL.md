# FM 21-77 Evasion and Escape

## Purpose

FM 21-77, *Evasion and Escape* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, May 1958 — title page dated 19 December 1958, 176 pages) — the Army's first comprehensive evasion and escape manual, drawn from the two world wars and Korea. A commander's guide to evasion and escape training: the legal status of evaders and escapees, the Code of Conduct, short- and long-range evasion, contacting friendly forces, capture and early escape, POW camp life, resisting interrogation and indoctrination, and escape from camp — plus a training program and field exercise in the appendices.

**Historic material — read this first.** This is a 1958 Cold War manual, and much of it is period-bound. Its adversary is the communist bloc (Soviet Union, Communist China, North Korea); its POW-camp, interrogation, and indoctrination chapters describe communist practice as understood from World War II and Korea. Its area hints (Ch. 4, §43) name specific 1950s populations — dissident peasants in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, and Hungary; Baltic nationalists; Ukrainian nationalists; Muslims and Taoists in Manchuria — and are historical, not current area intelligence. For current doctrine, use the **`sere` skill (AFH 10-644, *SERE Operations*, 2017)** and the **`survival-evasion-recovery` skill (MCRP 3-02H)**.

**A gap in the source.** Many escape details are not in this volume: paragraphs 89–94, 96–97, 99–105, and 107 (escape committee, methods, tunneling, escape aids and tools, identity documents, maps, photographs, stamps, casting, clothing, rollcall cover-up) read "See paragraph [n], FM 21-77A" — the classified supplement, not in the vault. This skill distills only what the unclassified manual actually contains and says so where the supplement is the authority.

## 1. Purpose, Definitions, and Why Evasion Training Exists

**Purpose and scope.** A guide to commanders for training and indoctrination in evasion and escape, applicable "without modification to both nuclear and nonnuclear warfare." Area-specific intelligence is deliberately excluded — it was to be given to the individual soldier through special briefings after arrival overseas.

**The necessity for training.** "That some men were captured in Korea when ways to avoid it existed indicates clearly the necessity for training." Troops must be trained to recognize the most feasible alternative, and convinced that while a means to avoid capture exists, surrender is not to be accepted. "The evasion area is merely an extension of the battlefield."

**Definitions (Ch. 1, ¶3).** **Evader:** one cut off behind enemy lines who eludes capture, with or without organized assistance. **Escapee:** one physically captured who frees himself. **Returnee:** one who eluded the enemy with no assistance and infiltrated to his own troops alone or with other returnees. **Short-range evasion:** avoiding capture in the enemy combat zone (e.g., units bypassed during a breakthrough). **Long-range evasion:** surviving and avoiding capture while traveling far behind enemy lines (e.g., an escapee from a POW camp, downed aircrew). **Breakout:** a surrounded unit or individual disengaging to return to friendly lines. **Infiltration:** returning to friendly lines through enemy-held areas, usually by stealth and deception. **Brainwashing:** a calculated attempt to distort convictions — "a carefully planned, well-thought-out educational process," not a "third degree."

**Six reasons for the program (Ch. 2, ¶6).** (1) Humanitarian — communist imprisonment meant slave labor, bare subsistence, and cruelty. (2) Morale — a man reported missing who returns raises the whole unit. (3) Preservation of manpower — ~2,000 airmen recovered pre-Normandy in World War II at ~$30,000 of training each, about $60,000,000 regained. (4) Safeguarding military information — fewer captures mean fewer interrogations. (5) Impeding the enemy — every soldier searching for evaders is one not attacking or defending. (6) Collection of information — evaders report what they see: enemy units, equipment, installations, morale.

**History in brief (Ch. 2, §II).** World War I: ~107,000 Allied POWs escaped from German camps. World War II: the British began organized evasion planning early; the American organization became operational 1 January 1943 in G-2, War Department General Staff. Korea: thousands of unbriefed Americans were cut off; very few attempted to evade or escape, and captured Chinese documents showed some revealing information without compulsion. In February 1951 the Department of the Army established an evasion and escape training program; after the truce, repatriated POWs' conduct led to the Code of Conduct as a Presidential Executive Order.

## 2. Legal Status and the Code of Conduct

**Legal status (Ch. 2, ¶5).** Evaders' and escapees' rights are defined in the Hague Convention of 1907 and the Geneva Conventions of 1929, restated in 1949. Article 17: a POW is bound to give only surname, first name, rank, date of birth, and serial number — and may not be mistreated or coerced for more. Article 42: weapons against POWs, especially escaping ones, are an extreme measure always preceded by warnings.

**The Code of Conduct (Ch. 2, §III).** Prescribed by Executive Order 10631, 17 August 1955. The governing idea: the POW camp is an extension of the battlefield, and the prisoner must continue fighting with any means available. Capture does not relieve an officer or NCO of the responsibilities of rank: the senior must assume command of all American POWs.

- **I — "I am an American, fighting in the forces which guard my country and our way of life. I am prepared to give my life in their defense."** A fighting man always; duty to oppose the enemy regardless of circumstances — "I am prepared to give my life" does not require needless sacrifice.
- **II — "I will never surrender of my own free will. If in command, I will never surrender my men while they still have the means to resist."** When isolated and no longer able to inflict casualties, the duty is to evade capture and rejoin friendly forces.
- **III — "If I am captured I will continue to resist by all means available. I will make every effort to escape and aid others to escape. I will accept neither parole nor special favors from the enemy."** Escape if able; assist others; never sign a parole agreement.
- **IV — "If I become a prisoner of war, I will keep faith with my fellow prisoners… If I am senior, I will take command."** Informing on fellow prisoners is forbidden. The senior line officer or NCO assumes command by rank without regard to Service; if that cannot be effected, an elected-representative or covert organization is formed. Hygiene, camp sanitation, and care of the sick and wounded are imperative.
- **V — "When questioned, should I become a prisoner of war, I am bound to give only name, rank, service number, and date of birth… I will make no oral or written statements disloyal to my country."** Confessions, questionnaires, personal-history statements, propaganda recordings and broadcasts, appeals, self-criticisms, and signatures to peace or surrender appeals are forbidden. Under Communist Bloc reservations to the Geneva Convention, a signed confession was likely to be used to convict the prisoner as a war criminal, removing POW status.
- **VI — "I will never forget that I am an American, fighting man, responsible for my actions, and dedicated to the principles which made my country free. I will trust in my God and in the United States of America."** The UCMJ continues to apply to POWs; conduct is examined after repatriation.

Training in support of the Code must instill the will to avoid capture, resist interrogation and indoctrination, and escape — and assure the soldier he will not be forgotten, that every national means will be used to contact, support, and release him, and that U.S. law provides for his dependents. → `references/code-of-conduct-pow-camp.md`

## 3. Short-Range Evasion — The Isolated Unit

**Short vs. long range (Ch. 3, ¶12).** No time or distance line separates them; the difference is conditions. Short-range evasion occurs in the forward combat zone within hours or perhaps days of isolation, while the soldier or small unit is still in good physical condition, with adequate food and clothing, generally oriented — the long-range evader, usually an escaped POW, operates over strange terrain with little or no food or equipment.

**The isolation hazard (Ch. 3, ¶¶13–15).** Studies of World War II and Korea showed the greatest POW losses occurred in isolated units: about three-fourths of the men captured in Korea reported their unit had been isolated by enemy action, and a comparable portion in World War II. Capture often results from giving up before all alternatives are tried; hours to days usually elapsed before capture, time enough to attempt another course.

**Five courses of action (Ch. 3, ¶16).** (a) Defense of present position. (b) Breakout to areas under friendly control. (c) Evasion by infiltration. (d) Deeper penetration to conduct guerrilla operations. (e) Any combination.

**Defense of present position (¶17).** Sometimes there is no alternative — a position ordered held at all costs must be held until relief or the order is rescinded. Examples: Company B, 109th Infantry, 28th Division — 75 men in the Hürtgen Forest held an isolated position against superior German forces for 3 days until relieved; near Bastogne, one rifle company holding a roadblock delayed two German divisions for almost two days, the Germans halting to study the unexplained American unit.

**Breakout (¶18).** Fighting through to friendly territory. Success depends on leaders who act rapidly to find enemy weaknesses, know the terrain, and choose a simple plan using a route and time calculated to catch the enemy off guard. Destroy ammunition and equipment that cannot be evacuated.

**Evasion by infiltration (¶19).** For separated elements or small cut-off groups, often the best means home. Plan carefully: present location; direction and distance to friendly lines; enemy disposition and movement (observe from concealment — forward displacement of artillery indicates the enemy is still moving forward); terrain and obstacles (avoid ridgelines, bridges, roads, and towns; choose a route needing minimum exertion, avoiding strong points); weather and visibility (darkness is the strongest ally; travel only at night, preferably after midnight; near the front, move just after sunset or just before sunrise, light enough to avoid minefields yet dark enough to prevent detection).

**Deeper penetration — guerrilla operations (¶20).** Sometimes the best way to escape: communist offensives stressed superiority of more than four to one and encirclement, so the bulk of the enemy force sweeps past the isolated unit, leaving its rear lightly manned by service troops. The isolated unit must know guerrilla operations, select an operating area offering concealment and living off the land, and exploit opportunities — destroying lines of communication, attacking headquarters, collecting information.

**Adapting the plan; unlimited alternatives (¶¶21–22).** The situation changes, often rapidly; the ability to adopt a new alternative without delay is the key — during the Battle of the Bulge, December 1944, several hundred scattered men of an infantry division under a resourceful officer defended as a single force as long as possible, then split into small groups to infiltrate to Allied lines; most succeeded. Any action is justified so long as it does not violate the Geneva Convention — e.g., never misuse the Red Cross emblem.

**Factors influencing the course of action (¶23).** Four questions: (1) Are we really isolated? — confusion, darkness, and poor information can create the illusion; verify by reconnaissance before breaking up the unit. (2) Can we still communicate with higher headquarters or adjacent units? (3) Can the commander still control his unit? (4) To what extent do casualties affect the course? — never routinely abandon casualties; care for and evacuate them whenever possible, but under some conditions men who remain with the wounded may jeopardize the wounded, themselves, and the unit — then leave supplies and, if possible, medical personnel with the wounded. → `references/short-range-evasion.md`

## 4. Long-Range Evasion — Will, Planning, Travel

**The evader's problems (Ch. 4, ¶¶24–25).** Long-range evasion: usually escaped POWs, in poor physical condition after camp, with little or no food or equipment, who must live off the land or, as a last resort, seek native assistance. FM 21-76 covers living off the land; the evader also needs area background: topography, language, population and customs, communications, transportation, internal affairs.

**The will to survive (¶¶26–28).** "The will to survive, coupled with your ability to withstand hardships and overcome obstacles, is the key to successful long-range evasion." Sixteen factors sustain the will: planning; action; effort and determination; training and basic skills; control of panic; group integrity and solidarity; strong leadership; the stronger helping the weaker; mutual support and the buddy system; patience ("sweat it out"); endurance and physical condition; release of emotions; responsibility and loyalty; religious faith and prayer; family and home ties; and hate and revenge.

**Plan evasion and escape together (¶29).** Plan the early phases of long-range evasion concurrently with the escape, in the camp. Recapture of escapees is largely attributable to lack of information about enemy security measures immediately outside the camp. Establish a starting point before the escape and move rapidly to it on clearing the camp; select alternates; if several prisoners escape, starting points serve as rally points.

**Courses of action (¶¶30–34).** (a) **Infiltration** — the most successful course for the individual or small group far behind enemy lines: complete the journey — possibly hundreds of miles over several months — without contacting natives, living off the land; travel in groups of 2–4, ranking officer or NCO in command. (b) **Deception** — disguise and cover stories; mandatory where contact with civilians is unavoidable (especially in Asia), and the most difficult tactic; more successful in Europe than in Asia given differences in features, language, and customs. (c) **Guerrilla-type operations** — not practical for individuals or small groups given the minor damage they can inflict, but not precluded (see ¶20).

**Planning, information, sabotage (¶¶35–37).** Planning must be as detailed as knowledge of the enemy situation permits, continuous and flexible. Information is a byproduct — never jeopardize personal safety or compromise cover to collect it.

**Orientation and cross-country travel (¶¶38–41).** Orient by sun and watch — midway between the hour hand and 1200 is south in the Northern Hemisphere — or by the Big Dipper's pointers, about five times the pointer spacing to the North Star, with Cassiopeia as a backup. Avoid major roads and populated areas of all types, even at the cost of extra time and distance: **"As a general rule, the longest route between two evasion march objectives is the safest route."** Use cover and concealment; move rapidly across exposed areas — "If the enemy can't see you, he can't capture you." Unimproved roads in remote areas may be followed, or paralleled; avoid crossroads and junctions.

**Travel in populated areas; area hints (¶¶42–43).** Deception requires a yes to five questions: (1) Can I pass as a native? (2) Is my language/dialect acceptable? (3) Are my credentials and clothing acceptable? (4) Do I have sufficient local money? (5) Is my cover story plausible under local police examination and trained counterintelligence interrogation? If yes to all, deception offers the fastest evasion route.

**Native assistance — last resort (¶¶44–46).** Avoid people as long as possible; seek native assistance only when sickness, lack of food, or other cogent reasons make it impossible to proceed alone. **Who:** avoid the young (thoroughly indoctrinated) and the rich (usually government officials); safer contacts are farmers, shepherds, itinerant workers, isolated travelers; Christians in communist-dominated countries are valuable, lower clergy less risky than higher; native women are described as unreliable contacts — deal courteously, businesslike, briefly. **Where:** never in a group or in a village or town; approach a native alone, in an isolated area, around dusk, just after he rises, while working in his field, or on an isolated road.

## 5. Contacting Friendly and Neutral Elements

**The crucial phase (Ch. 5, ¶¶47–48).** Establishing contact with friendly lines or crossing to a neutral country is the most crucial point of the evasion — all the patience, planning, and hardship are wasted if caution is thrown away in sight of friendly forces. Regular patrols are given the challenge and password of the day and their entry time and place are known; the evader has none of that unless he rejoins within 24 hours of separation.

**Friendly patrols and outposts (¶¶49–51).** Watch from a concealed observation point covering the battle zone; memorize terrain features so you can infiltrate to friendly positions in darkness. Let a patrol come to you if possible; at 25–50 yards, display a white cloth and call out in unmistakably American English. Beyond 50 yards a reconnaissance patrol may avoid contact; closer than 25 yards a member may fire instantly.

**Rescue by aircraft; neutral countries; debriefing (¶¶52–55).** Rotary-wing aircraft are best suited for rescue behind enemy lines — they fly at extremely low altitude, pass through valleys, and can rescue without landing. Without signal panels, improvise the standard SOS distress signal from local materials; a civil aeronautics signal card recommends signals 10 feet high, but in hostile territory size must be modified — in Korea some evaders used small scarves, panels, or rocks.

## 6. Capture, Early Escape, and the POW Camp

**Prevention of capture; early escape (Ch. 6, ¶¶56–60).** Capture is neither dishonorable nor heroic; at times unavoidable. But a majority of those taken into custody could have avoided capture by applying basic evasion tactics — capture is "something like a disease that is 90 percent preventable." If captured despite all efforts, every waking moment must be spent developing and executing a plan of escape — for yourself and for other prisoners. Escape is a duty (Code of Conduct, Article III). Remain alert — each opportunity may be the last. (Specific early-escape opportunities are in FM 21-77A, ¶2.)

**POW camp organization and typical camps (Ch. 7, ¶¶61–68).** Under the Geneva Convention of 1949: where there are no officers, the prisoners' representative is elected by secret ballot every 6 months; in officers' or mixed camps (excluding labor camps), the senior officer is the representative; in labor camps with officers for administrative duties, the representative is elected. Typical communist camps: remote, long low wooden shacks in rows, tiered bunks, substandard heat, cooking, water, toilets; armed guards, trained dogs, searchlights, guard towers; informers used to penetrate clandestine organizations; prisoners segregated by nationality, rank, race, and susceptibility to indoctrination. Covert organizations are covered in FM 21-77A, ¶3.

**Survival in the camp (¶¶69–74).** The will to survive must be kept alive through self-discipline and mutual aid; hardships and pleasures shared; the burden of the weak, wounded, or demoralized carried in common. Humor is important: "you must laugh to sustain your will to survive" — case histories show POWs who died with nothing organically wrong, having simply lost the will to survive. Survival checklist: leadership; discipline; morale; control fear; keep on your feet; eat everything edible; humor; self-aid; common sense; will to survive. POW handicrafts are in FM 21-77A, ¶4.

**Tactical interrogation (¶¶75–76).** Newly captured prisoners face early interrogation in the combat zone. In World War II, German prisoners taken by the Russians were interrogated ~30 minutes at battalion on immediate tactical matters, in more detail at regiment on Order of Battle, further at division (officers and NCOs), and at army level got a ~140-question questionnaire on strategic and personal subjects. In Korea, tactical interrogation covered unit identifications, strength, reserves, defenses, and minefields. The prisoner's authorized response: **name, rank, service number, date of birth, and nothing more** — silence is the most effective defense.

## 7. Enemy Interrogation and Indoctrination

**Strategic interrogation (Ch. 8, ¶¶77–78).** Beyond tactical matters, the enemy seeks strategic information on conditions in the United States and the U.S. Army, and the prisoner's attitudes and susceptibility to communist influence. A prepared questionnaire at army level or en route to camp covers: the POW's whole past life; the condition of the U.S. Armed Forces, with an elaborate quiz on his career; conditions at home; political attitudes; and attitude toward communist propaganda. Giving information does not improve comfort: "No matter how friendly the interrogators appear before you talk, they invariably despise you afterwards."

**Resisting interrogation (¶79).** The first line of defense is military bearing, silence, and trust in yourself, the Army, your country, and your God. Give only name, rank, service number, and date of birth; be respectful but not obsequiously polite (apparent willingness to cooperate prolongs the inquisition); acting stupid may get you dismissed as a waste of time, while appearing educated gets you passed higher. Beware of "stool pigeons" and camp medical personnel collecting information; if you can speak the enemy's language, **do not disclose it** — using an interpreter gains time and lets you overhear enemy soldiers. Never believe statements that your friends have talked; never give information about other POWs, and warn a fellow POW if the enemy shows interest in him; never boast of exploits, true or fabricated — "a prisoner in Russia has only one effective means of defense and that is absolute silence toward everyone."

**Political nature; methods of persuasion (¶¶80–82).** Communist interrogation is preoccupied with politics throughout: rooting out bitter enemies and discovering possible friends; indoctrination begins as early as possible. Russians show wide mood swings from exuberant cordiality to gloomy depression and cruelty, a superior attitude compensating for a "vast national inferiority complex," and an atmosphere of distrust in which failure is treated as a crime tantamount to treason — interrogators go to any lengths for information or a signed confession.

**Indoctrination (Ch. 9, ¶¶83–86).** The communist indoctrination program aimed to develop propaganda, gain control over prisoners, and convert the POW to communist ideology. The only effective defense is don't talk. Three techniques: **repetition** — same propaganda and questions for days, weeks, months; the "big lie" told often enough (prisoners with no education beyond sixth grade could recite long essays on communism yet not a line of the Bill of Rights); **harassment** — a precise, unvarying schedule, odd-hour summons, trumped-up charges, extracted "confessions" of self-criticism; **humiliation** — breaking personal pride before other prisoners (one prisoner was made to write and publicly read a "confession" that he stole a cucumber because stealing was part of the American way of life), then recruiting the humiliated as informants, threatening them with signed confessions and secret recordings sent to the United States.

## 8. Escape from a POW Camp

**The value of escape (Ch. 10, ¶87).** "The best defense is a good offense" — carry that aggressive fighting spirit into the POW camp; escape is a duty, not a passive acceptance of captivity. Results: the escapee may reach freedom and collect military information for U.S. intelligence; the enemy must augment guards and send out search parties; constant crises disrupt POW administration; the nuisance effect harms enemy morale; escapes improve our troops' morale, serve as propaganda, and add manpower.

**Organizing, techniques, devices, documentation (¶¶88–105).** Escape planning, the escape committee, methods, and tunneling — all in FM 21-77A, ¶¶5–11. Stealing tools from guards or working parties produces good tools but risks serious reprisals, and quantity and type are rarely adequate — so improvisation is extremely important; "few undertakings are beyond the capabilities of the average POW camp personnel." Escape aids and tools — FM 21-77A, ¶¶12–13.

**Cover at rollcall (¶¶106–107).** The best time to escape is at night just after the last rollcall — the next rollcall is usually not until morning, giving several hours before guards detect the break; covering up the absence for several days gains more time. Methods of covering up — FM 21-77A, ¶21.

## 9. Appendices — Training Program and Evasion Exercise

**Appendix I — References.** FM 21-76 *Survival*, FM 21-26 *Map Reading*, FM 21-75 *Combat Training of the Individual Soldier and Patrolling*, FM 31-21 *Guerilla Warfare and Special Warfare Operations*, FM 27-10 *The Law of Land Warfare*, DA Pam 30-101, DA Pam 20-151.

**Appendix II — Training program.** A guide for training individuals and units in evasion and escape as related to the Code of Conduct; the general objective is to increase the individual will to resist, thereby increasing unit fighting strength. Individual objectives: knowledge of democratic institutions and the fallacies of communism; loyalty to unit and fellow soldiers; unit, Army, and national history; moral fiber and religious motivation; sanitation, hygiene, and survival; the Geneva Conventions and the war-criminal concept; POW-camp organization and leadership; communist interrogation, indoctrination, and exploitation techniques and countering them.

**Appendix III — Evasion exercise.** A field exercise set near Eelbeck (Fort Benning, Georgia), adaptable to any locality, with an aggressor force (1 officer, 40 enlisted men), a critique as each group reaches the objective, plus an interrogation phase and medical aid.

## Output Contract

When answering an evasion or escape question, give the course of action first (defend, breakout, infiltrate, penetrate deeper, or combine), then the planning factors (isolation verified, communications, control, casualties), then the movement rules (night travel, groups of 2–4, longest route safest), then the cautions. For captivity questions, give the Code of Conduct position first (name, rank, service number, date of birth — and nothing more), then the resistance technique, then the warning about what the enemy does with anything more. State the historic status up front: 1958 doctrine; where current SERE doctrine (AFH 10-644) differs, follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter and paragraph; quote sparingly. Where the manual defers to the classified FM 21-77A supplement, say so — do not reconstruct the missing escape-committee, tunneling, forgery, or rollcall-cover methods and present them as FM 21-77.

## Operating Rules

1. **Surrender is not a choice while an alternative exists.** As long as a means to avoid capture exists, or someone capable of assuming leadership is present, evade; the evasion area is an extension of the battlefield.
2. **Short-range first.** The Army's program is based on short-range evasion and early escape in forward areas; long-range evasion and POW-camp escape are essential but secondary.
3. **Verify isolation before breaking up.** Confirm you are really cut off; acting on imagined isolation can lead to disaster.
4. **Small groups, night movement, longest route.** Groups of no more than four (2–4 for long range); travel at night, preferably after midnight; the longest route between objectives is the safest.
5. **Name, rank, service number, date of birth — and nothing more.** Silence is the most effective defense against interrogation and indoctrination; never believe the claim that friends have talked.
6. **The POW camp is a battlefield.** Senior takes command; keep faith with fellow prisoners; maintain discipline, hygiene, morale, and the will to escape.
7. **Historic doctrine, not current authority.** 1958 Cold War material: use `sere` (AFH 10-644) and `survival-evasion-recovery` (MCRP 3-02H) as the authority; FM 21-77 is the historical foundation for evasion movement, native contact, and POW-camp organization.
