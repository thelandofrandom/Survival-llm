# Short-Range Evasion — FM 21-77 (Ch. 3)

Distilled from FM 21-77, *Evasion and Escape*, U.S. Army, May 1958, Chapter 3 (¶¶12–23). Short-range evasion is the Army's primary emphasis: the program is built on evasion and early escape in forward areas, with long-range evasion and POW-camp escape essential but secondary.

## Short vs. Long Range (¶12)

No time or distance line separates short- from long-range evasion; the difference is conditions. Short-range evasion occurs in the forward combat zone within hours or perhaps days of isolation, while the soldier or small unit is still in good physical condition, has adequate food and clothing, and is generally oriented. The long-range evader, usually an escaped POW, operates over strange terrain with little or no food or equipment.

## The Isolation Hazard (¶¶13–15)

Studies of World War II and Korea showed the greatest POW losses occurred in isolated units: about three-fourths of the men captured in Korea reported their unit had been isolated by enemy action, and a comparable portion in World War II. Isolation is the danger, not lack of supplies — more than 7 in 10 captured units still had enough ammunition, and more than 9 in 10 reported food was not a factor. Capture often results from giving up before all alternatives are tried; repatriated POWs reported hours to days usually elapsed before capture, time enough to attempt another course. The lesson: isolation must be verified, not assumed, and the time before capture is time to act.

## Five Courses of Action (¶16)

(a) Defense of present position. (b) Breakout to areas under friendly control. (c) Evasion by infiltration. (d) Deeper penetration to conduct guerrilla operations. (e) Any combination of these.

## Defense of Present Position (¶17)

Sometimes there is no alternative — a battle position ordered held at all costs must be held until relief or the order is rescinded. Two examples from the manual:

- **Hürtgen Forest:** Company B, 109th Infantry, 28th Division — 75 men held an isolated position against superior German forces for 3 days until relieved.
- **Bastogne:** one rifle company holding a roadblock delayed two German divisions for almost two days, because the German commanders were confused by an American unit in an unexplained location and halted to study the situation.

## Breakout (¶18)

Fighting through to friendly territory. Success depends on leaders who act rapidly to find enemy weaknesses, know the terrain, and choose a simple, well-thought-out plan using a route and time calculated to catch the enemy off guard. Destroy ammunition and equipment that cannot be evacuated.

## Evasion by Infiltration (¶19)

For separated elements or small cut-off groups, often the best means home. Travel in groups of no more than four men. Plan carefully:

- **Present location** — know exactly where you are.
- **Direction and distance to friendly lines.**
- **Enemy disposition and movement** — observe from a concealed vantage point; forward displacement of artillery indicates the enemy is still moving forward.
- **Terrain and obstacles** — avoid ridgelines, bridges, roads, and towns; choose a route needing minimum exertion that avoids enemy strong points.
- **Weather and visibility** — darkness is the strongest ally; travel only at night, preferably after midnight; near the front, move just after sunset or just before sunrise, light enough to avoid minefields yet dark enough to prevent detection.
- **The evaders' capabilities** — physical condition, equipment, and training.

## Deeper Penetration — Guerrilla Operations (¶20)

Counterintuitive but sometimes the best way to escape: communist offensives stressed superiority of more than four to one and encirclement, so the bulk of the enemy force sweeps past the isolated unit, leaving its rear lightly manned by service troops. The isolated unit must know guerrilla operations, select an operating area offering concealment and living off the land, and exploit opportunities — destroying lines of communication, attacking headquarters, collecting information.

## Adapting the Plan; Unlimited Alternatives (¶¶21–22)

The situation changes, often rapidly; the ability to adopt a new alternative without delay is the key. During the Battle of the Bulge, December 1944, several hundred scattered men of an infantry division under a resourceful officer defended as a single force as long as possible, then split into small groups to infiltrate to Allied lines, and most succeeded. Any action is justified so long as it does not violate the Geneva Convention — for example, never misuse the Red Cross emblem to protect personnel or material not entitled to it.

## Factors Influencing the Course of Action (¶23)

Four questions decide the course:

1. **Are we really isolated?** Battlefield confusion, darkness, and poor information can create the illusion of isolation; verify by reconnaissance before breaking up the unit. Acting on imagined isolation can lead to disaster.
2. **Can we still communicate?** Can the unit still reach higher headquarters or adjacent units?
3. **Can the commander still control his unit?** Loss of control changes what courses are feasible.
4. **To what extent do casualties affect the course?** Never routinely abandon casualties — it is reprehensible and damaging to morale. Care for and evacuate them whenever possible. But recognize that under some conditions men who remain with the wounded may jeopardize the wounded, themselves, and the unit — in such a case leave supplies and, if possible, medical or other personnel with the wounded.
