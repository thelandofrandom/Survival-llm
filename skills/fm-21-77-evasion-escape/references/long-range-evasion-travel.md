# Long-Range Evasion and Travel — FM 21-77 (Ch. 4)

Distilled from FM 21-77, *Evasion and Escape*, U.S. Army, May 1958, Chapter 4 (¶¶24–46). Long-range evasion is evasion far behind enemy lines — usually by escaped POWs, in poor physical condition after camp, with little or no food or equipment, who must live off the land or, as a last resort, seek native assistance.

## The Evader's Problems (¶¶24–25)

The difference from short-range evasion lies in enemy deployment, security and police elements, travel restrictions, and civilian population. The manual points to FM 21-76 for living off the land, and requires background knowledge of the area: topography, language, population and customs, communications and transportation systems, internal affairs.

## The Will to Survive (¶¶26–28)

"The will to survive, coupled with your ability to withstand hardships and overcome obstacles, is the key to successful long-range evasion." It is not mysterious — it is maintained by sheer willpower. Many obstacles are mental rather than physical; wherever you are, other people have gone there intentionally and adjusted to the terrain, climate, and environment. Psychologists cited by the manual conclude: "Failure to survive, because of a loss of will to do so, may be considered either a setting in of apathy or the arousal of extreme panic."

**Sixteen factors that sustain the will (¶27a):** (1) Planning — survey the situation, formulate plans, make the best use of available resources. (2) Action — take the action necessary to survive and evade. (3) Effort — sheer effort and determination even under the most trying circumstances. (4) Training — survival and evasion training, experience, common sense, basic skills. (5) Control of panic. (6) Group integrity — group cooperation and solidarity. (7) Group leadership — strong, forceful leadership. (8) Stronger helps the weaker — encourage, calm fears, persuade to act. (9) Mutual support — the buddy system. (10) Patience — be willing to "sweat it out." (11) Endurance — endure pain and discomfort; keep in good physical condition. (12) Release of emotions — release pent-up emotions through an outburst or violent activity, then become calm and able to solve the problem. (13) Sense of responsibility — loyalty to country and military duty. (14) Religious faith — faith in God and prayer. (15) Family and home ties. (16) Hate and revenge.

**Thirteen factors that destroy the will (¶27b):** (1) Lack of survival and evasion training. (2) Failure to plan. (3) Panic — irrational, frantic, disorganized. (4) Inaction — doing nothing from fear, carelessness, or laziness. (5) Loneliness — helplessness and overwhelming fear. (6) Low self-esteem — lack of confidence, loss of self-respect, guilt. (7) Lack of teamwork — rivalry and dissension. (8) Prolonged exposure to heat, cold, thirst, hunger. (9) Insurmountable difficulty — weather, injury, sickness, lack of food. (10) Inability to endure — poor condition, unable to tolerate pain, thirst, discomfort, hunger. (11) Impatience — inability to "sweat it out." (12) Hysteria — "go berserk," a sharp breakdown, complete loss of control. (13) Lack of religious faith.

**Overcoming obstacles (¶28).** It is impossible to survive and evade over long periods in the open or to overcome obstacles unless you have psychologically prepared yourself for the worst. You are convinced that you can and will survive, whatever the odds, and return to friendly control. You have accepted the evasion area as an extension of the battlefield and will not give up the fight as long as you have the means to resist.

## Plan Evasion and Escape Together (¶29)

An escaped POW should have planned the early phases of long-range evasion concurrently with the escape, in the camp. Recapture of escapees is largely attributable to lack of information about enemy security measures immediately outside the camp. Establish a starting point before the escape and move rapidly to it on clearing the camp; select alternate starting points; if several prisoners escape, starting points serve as rallying points. Locate them far enough from the camp to avoid the security cordon set up after an escape alert. A well-organized escape committee can usually, by distracting guards, buy at least a few hours before the escape is detected.

## Courses of Action (¶¶30–34)

- **(a) Infiltration** — the most successful course for the individual or small group far behind enemy lines: complete the journey, possibly hundreds of miles over several months, without contacting natives, living off the land; travel in groups of 2–4, ranking officer or NCO in command.
- **(b) Deception** — disguise and cover stories; mandatory where contact with civilians is unavoidable (especially in Asia), and the most difficult tactic; more successful in Europe than in Asia given differences in facial features, language, and customs.
- **(c) Guerrilla-type operations** — not practical for individuals or small groups given the minor damage they can inflict, but not precluded (see ¶20).
- **(d) Combination** of the above.

## Planning, Information, Sabotage (¶¶35–37)

Planning must be as detailed as knowledge of the enemy situation permits, continuous and flexible (factors as in Ch. 3). Collecting enemy information: intelligence officers rate the evader a very reliable source, but information is a byproduct — never jeopardize personal safety or compromise cover to collect it. Observe and memorize enemy strength, equipment, location, disposition, organization, and movement (Order of Battle factors); **never record anything in writing** — such information found on your person is sufficient to convict you of espionage. No sabotage unless operating as part of a guerrilla group.

## Orientation and Cross-Country Travel (¶¶38–41)

Natural obstacles (weather, terrain) and human obstacles (travel restrictions, the whole enemy security apparatus for population control). Orient by sun and watch — midway between the hour hand and 1200 is south in the Northern Hemisphere — or by the Big Dipper's pointers, about five times the pointer spacing to the North Star, with Cassiopeia as a backup. Avoid major roads and populated areas of all types, even at the cost of extra time and distance: "As a general rule, the longest route between two evasion march objectives is the safest route." Use cover (ditches, hollows, embankments, folds in the ground) and concealment (trees, bushes, grass, log piles); move rapidly across exposed areas — "If the enemy can't see you, he can't capture you." Travel at night, preferably after midnight. Use the middle third of a ridgeline when crossing high ground. Unimproved roads in remote areas may be followed, or paralleled; avoid crossroads and junctions. Keep your identification tags; bury all traces of a campsite; practice supply economy; use firearms only in an emergency.

## Travel in Populated Areas; Area Hints (¶¶42–43)

Deception requires a yes to five questions: (1) Can I pass as a native by facial characteristics? (2) Is my language/dialect acceptable? (3) Are my credentials and clothing acceptable in this village or town? (4) Do I have sufficient money of the type used in this area? (5) Is my cover story plausible — will it stand up under local police examination, and under trained counterintelligence interrogation? If yes to all, deception offers the fastest evasion route.

**Area hints (§43) — historic 1958, not current area intelligence.** In Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, and Hungary, dissident peasants may help; Baltic nationalists and Ukrainian nationalists are potential contacts; in Crimea and the Caucasus little aid can be expected. In European Russia: board trains only at small stations; if unable to speak Russian, impersonate a shock victim or a deaf-mute; Russian funds help, bribery is widespread. In Manchuria: Muslims — identified by shaved-center mustaches, crescent shrines, and blue tassels — and Taoist temples give refuge; fishermen may help. Barter goods: clothing, sewing kits, watches, tobacco, pipes, razor blades, soap, mirrors — of native or neutral origin.

## Native Assistance — Last Resort (¶¶44–46)

Avoid people as long as possible; seek native assistance only when sickness, lack of food, or other cogent reasons make it impossible to proceed alone. **Who:** avoid the young (thoroughly indoctrinated) and the rich (usually government officials); safer contacts are farmers, shepherds, itinerant workers, isolated travelers; Christians in communist-dominated countries are valuable contacts, laymen and lower clergy less risky than higher clergy; native women are described as unreliable contacts — deal courteously, businesslike, and briefly, in favor of a male contact. **Where:** never in a group or in a village or town; approach a native alone, in an isolated area, around dusk, just after he rises, while working in his field, or on an isolated road. **Procedure:** be extremely cautious at first contact. If a native goes for help, move to a concealed observation point and watch.

## Escape Lines (¶46)

Escape lines ("rat lines") are organized networks that move evaders to safety. They work through cutouts, take weeks, and demand patience. Never discuss your unit or history, or the helpers' names and locations; never write helpers' names or addresses down — memorize them. A line can provide food and shelter, clothing and credentials, maps and security information, money and tickets, medical treatment, and a guide. Keep an emergency escape plan of your own at all times.
