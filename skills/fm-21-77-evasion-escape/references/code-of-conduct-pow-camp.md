# Code of Conduct, POW Camp, Interrogation, and Escape — FM 21-77 (Chs. 2, 5–10)

Distilled from FM 21-77, *Evasion and Escape*, U.S. Army, May 1958, Chapters 2 and 5–10 (¶¶5, 47–107). Historic 1958 Cold War material: the adversary is the communist bloc, and the interrogation and indoctrination chapters describe communist practice as understood from World War II and Korea.

## Legal Status under the Geneva Conventions (Ch. 2, ¶5)

Evaders' and escapees' rights are defined in the Hague Convention of 1907 and the Geneva Conventions of 1929, restated in the Revised Geneva Convention of 1949.

- **Article 17:** a POW is bound to give only surname, first name, rank, date of birth, and Army/regimental/personnel or serial number — and may not be mistreated or coerced for more.
- **Article 42:** "The use of weapons against prisoners of war, especially against those who are escaping or attempting to escape, shall constitute an extreme measure, which shall always be preceded by warnings appropriate to the circumstances."
- **Article 91:** an escape is deemed to have succeeded when the POW has (1) joined the Armed Forces of the Power on which he depends or those of an allied power; (2) left the territory under the control of the Detaining Power or an ally of that Power; or (3) joined a ship flying the flag of the Power on which he depends, or of an allied Power, in the territorial waters of the Detaining Power, the ship not being under the control of the last named power. POWs who have made good their escape in this sense and are recaptured are not liable to any punishment for their previous escape.
- **Article 92:** a POW who attempts to escape and is recaptured before having made good his escape in the sense of Article 91 is liable only to a disciplinary punishment, even if it is a repeated offense. A recaptured POW is handed over without delay to the competent military authority. Special surveillance after an unsuccessful escape must not affect his health, must be undergone in a POW camp, and must not entail the suppression of any safeguards granted by the Convention.
- **Article 93:** escape or attempt to escape, even if repeated, is not an aggravating circumstance if the POW is tried by judicial proceedings for an offense committed during his escape. Offenses committed with the sole intention of facilitating escape and not entailing violence against life or limb — offenses against public property, theft without intention of self-enrichment, drawing up or use of false papers, wearing civilian clothing — occasion disciplinary punishment only. POWs who aid or abet an escape are liable to disciplinary punishment only.
- **Article 94:** if an escaped POW is recaptured, the Power on which he depends is notified as defined in Article 122, provided notification of his escape has been made.

## The Code of Conduct (Ch. 2, §III)

Prescribed by Executive Order 10631, 17 August 1955. The governing idea: the POW camp is an extension of the battlefield, and the prisoner must continue fighting with any means available — resistance becomes passive or surreptitious. Capture does not relieve an officer or NCO of the responsibilities of rank: the senior must assume command of all American POWs; the junior must follow seniors' orders.

- **I — "I am an American, fighting in the forces which guard my country and our way of life. I am prepared to give my life in their defense."** A fighting man always; duty to oppose the enemy regardless of circumstances. "I am prepared to give my life" does not require needless sacrifice.
- **II — "I will never surrender of my own free will. If in command, I will never surrender my men while they still have the means to resist."** When isolated and no longer able to inflict casualties, the duty is to evade capture and rejoin friendly forces.
- **III — "If I am captured I will continue to resist by all means available. I will make every effort to escape and aid others to escape. I will accept neither parole nor special favors from the enemy."** Escape if able; assist others; never sign a parole agreement.
- **IV — "If I become a prisoner of war, I will keep faith with my fellow prisoners… If I am senior, I will take command."** Informing on fellow prisoners is forbidden. The senior line officer or NCO assumes command by rank without regard to Service; if that cannot be effected, an elected-representative or covert organization is formed. Hygiene, camp sanitation, and care of the sick and wounded are imperative.
- **V — "When questioned, should I become a prisoner of war, I am bound to give only name, rank, service number, and date of birth… I will make no oral or written statements disloyal to my country."** Confessions, questionnaires, personal-history statements, propaganda recordings and broadcasts, appeals, self-criticisms, and signatures to peace or surrender appeals are forbidden. Under Communist Bloc reservations to the Geneva Convention, a signed confession was likely to be used to convict the prisoner as a war criminal, removing POW status.
- **VI — "I will never forget that I am an American, fighting man, responsible for my actions, and dedicated to the principles which made my country free. I will trust in my God and in the United States of America."** The UCMJ continues to apply to POWs; conduct is examined after repatriation with regard for the conditions of captivity.

Training in support of the Code must instill the will to avoid capture, resist interrogation and indoctrination, and escape — and assure the soldier that he will not be forgotten, that every national means will be used to contact, support, and release him, and that U.S. law provides for his dependents.

## Contacting Friendly and Neutral Elements (Ch. 5, ¶¶47–55)

**The crucial phase.** Establishing contact with friendly lines or crossing to a neutral country is the most crucial point of the evasion — all the patience, planning, and hardship are wasted if caution is thrown away in sight of friendly forces. Personnel behind enemy lines have been killed by friendly outposts while attempting to pass through; evaders have been shot by friendly patrols because they did not identify themselves properly; great numbers of refugees have been shot by friendly forces. The evader is handicapped: regular patrols are given the challenge and password of the day and their entry time and place are known; the evader has none of that unless he rejoins within 24 hours of separation.

**Friendly patrols and outposts (¶¶49–51).** Watch from a concealed observation point with maximum coverage of the battle zone; memorize terrain features so you could infiltrate to friendly positions in darkness. The safest contact is with a reconnaissance patrol (combat patrols are strong, heavily armed, and engage the enemy; extended ground reconnaissance units may operate as far as 25 miles behind enemy lines) — or, if you elect not to make contact, observe its route and approach friendly lines at approximately the same location, avoiding minefields and booby traps. Once you spot a patrol, let it come to you if possible; when it is **25 to 50 yards** away, display a white cloth and call out in unmistakably American English, with dialect. Beyond 50 yards a reconnaissance patrol may avoid contact; closer than 25 yards a member may fire instantly. There must be enough light for the patrol to identify you as an American. Alternatives: swim down a stream flowing toward friendly lines, placing a log or flotation device in front of you and swimming 30 to 50 feet behind it so the log trips booby traps or mines; or float out to sea around the enemy's flank. Frontline troops, especially delaying-action forces and outposts forward of the forward edge of the battle area, more often than not shoot first and ask questions later — contact is a calculated risk, but the only alternative if no patrol can be found. Crawl through the enemy's forward position and no man's land during darkness, arriving near friendly positions shortly after dawn; call out with something typically American and produce a white cloth, or crawl in and whisper to a frontline sentry.

**Rescue by aircraft; neutral countries; debriefing (¶¶52–55).** Rotary-wing aircraft are best suited for rescue behind enemy lines — they fly at extremely low altitude, pass through valleys, and can rescue without landing. Positive identification is the critical part; enemy units have erected ground signals to attract rescue missions. Without signal panels, improvise the standard SOS distress signal from local materials; a civil aeronautics signal card recommends signals 10 feet high, but in hostile territory size must be modified — in Korea some evaders used small, distinctly colored scarves or panels, or rocks. Arrange signals so they can be removed at a moment's notice, since enemy planes may also fly over. It may be advantageous to travel to a neutral country rather than friendly territory — especially if camps are hundreds or thousands of miles from friendly areas, or travel restrictions are lighter on the neutral route. On reaching a neutral country, represent yourself as an escaped prisoner of war even if technically you only evaded capture: the right under international law to repatriation to the United States rests on being an escaped POW. Once back in friendly hands, remain silent about your exploits — talking may endanger those who helped you and compromise methods another evader might use. Give only information of immediate tactical importance to frontline units; tell the first officer or NCO you are returning from "missing in action," "prisoner of war," or "internment" status and ask to be taken to someone authorized to receive evasion and escape information. Secrecy continues after debriefing: guard against disclosure to relatives, friends, press, radio, or television, and never submit information gained behind enemy lines without specific written authority from the appropriate U.S. government agency.

## Capture and Early Escape (Ch. 6, ¶¶56–60)

Capture is neither dishonorable nor heroic; at times it is unavoidable. But experience shows a majority of those taken into custody could have avoided capture by applying basic evasion tactics — capture is "something like a disease that is 90 percent preventable." If captured despite all efforts, every waking moment must be spent developing and executing a plan of escape — for yourself and for other prisoners. Escape is a duty (Code of Conduct, Article III). It is each POW's responsibility to assist others in escaping, even at the cost of punishment to himself. The earlier the escape, the simpler it can be. (Specific early-escape opportunities are in FM 21-77A, ¶2.)

**Soviet POW handling (World War II).** Under Soviet regulations, movement to the rear used covered terrain where possible; halts in villages were avoided; collection points were not placed in dense woods or underbrush; POWs were not permitted to delay the march or talk to guards, civilians, or other POWs — the manual treats these procedures as typical and likely to be followed by any future enemy. Chances of successful escape are immeasurably greater shortly after capture: you are closer to friendly lines than you will ever be again in custody, oriented as to direction, and in better physical condition; forward-area guards are not highly trained as guards, so opportunities exist — but the danger of being killed by a guard is also greater in the forward combat zone, since combat troops are keyed to battle pitch. Remain alert to all escape opportunities — each may be the last.

## POW Camp Organization and Typical Camps (Ch. 7, ¶¶61–68)

A well-organized camp where all POWs work for the common good can spell the difference between hope and despair. Under the Geneva Convention of 1949: where there are no officers, the prisoners' representative is elected by secret ballot every 6 months; in officers' or mixed camps (excluding labor camps), the senior officer is the representative; in labor camps with officers for administrative duties, the representative is elected. The representative sets up an organization ensuring the best possible living conditions — every POW responsible to someone or for something, committees for administration, welfare, education, sports, and recreation; food shared carefully, Red Cross packages distributed fairly, "share and share alike" strictly enforced. Protecting powers (neutral nations) examine application of the Convention's provisions; the International Committee of the Red Cross may assume those humanitarian functions if protecting powers cannot.

**Typical communist camps:** remoteness, ill treatment, hard work, insufficient food, inadequate clothing and housing; long, low, one-story wooden shacks in rows, tiered bunks, substandard heating and cooking, inadequate water and toilets. Security: armed guards, trained dogs, searchlights, guard towers — controlling activity more than preventing escape; informers used to gain intelligence and penetrate clandestine organizations. Camps are divided into compounds, organized by barracks leaders in a squad/platoon/company-like structure, sometimes with a communist leader for each echelon and political instructors wielding influence over all phases; prisoners may be segregated by nationality, rank, race, and susceptibility to indoctrination. Overt organizations (singing groups, sports committees, theatrical and musical groups) are tolerated as harmless and useful for propaganda. Covert organizations are covered in FM 21-77A, ¶3.

## Survival in the Camp (¶¶69–74)

The will to survive must be kept alive through self-discipline and mutual aid; hardships and pleasures shared; the burden of the weak, wounded, or demoralized carried in common. Fear is normal; pain, cold, thirst, hunger, fatigue, boredom, and loneliness can accumulate until panic — boredom and loneliness are particularly likely to bring mental imbalance unless met with strength of character or spiritual faith. Groups behave better than individuals under stress when organized, with good morale and a time perspective — prisoners become depressed by the seeming endlessness of time and must establish definite goals to look forward to. Humor is important: "you must laugh to sustain your will to survive" — case histories show POWs who died with nothing organically wrong, having simply lost the will to survive. Morale: the worst enemy is yourself — keep neat and clean, mentally and physically busy; good morale produces courage, optimism, determination, loyalty, and consideration for others, and fosters the will to escape.

**Medical — historic field-expedient practice, not current doctrine.** The most serious threats are exposure, malnutrition, unsanitary conditions, and lack of medical care; attend the weak and ailing, encourage them to eat and exercise. Dysentery: 8–10 stools per day was normal in Korean camps, 15 simple diarrhea, 25+ dysentery; remedies included charcoal, burned bone powder, powdered chalk, oak-bark tannic-acid brew (12 hours to 3 days), and tea. Wounds: maggots eat only dead tissue. Lice: inspect body and every garment seam daily. Worms: kerosene, two tablespoons daily. Pneumonia: remain standing for sufficient periods — "many POW's with pneumonia simply covered their heads, rolled over into a corner, and died." Malnutrition: eat everything edible — dogs, cats, rats, weeds, maggots; rats thoroughly cooked, snakes with head and skin removed, cooked until well done.

**Survival checklist:** exercise leadership; maintain military and self-discipline; keep up morale, your own and others'; recognize and control fear; keep on your feet and keep going; eat everything edible; nourish your sense of humor; practice survival, self-aid, and preventive medicine; use common sense; keep up the will to survive. **Supply economy:** conserve everything — you enter confinement with a minimum, replacement may be impossible; keep a stock of clothing, food, and utensils against unpleasant eventualities, especially shoes, trousers, jackets, and food. POW handicrafts are in FM 21-77A, ¶4.

## Tactical Interrogation (¶¶75–76)

Newly captured prisoners face early interrogation in the combat zone. In World War II, German prisoners taken by the Russians were interrogated ~30 minutes at battalion on immediate tactical matters, in more detail at regiment on Order of Battle, further at division (officers and NCOs), and at army level received a prepared questionnaire of ~140 questions on strategic and personal subjects. In Korea, tactical interrogation covered unit identifications, strength, reserves, defenses, and minefields, before evacuation to the rear. The prisoner's authorized response: **name, rank, service number, date of birth, and nothing more** — silence is the most effective defense.

## Strategic Interrogation and Resistance (Ch. 8, ¶¶77–82)

**Strategic interrogation (¶¶77–78).** Beyond tactical matters, the enemy seeks strategic information on conditions in the United States and the U.S. Army, and the prisoner's attitudes and susceptibility to communist influence. A prepared questionnaire at army level or en route to a permanent camp covers: the POW's whole past life up to military service; the condition of the U.S. Armed Forces, with an elaborate quiz on his career; conditions in the zone of interior; political attitudes; and attitude toward communist propaganda. In a permanent camp a second questionnaire is required; the two are checked against each other for discrepancies, and all interrogations are based on them, with emphasis on discrepancies. Giving information does not improve comfort: "No matter how friendly the interrogators appear before you talk, they invariably despise you afterwards."

**Resisting interrogation (¶79).** The first line of defense is military bearing, silence, and trust in yourself, the Army, your country, and your God. The manual's points: give only name, rank, service number, and date of birth; be respectful but not obsequiously polite (apparent willingness to cooperate prolongs the inquisition); acting stupid may get you dismissed as a waste of time — appearing educated or knowledgeable gets you passed to higher levels; put interrogators off by saying you do not know, or by refusing to talk; beware of "stool pigeons" and POW camp medical personnel used to collect information; if you can speak the enemy's language, **do not disclose it** — using an interpreter gains time to formulate answers and lets you overhear enemy soldiers speaking freely, and language knowledge increases escape chances; never believe statements that your friends have talked — that is orthodox procedure to catch you off guard; avoid looking the interrogator directly in the eye (stare at a spot between the eyes or on the forehead); be courteous but firm, salute all interrogation officers; never give information about other POWs, and warn a fellow POW if the enemy shows interest in him; never boast of exploits, true or fabricated — one escapee's analysis of Russian methods concluded that many men "paid a high price for an overpowering ambition to appear important" and that "a prisoner in Russia has only one effective means of defense and that is absolute silence toward everyone."

**Political nature; methods of persuasion (¶¶80–82).** Communist interrogation is preoccupied with politics throughout: rooting out bitter enemies and discovering possible friends; indoctrination begins as early as possible; at division level, special interrogators conduct exhaustive interrogations, sometimes a panel questioning simultaneously on unrelated matters to confuse the POW. Russians show wide mood swings from exuberant cordiality to gloomy depression and cruelty, a superior attitude compensating for a "vast national inferiority complex," and an atmosphere of distrust in which failure is treated as a crime tantamount to treason — interrogators will go to any lengths to get information or a signed confession, even about things the POW could not possibly know. Strategic interrogations used physical and psychological coercion: double-timing in a heated room wearing a fur coat; confinement in a dungeon in underwear on sharp-edged stones for days; sitting on a backless, armless stool, hands at sides, feet stretched out, for up to 180 hours; hands tied and raised behind the back, forced to stand on tiptoes; lashings with whips of small steel rods; standing for 5 days until legs swelled enormously; sitting at attention for days without sleep; electrical contacts on throat and stomach, current for 3 minutes at a time, a doctor in attendance; starvation, beatings with a blackjack, day-and-night interrogation, confinement in a small cupboard; torture and solitary confinement as routine, sometimes in cells so small the prisoner had to stand; water poured over a towel draped on a bent-back head until suffocation or drowning or revival; kneeling with steel rods behind the knees for up to 2 days; fingernails ripped off; standing on tiptoes with hands tied behind the back and a noose around the neck tied to the secured hands, so the prisoner eventually tired, fell, and hanged himself; and methods adjusted to the person — harshness for the timid, bluff for the stupid, reminders of family for the sentimental, promises of early release and better food for the unstable. MVD (secret police) interrogations sought mainly to convict POWs of criminal acts to prevent repatriation. Chinese and North Korean interrogations in Korea were harsh and brutal, but in no instance was an American POW reported killed for failure to give more than name, rank, service number, and date of birth — the Chinese were more interested in converting the POW to communist ideology.

## Indoctrination (Ch. 9, ¶¶83–86)

The communist indoctrination program aimed to develop propaganda, gain control over prisoners during internment, and convert the POW to communist ideology. The communists did not try to convert every prisoner — they selected a few they could trust to become revolutionists, and otherwise sought to destroy or reduce hostility and plant seeds of doubt. Defense: the same as against interrogation — if the interrogator cannot induce you to give more than name, rank, service number, and date of birth, indoctrination is impossible; confidence in yourself, trust in your unit, your country, and your God are the bulwark. Three basic techniques:

- **Repetition** — prisoners required to memorize material and answer the same questions for days, weeks, months, reading the same propaganda over and over; some with no formal education beyond sixth grade could recite long essays on communism yet not a single line of the Bill of Rights or the Constitution; the theory is the "big lie" told often enough.
- **Harassment** — a precise, unvarying schedule designed to create anxiety and constant uncertainty, depriving prisoners of "free time" by summoning them to headquarters at odd hours (about to enter the latrine, preparing to eat, after going to sleep, 3 o'clock in the morning) on trumped-up charges, each time with a lecture and a few questions, each time the prisoner believing it would be the last and giving a little more — and extracting "confessions" of self-criticism. The only effective defense is don't talk.
- **Humiliation** — breaking personal pride before other prisoners, e.g., a prisoner who picked up a cucumber in a farmer's garden was made to write and then publicly read a "confession" that he stole because stealing was part of the American way of life, then told the communists were his only friends; humiliated prisoners were then recruited as informants, threatened with their signed confessions and secret recordings being sent to the United States.

**Selection.** A captured Chinese document described selecting officers with the longest service ("more experienced and clear minded"), segregating prisoners by manners and political attitudes, learning their backgrounds, explaining policies to remove fear, conducting special interrogations for the stubborn, and dividing prisoners into the "progressive type" (youthful, sympathetic, willing to talk — treat well, encourage, get them to write statements) and the "stubborn, reactive, afraid-of-death, homesick type" (no firm convictions — find their weakness and instill fear).

## Escape from a POW Camp (Ch. 10, ¶¶87–107)

**The value of escape (¶87).** "The best defense is a good offense" — that aggressive fighting spirit must be carried into the POW camp; escape is a duty, not a passive acceptance of captivity. Results: the escapee may reach freedom; during escape travel he may collect military information of interest to U.S. intelligence; the enemy must augment guards and send out search parties; constant crises disrupt enemy POW administration; the nuisance effect harms enemy military and civilian morale; escapes improve the morale of our troops and are good propaganda; and the returnee adds to the country's manpower. Welfare efforts such as theatrical or musical groups are desirable for morale but must not detract from escape activity — if possible they should augment it by providing cover or aid.

**Organizing, techniques, devices, documentation (¶¶88–105).** Planning an escape, the escape committee (purpose, composition, security), methods of escape, and tunneling — all in FM 21-77A, ¶¶5–11. This volume states only the principle: concentrate energy on the preparation and execution of escapes or allied activity. Escape devices: stealing tools from guards or working parties produces good tools but may lead to serious reprisals, and quantity and type are rarely adequate — so improvisation of tools is extremely important; "few undertakings are beyond the capabilities of the average POW camp personnel." Materials useful as escape aids and tools — FM 21-77A, ¶¶12–13. Escape documentation: escape from camp is only the first phase; after escape you must move through hostile territory to safety, possibly many miles distant, either cross-country in darkness or in disguise — and disguise requires documents. Identity documents, methods of production, maps, photographs, official stamps, casting, clothing — FM 21-77A, ¶¶14–20.

**Cover at rollcall (¶¶106–107).** The best time to escape is at night just after the last rollcall — the next rollcall is usually not until morning, giving several hours before guards detect the break; covering up the absence at rollcall for several days gains even more time. In World War II most failed escapes were recaptured not far from camp shortly after escaping, because security forces had been alerted; evaders traveling before the alert, or where the enemy had not been alerted, enjoyed "astounding freedom of movement on occasion, even in daylight and in direct contact with enemy elements." The rollcall cover-up may be part of the evasion plan — if it fails, the evasion collapses. Methods of covering up — FM 21-77A, ¶21.
