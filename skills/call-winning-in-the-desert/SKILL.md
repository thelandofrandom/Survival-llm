# CALL: Winning in the Desert

## Purpose

CALL: *Winning in the Desert* (Center for Army Lessons Learned newsletter, DTIC ADA446544, August 1990, 32 pages) — a lessons-learned compilation drawn from the National Training Center, Exercise BRIGHT STAR, *Saudi Arabia Lessons Learned* (Army Materiel Command, 1983), and the CALL database. It is a report of findings, not a manual: desert environment and geography, effects on personnel and equipment, Saudi Arabia lessons learned, tips on fighting in the desert, and knowledge of Arab customs. Its governing premise: "principles and fundamentals of combat do not change in the desert" — read FM 90-3 for desert doctrine; this newsletter adds the practical lessons. Written just before Desert Storm, it reads as a pre-deployment primer for the theater the U.S. Army was about to fight in.

**Source quality note.** The cover page OCR is garbled — an extraction artifact, not content. The body text is readable throughout.

## 1. The Desert Environment

**Saudi Arabia.** About 2,217,949 sq km, with three great deserts — the Nafud, Ad Dahna, and the Rub Al Khali, the last over 500,000 sq km. The Hijaz mountains run 2,000–6,000 ft; the Asir range reaches 9,000 ft. Temperatures reach 130°F inland, with day-night swings up to 70°F; the highest recorded desert temperature is 136°F, and tank interiors reached ~160°F in the Sahara in World War II. **Iraq** is about 435,000 sq km in four regions, with ~90% of its rain falling November–April.

**Desert types and driving.** Three types: mountain, rocky plateau, and sandy dune — dunes over 1,000 ft high and 10–15 miles long. Driving: night and early morning are best; sand build-up throws tracks; recon a dune crossing on foot first; watch for crust, thorn punctures, and rock wear. **Lost or stranded:** use the buddy system — never fewer than two vehicles; stay with the vehicle; hood or trunk up means help needed; white cloth on the antenna; signal with a mirror or oil smoke; make a shadow-stick compass (15 minutes).

## 2. Personnel — Water, Heat, Acclimation

**Water is the first priority.** Water is ~75% of body fluid. A 2-quart loss (2.5% of body weight) costs ~25% of efficiency; a 15% loss is usually fatal. Leaders must force drinking — **2 quarts per hour** when active, small sips often. "YOU CAN'T DRINK TOO MUCH WATER!" Best water temperature is 50–60°F. Plastic cans keep water good 72 hours vs. 24 hours for metal; change canteen water at least every 24 hours; trailer water lasts 5 days in shade — change it if it exceeds 92°F (bacteria and diarrhea). **Salt:** MRE salt is enough up to 4 quarts per day; salt tablets (2 per 1-quart canteen, dissolved) only under medical direction.

**Heat and sun.** Acclimation takes 2 weeks of progressive exposure. Sunburn: 5 minutes the first day, plus 5 minutes a day; the sun is as dangerous on a cloudy day. Heat illness runs cramps, exhaustion, stroke — and a heat-stroke victim creeps away to hide, so watch for missing men. **Clothing:** lightweight camouflage, not fatigue green; long sleeves and trousers tucked into boots; no jungle boots (sand); sweater and field jacket for cold nights; wool and cotton scarves; sleeping bag; chapstick, sunburn ointment, salt tablets, foot powder, insect repellent, sunglasses, 2-quart canteens, goggles.

## 3. Sanitation and Disease

**Field sanitation.** Garbage at least 100 ft from water; burial at least 30 yards from the kitchen; garbage pits 4×4×4 ft for 100 men for 1 day, filled to no more than 1 ft from the top. Latrines at least 100 yards from mess and water; close when waste is within 1 ft of the surface, mound 1 ft high and spray 2 ft on each side, mark "Closed Latrine" with the date. **Diseases to expect:** plague, typhus, malaria, dengue, dysentery, cholera, typhoid. → `references/desert-environment-personnel.md`

## 4. Equipment and Vehicles

**Sand and dust are the greatest danger to equipment.** Use minimum lubrication — weapons may be better dry; use muzzle covers; protect optics from pitting; clean air filters daily (a filter is useless in 3 days); change oil about twice as often, 20W-50. Batteries: specific gravity 1.200–1.225, electrolyte 1.2085–1.2185 (TM 9-6140-100-12). Cooling: a gauge reading 10–20° hotter than normal is expected; 180° is average, 200° means monitor; if overheating, shift to neutral and run at ~1,200 RPM; a drip per second is 7 gallons in 24 hours; don't remove hood side panels while running. **Static electricity:** ground tankers and vehicles; a helicopter hook must touch ground first. **Expansion:** tires inflated at night may burst by day; don't overfill fuel tanks. → `references/equipment-vehicles.md`

## 5. Saudi Arabia Lessons Learned (SANG, 1982)

From the Saudi Arabian National Guard program: tire flats ran 40% per 3-day exercise; recover a stuck vehicle by letting out about a quarter of the air, sand ladders, the rocking method — a winch is best. Driver skill matters more than vehicle type. Every vehicle carries a survival kit (13 items, including 5 gallons of water per occupant). Observation from a 300-meter hill sees 20–30 km but misses intermediate ridges — recon routes; few hard roads exist. The TOW dust signature is not serious. Repair parts: stock +50%. Batteries go dead after 5–10 days idle. Cooling systems calcify; clean filters daily. The weapons-lubrication debate: U.S. standard in storage, light in use. WD-1 wire dry-rots — use shielded cable. Wear eye protection. Force liquids — Arabs drink sweet hot tea. Bad water causes gallstones and urinary disorders. Fresh fruit and ice cream are morale items. Jet lag takes 2–3 days — ideally 1 day per time zone. Respiratory disorders are common. Nonpotable saline water is a theater problem: a unit needs **at least 7 gallons of water per man per 24 hours**. Fuel contamination: Petromin is the only flushing facility; POL storage has no sampling, gauging, or strapping. Ammo consumption runs up and ballistics are erratic in heat — store ammo double-shaded and about 1 meter below the floor to keep it under 100°F. Desert fog brings zero visibility; optics condense near the Gulf and Red Sea. Dispersion matters: bunching is visible 10 km from the air.

## 6. Tips on Fighting in the Desert

**Intelligence:** allow recon lead times; confirm ~80% of enemy anti-tank systems to 6-digit accuracy; scouts are reconnaissance, not combat; visibility is limited. **Maneuver:** terrain masking is the only cover; shadows betray positions from the air; dig in; move as the sun moves; shade optics; stake the trigger point; range estimation is distorted; inspect your position from the enemy's point of view; light and noise carry for miles; move at night; sand reveals movement. **Fire support:** hot ammunition affects ballistics; dig in ammo storage; use illumination and smoke to reorient; the FSC plans all fires. **Mobility, countermobility, survivability:** linear obstacles; minefields can be laid rapidly over large areas; the enemy attacks with the sun low behind him; two operators per piece of engineer equipment; a tank platoon dug in two-step positions can destroy a battalion; basic loads of Class IV with all vehicles; reverse slopes; dummy positions; MOPP; chemical agents — heat increases liquid-agent incapacitation, wind evaporates and disperses agents. **Air defense:** multinational airspace SOPs; host-nation liaisons to the BCE; sunlight affects Stinger. **CSS:** medical distances are long; the battlefield is nonlinear and intermingled; at least one combat lifesaver per squad; medics must recertify; weapons cleaned constantly; PMCS is essential; food service needs fresh fruit, vegetables, and breads. **C2:** operate where contact is maintained with forward units and the TOC; EENT/BMNT and illumination matter; FM dead spots come from minerals; know the commander's intent and rehearse the battle drill. → `references/fighting-tips-customs.md`

## 7. Knowledge of Arab Customs

Alcohol and pornography are prohibited in theater. Shake hands on meeting and leaving. Never expose the sole of your shoe. Don't ask about women. Friendship is measured by duration and intensity. Never give a flat "no." Same-sex touching and holding hands are acceptable; opposite-sex touching and kissing in public are obscene. Stand close. Don't be impatient — a late arrival is an insult. No direct public criticism: take a man aside privately. Don't patronize. No photos of installations, police, airports, or seaports, or close-range photos of people — especially women — without permission. Don't stare at or strike up conversation with Arab women in public.

## Operating Rules

1. **Water first.** Force 2 quarts per hour when active; you can't drink too much water.
2. **Sand kills equipment.** Clean filters daily, lubricate minimally, protect optics.
3. **Move at night, dig in by day.** Terrain masking is the only cover; sand reveals movement.
4. **Know the commander's intent and rehearse.** FM dead spots, long medical distances, and nonlinear battlefields punish units that don't.
5. **Respect the customs.** No alcohol or pornography, no photos of installations or women without permission, no public criticism, no flat "no."
6. **Lessons, not doctrine.** This is a 1990 CALL newsletter — use FM 90-3 and current doctrine as the authority.
