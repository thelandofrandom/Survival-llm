# Combatives

## Purpose

TC 3-25.150, Combatives (manual text: TC 3-22.150), U.S. Army, March 2017, 408 pages — the Army's hand-to-hand fighting system, distilled into field-usable doctrine. It is not a martial-arts catalog. It is a system built on one strategic idea: a Soldier's fight is a fight over weapons, fought at four ranges, and won by controlling range, angle, and level to gain a dominant body position and finish the fight. The manual's own warning frames everything that follows: there is no shortcut to developing fighting skill; it can only be developed by a systematic approach to training, or in any realistic training against a fully resistant partner.

Note the publication quirk: the cover and headers say TC 3-25.150, but the running text is TC 3-22.150 — the manual supersedes TC 3-25.150 of 1 April 2009, and the text block carries the newer number. Cite it as TC 3-25.150 (2017).

## 1. The system — why it works the way it does

**The universal fight plan, and why it fails.** When two untrained fighters meet, they instinctively pummel each other with fists until one takes enough damage to stop fighting. Most martial-arts training makes fighters better at that plan. It has three drawbacks: one or both fighters are unarmed; progress depends solely on developing skill; and bigger, stronger, faster fighters have a natural advantage — developing enough skill to overcome those advantages takes more time than institutional training can give.

**The Army's answer: fight by strategy, not by attrition.** Fighting is taught in the context of strategy. The basic techniques are an educating metaphor for the basic fight strategy: defeat the opponent by controlling the elements of the fight — **range, angle, and level**. Range is the number-one element. The fight has four phases, in order of distance: **projectile, striking, clinching, grappling**. The Soldier's goal at each phase is to move to a better one — and ultimately to a dominant position from which the fight can be finished.

**The three combat-feedback lessons** (para 1-12), learned from Soldiers actually engaged in hand-to-hand combat:
- **Every fight is a grappling fight.** That does not mean there is no striking — every fight also involves striking, but always as an integral part of grappling.
- **Every fight is over weapons.** Control of the weapon — yours or his — will most likely determine the outcome.
- **There is no shortcut.** Fighting skill comes only from a systematic approach to training and live training against resistance.

**The system principles (Chapter 1):** foundation (institutional training builds the base, from individual entry training through NCO and officer education); continuous training (most development happens in the unit, not in a course — units must run their own programs); competition (the principal motivational tool — competitions drive excellence, and referees enforce the standard); drills (techniques are drilled to automaticity before live training); live training (against a fully resistant partner, approaching the reality of combat, using several methods combined); integrated training (combatives happen as part of a mission, not in a vacuum); combat feedback (lessons from real fights are captured and the program evolves). Safety and risk assessment run through all of it — the injury data in Tables 1-1 and 1-2 (Armed Forces Health Surveillance Center study) shows where injuries actually happen, and the program is designed around preventing them. → `references/system-and-training.md`

## 2. The basic fight strategy — four decisions

The basic strategy is a sequence of decisions, and every technique in the manual serves one of them:

**1. Create space.** The Soldier's primary goal in combat is to establish space between himself and his attacker — enough space to transition to his primary weapon. If you can create space and get to your rifle, the hand-to-hand fight is over.

**2. Maintain space.** If the Soldier cannot create enough space for the primary weapon, he maintains space to transition to his secondary weapon — or he closes the distance instead.

**3. Close the distance.** If space cannot be maintained, close it. The primary goal in training is instilling the courage to close the distance: the willingness to close with the enemy is a defining characteristic of a Warrior, and the ability to do it against an aggressive opponent is the first step in using range to control a fight.

**4. Gain dominant position.** An appreciation for dominant position is fundamental. It ties together what would otherwise be a list of unrelated techniques. From a dominant position, finish the fight.

**The dominant positions, in order of control:** rear mount (behind the opponent, both feet hooked around his thighs — the most dominant position in the system), mount (astride the face-up opponent's chest or abdomen), side control (perpendicular across the opponent's torso), knee mount (one knee in the abdomen, the other off the ground), and the guard (legs locked around the opponent, controlling him from the bottom — a defensive position that is also a platform for sweeps and submissions). The hierarchy is the whole game: every positioning move exists to climb it, every escape exists to descend it. → `references/ground-fighting.md`

## 3. Basic ground fighting (Chapter 3)

**Ground grappling — the fundamentals.** Stand in base (prone and supine, with and without the primary weapon) is the position from which everything starts — a stable base that cannot be easily moved, with the weapon protected. Fighting stance, the opposing-thumb grip, the wrestler's grip, and the thumb/thumbless grip are the hand tools that make the positions work.

**Basic body positioning moves:** arm trap and roll; passing the guard; achieve the mount from side control; arm push and roll to the rear mount; escape the rear mount; escape the mount (shrimp to the guard). Each is a bridge between two positions on the hierarchy — the manual teaches them as movements, not as static holds, because a fight is won in the transitions.

**Basic finishing moves:** rear naked choke; cross-collar choke from the mount and guard; bent arm bar from the mount and side control; straight arm bar from the mount; straight arm bar from the guard; guillotine choke. A finish is a technique that ends the fight — a choke that cuts blood or air, or a joint lock that breaks the arm. The manual's order matters: position first, then finish. A choke attempted from a bad position is a gift to the opponent.

**Basic sweeps:** scissors sweep; sweep from the attempted straight arm bar. A sweep reverses the position — the bottom fighter puts the top fighter on his back and takes the top.

**Basic range-controlling techniques:** the frame (arms and legs creating a barrier that keeps the opponent at a distance); hook and head control. Then the three options from the bottom: **Option 1** — create space, engage with the primary weapon; **Option 2** — maintain space, employ the secondary weapon; **Option 3** — achieve the clinch. These three options are the basic fight strategy applied from the ground.

**Clinch fighting — achieve the clinch:** close the distance; modified seatbelt clinch; double underhooks; rear clinch. **Basic takedowns:** front takedown; rear takedown. The clinch is the bridge between striking range and grappling range — and the place where the fight is most often decided, because the opponent who controls the clinch controls the takedown, and the takedown decides who is on top. → `references/ground-fighting.md`

## 4. Intermediate ground fighting (Chapter 4)

Intermediate work is about what happens when the basic plan fails — the opponent defends, escapes, or reverses you.

**Regaining the initiative — defense:** defend against chokes; defend against the standing headlock; escape the headlock from the bottom (form the frame; follow the leg; lift and turn; block and lift; arch over); defend against attempts to mount (arch, backdoor); escape the half-guard; reverse bent arm bar from side control. The principle: a defense that only stops the attack is half a defense — the good ones put you in a better position than you started in.

**Intermediate body positioning — pass the guard:** with the knee in the tailbone; under one leg; under both legs; near-side leg through; far-side leg through. Passing the guard is the gateway skill of the intermediate level — until you can pass, you cannot reach the dominant positions against a resisting opponent.

**Attacks from the mount:** papercutter choke; nutcracker choke; leaning choke; sleeve choke; collar choke; single wing choke; straight arm bar from the rear mount. **Attacks from the guard:** triangle choke; elevator sweeps (options one and two); elevator sweep escape from the mount (shrimp to the guard); reverse bent arm bar from the guard; hip heist sweep; guillotine choke from the guard. **Knee mount:** knee mount from side control; near-arm side bar; reverse bent arm bar from the knee mount. **Leg attacks:** straight ankle lock; straight knee bar.

**Intermediate takedowns:** drop to single leg; double leg; tree top; block the far knee; hook the foot; hip throw. **Grappling with strikes:** pass the guard with strikes; strikes from side control; defending against strikes in the guard at close, middle, and far range. **Regaining the initiative against chokes:** against the standing guillotine; against the standing headlock (knee strikes; step in front; step behind).

**Intermediate detainee procedures** — the part of the manual most relevant outside a fight: single-man cuffing techniques (including the wall variant); two-man cuffing (front takedown, rear takedown, wheelbarrow); detainee escort; vehicle extraction (door and window). These are the techniques for taking a person into custody and moving him without killing him — and the manual treats them with the same step-by-step precision as the chokes. → `references/ground-fighting.md`

## 5. Advanced ground fighting and striking (Chapter 5)

**Advanced ground fighting:** advanced finishing moves; advanced clinch positions (inside control; neck and biceps; double underhooks; front headlock; counter to inside control); pummeling (50/50 pummeling; modified seatbelt counter; overhook counter; outside-to-inside wedge; over-to-under lever; under-to-over lever; push the elbow inward; double elbow lift); knee strikes (long knee, up knee, round knee) and their defenses (hip check; pull toward/away from the knee; hand check; knee lift and grab; tilt the head); side control escapes (opponent in standard side control; near-side hip control; head and far arm underhooked; both hands in near-side control); north/south escapes; far-side arm bar; paper cutter from modified knee mount to belly scarf hold; reverse arm bar with leg from the guard; Achilles lock defense.

**Striking skills — movement and footwork:** basic footwork; weight shift drill. **Arm strikes, attack:** jab; cross; hook; uppercut. **Arm strikes, defense:** cross (front cover); hook (rear cover). **Kicks, attack:** round kick; switch kick; push kick (teep). **Kicks, defense:** evading a kick; beating a kick; stopping a kick; catching a kick; checking a kick. **Break fall:** forward rolling break fall. **Throw takedowns:** arch and turn. **Double-leg attacks:** double leg; snatch. **Defense:** sprawl; cross-face; quarter nelson; shoulder pressure roll to rear mount.

The striking section is deliberately short relative to the grappling sections — that is the system talking. Striking exists to set up the clinch and the takedown, and to punish the opponent who tries to strike you from inside your guard. A Soldier who boxes in a fight has chosen the wrong range. → `references/striking-and-weapons.md`

## 6. Contact weapons (Chapter 6)

**Thrusting weapons — the rifle and bayonet.** Grips; primary weapons (the rifle as a contact weapon); standing grappling with the rifle; body positioning; attack with force: **thrust, butt stroke to the head, butt stroke to the groin, slash, smash**; modified movements (modified thrust, modified slash); defense: **parry right, parry left, high block, low block, side block**. A sample sequence from the manual: parry right, thrust, butt stroke to the groin, slash, attack position. The rifle is a weapon at every range — the bayonet is the point, the butt is the hammer, and the manual teaches both as one system.

**Short-range contact weapons — knives.** Grips: straight grip, reverse grip. Defending against a knife: disarms by **strikes, leverage, and extension**. The manual is honest about knives: the defense is not to win a knife fight, it is to survive one long enough to get to a weapon or get away.

**Grappling with weapons:** muzzle strike; tug of war (both fighters gripping the same weapon); with the rifle — palm strike, clear primary weapon elbow strike, clear primary weapon front kick, clear primary weapon head butt; opponent reaches for your weapon with one hand. The through-line of the whole chapter: **every fight is over weapons** — the fight is won by the fighter who controls the weapon, and the techniques exist to take, keep, or clear one. → `references/striking-and-weapons.md`

## 7. Training, safety, and competition (Chapters 2, Appendices A–C)

**The three courses (Chapter 2):** Basic Combatives Course (BCC) — the foundation, taught by certified instructors; Tactical Combatives Course (TCC) — additional ground fighting, taught by TCC-certified instructors who also serve as assistant instructors for BCC and review platoon trainers' plans for quality control; Combatives Master Trainer Course (CMTC) — the instructor course; CMTC-certified instructors certify the others. Soldiers must be certified by a certified CMTC instructor, with a memorandum of training for all BCC graduates.

**Training areas and safety:** combatives can be conducted almost anywhere with little preparation; large grassy outdoor areas free of obstructions are suitable. Each Soldier needs **8 square feet** of training space; pair Soldiers by height and weight; when practicing throws or disarming techniques, Soldiers need twice the normal interval between ranks. Protective equipment: gloves, headgear, mats; certified personnel (referees or safeties) supervise. Training equipment includes rubber knives, bayonet scabbards, air guns, and electrically charged knives or stun guns of **no more than 100,000 volts** — and the Soldier being disarmed must never place a finger in the trigger guard during rifle and bayonet disarming. **Caution: to minimize weapon damage, limit weapon-to-weapon contact to half speed during training.** Training pads (NSN 8465-01-589-8803) are requisitioned through supply channels or purchased locally.

**Competition (Appendix A):** the bracketing scheme, matted-area layout, personnel and certification requirements, competitor actions and referee signals, competitor classification, uniforms, equipment, time limits, techniques allowed at each level, and points. **Points (Tables A-12, A-13):** passing the guard 3; sweep 3; knee mount 3; mount 4; rear mount 4; takedown to a non-dominant position 2; takedown to a dominant position 3; passivity 2 (awarded to the opponent when a fighter disengages from a top position); stalling 1 (three warnings, then a point to the opponent, repeating). Referees do not tolerate abuse of the points system — repeating techniques to accumulate points earns no points. **Basic drills and training evaluation (Appendix B)** and **training areas (Appendix C)** close the manual. → `references/system-and-training.md`

## Output Contract

When answering from this skill, lead with the strategy, not the technique: which range the fight is in, whether the goal is to create space (get to a weapon), maintain space, close the distance, or finish from a dominant position. Name the position on the hierarchy (rear mount > mount > side control > knee mount > guard) before naming the technique. If the question is about a specific technique, give the setup, the execution, and the common failure — in that order — and say what position it starts from and what position it ends in. Never present a technique as a trick: the manual's position is that there are no shortcuts, only systematic training against resistance.

## Operating Rules

1. **Position before submission.** A finish attempted from a bad position is a gift to the opponent.
2. **Every fight is over weapons.** Control of the weapon decides the outcome — create space to get to yours, close distance to deny his.
3. **Range is the number-one element.** Projectile, striking, clinching, grappling — know which range you are in and what it demands.
4. **Close the distance.** The willingness to close with the enemy is the defining characteristic of a Warrior.
5. **No shortcuts.** Skill comes only from systematic training and live training against a fully resistant partner.
6. **Train safely or don't train.** Protective equipment, certified supervision, 8 square feet per Soldier, half-speed weapon-to-weapon contact, no finger in the trigger guard.
