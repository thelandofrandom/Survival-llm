## System principles (paras 1-3 to 1-12)

**Why past systems failed (para 1-3):** commanders wanted a system that required no training time to learn and maintain, and training was often conducted by civilian martial-arts experts whose systems did not fit the Army's missions. Army combatives must be based in a system that lays a foundation of abilities Soldiers carry wherever they are assigned and is flexible enough to fit the wide range of specialized missions.

**Foundation (para 1-6):** institutional training builds the base — basic combatives in individual entry training (IET) and advanced individual training (AIT), then leading a unit program in the NCO Education System (NCOES) and Officer Education System (OES) leadership courses.

**Continuous training (para 1-7):** training must not end at graduation. The majority of development happens outside the institutional environment; units must develop their own programs and commanders must invest resources.

**Competition (para 1-8):** the principal motivational tool. Competitions encourage excellence and give Soldiers a reason to train; they are also the test that keeps the system honest.

**Drills (para 1-9):** techniques are drilled to automaticity before live training — the same logic as marksmanship: fundamentals first, then application under pressure.

**Live training (para 1-10):** training against a fully resistant partner, approaching the reality of combat. Many methods exist, each with strengths and weaknesses; leaders combine several approaches.

**Integrated training (para 1-11):** combative engagements happen as part of a mission, not in a vacuum. Combatives must become an integral part of mission training.

**Combat feedback (para 1-12):** lessons from real hand-to-hand combat are captured and analyzed so the program evolves. The three lessons: every fight is a grappling fight (striking happens as an integral part of grappling); every fight is over weapons (control of the weapon most likely determines the outcome); there is no shortcut to developing fighting skill.

## Safety and risk assessment (Chapter 1, paras 1-13+)

- **Tables 1-1 and 1-2:** distribution of injuries and the areas of the body most commonly injured (Armed Forces Health Surveillance Center study). The program's safety rules are built from this data — the places Soldiers actually get hurt are the places the rules protect.
- **Risk assessment:** every training event gets a deliberate risk assessment (Chapter 2, Deliberate Risk Assessment Worksheet). Physical controls include protective equipment (gloves, headgear, mats) and certified personnel (referees or safeties) supervising.
- **Protocol for injured Soldiers:** stop the training, assess, treat, and document — an injured Soldier who keeps training becomes a casualty.

## The three courses

| Course | Who it certifies | What it teaches / duties |
|---|---|---|
| Basic Combatives Course (BCC) | Individual Soldiers | The foundation: basic ground fighting, clinch, and the basic fight strategy. Graduates receive a memorandum of training. Instructors must be certified by a certified CMTC instructor |
| Tactical Combatives Course (TCC) | Platoon-level trainers | Additional ground-fighting techniques beyond BCC. TCC-certified Soldiers review platoon trainers' training plans for quality control and serve as assistant instructors for BCC |
| Combatives Master Trainer Course (CMTC) | Master trainers | The instructor course. CMTC-certified instructors certify BCC and TCC instructors and run the unit program |

Instruction during initial military training, NCOES, and OES is tabulated in Tables 2-1, 2-2, and 2-3 — combatives is threaded through the whole professional education system, not bolted on at the end.

## Training areas and equipment (paras 2-86 to 2-90, Appendix C)

- Combatives can be conducted almost anytime or anywhere with little preparation; large, grassy outdoor areas free of obstructions are suitable.
- **Each Soldier needs 8 square feet of training space.** Pair Soldiers by height and weight. When practicing throws or disarming techniques, Soldiers need **twice the normal interval** between ranks.
- PT formations may be used; if the extended rectangular formation is used, the first and third ranks face the second and fourth ranks, and ranks must be even in number so each Soldier has a partner (FM 7-22).
- **Training equipment** varies by skill: rubber knives, bayonet scabbards, air guns, electrically charged knives or stun guns of **no more than 100,000 volts** (Figure 2-2).
- **Safety guidelines for equipment:** the Soldier to be disarmed must not place a finger in the trigger guard during rifle and bayonet disarming; keep scabbards on knives and bayonets firmly attached to rifles while learning bayonet disarming; use electrically charged knives or stun guns of no more than 100,000 volts, bayonet scabbards, or rubber knives during knife disarming training.
- **Caution: to minimize weapon damage, limit weapon-to-weapon contact to half speed during training.**
- Training pads: NSN 8465-01-589-8803, requisitioned through supply channels or purchased locally.
- Protective equipment (Figure 2-1): headgear, gloves, mouthguard, groin protection, mats.

**Points — basic and standard competitions (Table A-12):**

| Competitor action | Points | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Passing the guard | 3 | From within the opponent's guard, clear the legs, pass the guard, gain side control, mount, knee mount, or North/South |
| Sweep | 3 | After establishing guard, the bottom fighter changes positions, placing the opponent on his back |
| Knee mount | 3 | One knee in the opponent's abdomen, the other off the ground and away from the opponent, stabilized |
| Mount | 4 | Astride the face-up opponent's chest or abdomen, both knees and feet on the ground, free from entanglement with the opponent's legs |
| Rear mount | 4 | Behind the opponent, both feet hooked around the opponent's thighs |
| Passivity | 2 | Awarded to the other fighter when a fighter disengages from a top position |
| Stalling | 1 | Using the clock to achieve victory: three warnings, then a point to the opponent; three more warnings, then another point, continuing until stalling stops or the match ends |
| Takedown, non-dominant position (standard only) | 2 | From standing, place the opponent on the ground, ending in the guard or behind the arms of a face-down opponent |
| Takedown, dominant position (standard only) | 3 | From standing, place the opponent on his back, pass the guard, gain side control, mount, knee mount, or North/South |

Table A-13 gives the points for intermediate competitions. **Guard, for competition purposes:** a position with the fighter's legs locked around an opponent with at least one leg above the knee, or, if the legs are not locked, controlling the opponent with the legs. **Referees do not tolerate abuse of the points system:** a competitor who repeats techniques to accumulate points earns no points for the repeats.
