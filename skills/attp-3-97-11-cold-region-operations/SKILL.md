# ATTP 3-97.11 Cold Region Operations

## Purpose

ATTP 3-97.11, *Cold Region Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 28 January 2011, 114 pages; published jointly with the Marine Corps as MCRP 3-35.1D) is the Army–Marine Corps tactics, techniques, and procedures manual for fighting in the cold. It covers the whole problem in six chapters and two appendices: what a cold region is and how its weather works, how to conduct full-spectrum operations in it, the special considerations for people and equipment, movement and maneuver, sustainment, and applying combat power — plus appendices on the Army cold-weather uniform and equipment posture and on Army-compatible heaters and tents.

**Supersession note — read this first.** ATTP 3-97.11 is superseded as current Army doctrine by **ATP 3-90.96, *Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations* (24 February 2025, including Change 2)**, already a skill in this vault as `atp-3-90-96-arctic-cold-weather`. Where the two disagree, follow ATP 3-90.96. ATTP 3-97.11 remains useful because it is the compact, joint Army–Marine TTP manual: its cold-temperature and snow categories, its cold-injury tables (hypothermia bands, immersion times, frostbite), and its weapons-effects section are the most directly usable in the vault for a leader planning a cold-weather operation, and its movement rates and sustainment planning factors are stated as planning numbers rather than buried in a larger Arctic campaign manual. Use ATP 3-90.96 as the authority and ATTP 3-97.11 as the field TTP reference.

The manual's opening warning frames everything that follows: the severe conditions of cold regions can render individuals and units combat ineffective **without ever engaging the enemy**. As energy resources dwindle, development in resource-rich cold regions will increase the probability of conflicts there — so the force must be able to fight in the cold, not merely survive it.

## 1. Defining Cold Regions
**1.1. What counts as a cold region.** For military purposes, cold regions are any regions where cold temperatures, unique terrain, and snowfall significantly affect military operations for **one month or more each year**. About one quarter of the earth's land mass is severely cold: mean annual air temperatures stay below freezing, maximum snow depths exceed **60 centimeters**, and ice covers lakes and rivers for more than **180 days each year**.

**1.2. Subregions.** Cold regions divide into three subregions (loosely derived from Bailey's ecoregions classification; see AR 70-38 for detail): - **Arctic:** south limit at the Arctic Circle (latitude **66°32' N**). Above it the sun never sets on the summer solstice and never rises on the winter solstice. Long, severe winters and short, cool summers; the mean monthly temperature of the warmest month hovers between **32°F and 50°F (0°C and 10°C)**. Annual precipitation less than **8 inches (200 mm)**, but low evaporation makes the climate humid. - **Subarctic:** between latitude **50°N** and the Arctic Circle. **Temperate:** varies greatly — maritime and continental zones, heavily forested areas, mountain ranges, deserts, plains; the effects of cold are generally short-term but can be catastrophic for unprepared units. At least one month of the year has a mean monthly temperature above **50°F (10°C)**. Snow cover exists for at least **6 months** in the lowlands; surrounding mountains can have perennial snow.

All three subregions can include mountains, which significantly complicate operations. **All mountain areas that receive snowfall are considered cold regions**, and performance starts to degrade after personnel ascend above **5,000 feet (1,500 meters)**. For mountain-specific detail the manual refers to FM 3-97.6 and FM 3-97.61.

**1.3. Oceans, land masses, and terrain.** Large bodies of water (maritime zones) and inland areas (continental zones) have the greatest overall effect on climate. Maritime zones have milder temperatures and more precipitation; continental zones have greater temperature extremes.

**1.4. Cold temperature categories.** The Army and Marine Corps use the same categories; the Army adds one more because it is designed to operate in colder temperatures: - **Wet cold:** +39°F to +20°F (4°C to −6°C). - **Dry cold:** +19°F to −4°F (−7°C to −20°C). - **Intense cold:** −5°F to −25°F (−20°C to −32°C). - **Extreme cold:** −25°F to −40°F (−32°C to −40°C). - **Hazardous cold (Army only):** −40°F (−40°C) and below.

**Wet cold** is often more dangerous than colder dry cold: wet snow and rain accompany it, the ground becomes slushy and muddy, clothing and equipment become perpetually wet, and water conducts heat **25 times faster than air** — core body temperature can drop quickly if troops become wet and then catch a wind. Wet cold can quickly lead to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot. The ground alternates between freezing and thawing, so areas trafficable when frozen can become severely restricted when the ground thaws — planning is problematic. **Hazardous cold** (below −40°F): commanders assume a great deal of risk engaging in operations at these temperatures; units need extensive training first.

**1.5. Snow categories.** Light snow: visibility equal to or greater than **5/8 mile (1,000 meters)** in falling snow; a trace to **1 inch (2.5 cm) per hour** accumulates. Moderate snow: visibility **5/16 to 1/2 mile (500–900 meters)**; **1–3 inches (2.5–7.6 cm) per hour**. Heavy snow: visibility less than **1/4 mile (400 meters)**; **3 or more inches per hour**.

**1.6. Weather phenomena.** Unique phenomena that affect operations: - **Ice fog:** occurs at **−30°F (−34°C) or colder** with a vapor source and still air. Moisture from combustion crystallizes into tiny ice particles. - **Blizzard:** sustained winds or frequent gusts to **35 mph (56 kph)** or greater, considerable falling or blowing snow, reduced visibility to a quarter-mile or less, for **three or more hours**. **Whiteout:** sunlight diffused through an unbroken cloud layer onto an unbroken snow surface — the horizon effectively disappears, with loss of depth perception and inability to distinguish terrain irregularities; restrict or cease movement until it clears. **Grayout:** over a snow-covered surface during twilight or when the sun is close to the horizon — overall grayness, horizon indistinguishable, travel difficult and dangerous. **Temperature inversions:** in mountainous areas inversions trap cold air in valleys and warm air above — affecting visibility, sound, and the performance of weapons and sensors. **Looming:** refraction that makes distant objects appear closer or displaced — a navigation and observation hazard. **Chinook winds:** warm, dry winds descending the lee side of mountains, rapidly raising temperatures and melting snow. **Aurora borealis:** can affect communications and navigation. **Light data:** in the arctic and subarctic, daylight varies from continuous to absent by season — plan movement, reconnaissance, and rest around it.
## 2. Conducting Full Spectrum Operations in Cold Regions
**2.1. Offense.** Offense is the decisive element in full-spectrum operations and the preferred posture of the Army — but in cold regions, maneuver is the hardest part. The most important element of a successful operation is the ability to maneuver to defeat or destroy the enemy, and the terrain and climate are not conducive to maneuvering.

**2.2. Characteristics of the offense in the cold.** Surprise: local surprise is harder when snow is present — the enemy can see moving objects on snow and hear movement (sound transmits better in cold air); surprise may come only from limited visibility, or from suddenly increasing the tempo of an initially slow attack when over-snow transportation is available. Execute assaults at night or in low visibility where possible — though in the arctic and subarctic, darkness depends on the season; it may be preferable to assault **without field artillery preparation fires** to preserve surprise. If attacking during a blizzard, **attack downwind or at a slight angle**, forcing the enemy to face the full force of the storm.

**2.3. Types of offensive operations.** The same four types as in temperate regions — movement to contact, attack, exploitation, pursuit — with emphasis on easy transition between them to capitalize on gains. Movement to contact: as mobility decreases, use a smaller force to establish contact; search-and-attack by cold-region-skilled elements can deny large areas and collect information.

**2.4. Defense, retrograde, stability, civil support.** Defensive operations in the cold use the same types as elsewhere, with obstacles constructed from snow and ice (snow walls, ice obstacles, frozen water as a barrier) and positions dug into frozen ground where possible. Retrograde operations must account for the slow movement of a withdrawing force and the risk of abandoning equipment that cannot be moved. **Patrol base operations** in the cold require heated shelters or snow shelters, security that can function in limited visibility, and a sustainment plan that accounts for the fuel and food consumption of a cold, static force.

**2.5. Tactical considerations.** The operations process in the cold is the same as elsewhere, but every step takes longer and costs more: planning, preparation, and execution all need additional time, and the staff must account for the cold's effect on every warfighting function. → `references/cold-region-operations.md`

## 3. Considerations for Operations in Cold Regions
**3.1. Personnel considerations.** Leaders must know their soldiers' and Marines' cold-weather experience, physical condition, and equipment state before committing them. Acclimatization to cold takes time; a unit arriving from a temperate climate is not a cold-weather unit on day one.

**3.2. Environmental risk factors.** Risk is a combination of the environment (temperature, wind, wetness, exposure time), the mission (exertion level, exposure, rest opportunities), and the individual (fitness, body composition, clothing, experience). **Frostbite risk** is assessed from the wind-chill chart: light grey means frostbite in 30 minutes, medium grey 10 minutes, dark grey 5 minutes. The wind-chill formula used in the manual is: Wind Chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75(V^0.16) + 0.4275T(V^0.16), where T is air temperature (°F) and V is wind speed (mph). **Skin cannot freeze (so frostbite cannot occur) at temperatures above 32°F (0°C), even when the equivalent wind-chill temperature is below 32°F.** Mission risk factors include exposure duration, work rate (sweating then chilling), and rest cycles. Individual risk factors include low body fat and high surface-area-to-mass ratio (cools faster), not having eaten in over 24 hours, and fatigue from physical exhaustion or sustained operations.

**3.3. Hypothermia.** Hypothermia occurs when core body temperature falls below **95°F (35°C)**. Mild hypothermia: core temperature **90–95°F (32–35°C)**. Moderate: **80–89°F (27–32°C)**. Severe: below **80°F (27°C)**. **Mild hypothermia symptoms:** confusion, sleepiness, slurred speech, shallow breathing, weak pulse, change in behavior or appearance, intense shivering, stiffness in arms or legs, poor control over body movements, slow reactions, abnormal heart rhythms (usually at the lower end of the 90–95°F range). **Warning signs:** as core temperature falls, shivering is the most noticeable symptom — shivering alone does not indicate hypothermia, but it indicates the body is having a problem with cold stress. **Response:** exchange wet clothing for dry, insulated clothing; conduct physical exercise; hydrate with warm liquids; add heat (shelter, sleeping bag, blankets). **Moderate and severe hypothermia symptoms:** dilated pupils, lack of shivering, low blood pressure, unconsciousness, absent or abnormal heart rhythms. As core temperature falls it may be difficult or impossible to detect signs of life — medical personnel have revived victims with core temperatures as low as **60°F (16°C)**, hence the adage "a person is not dead until they are warm and dead." Treatment requires immediate hospitalization; rescuers keep the casualty quiet and supine, do not jar or move the patient suddenly, do not let the casualty assist in the evacuation, and for prolonged litter evacuations use a full body-vapor barrier system as appropriate.

**3.4. Immersion syndrome (nonfreezing cold injury).** Developed by tissues exposed to cold-wet conditions — usually between **32°F and 60°F (0°C and 16°C)** — for prolonged periods (about **4 to 5 days minimum**). Occurs most often in the feet; in extreme cold regions, wearing vapor barrier boots for long periods with wet socks often causes it. **Prevention:** keep feet warm and dry; change wet socks as soon as possible; remove boots and allow feet to dry for **2 to 3 hours minimum per day** as mission permits; wipe the inside of vapor barrier boots dry at least once per day; place boots in the sleeping bag at night so body heat dries them and keeps them pliable; place an absorbent synthetic chamois towel or paper towels in boots overnight to draw out moisture.

**Immersion times (Table 3-4):** the allowable exposure time for the body core temperature to fall to 95.9°F (35.5°C), for average personnel. At **50–54°F** water: ankle-deep 7 hours (3.5 if raining), knee-deep 5 hours (2.5 if raining), waist-deep 1.5 hours (1 if raining), neck-deep 5 minutes. At **55–59°F**: 8 hours (4), 7 hours (3.5), 2 hours (1.5), 5 minutes. At **60–64°F**: 9 hours (4.5), 8 hours (4), 3.5 hours (2.5), 10 minutes. At **65–69°F**: 12 hours (6), 12 hours (6), 6 hours (5), 10 minutes. Above **70°F**: no limit except neck-deep, 30 minutes. Some soldiers cool faster than the table predicts — low body fat, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, not having eaten in over 24 hours, and fatigue all shorten the time.

**3.5. Frostbite.** Frostbite is frozen body tissue; ambient air temperature must be below **32°F (0°C)** for it to occur (wind chill alone below freezing cannot freeze skin). It generally occurs on exposed skin or extremities — nose, ears, cheeks, hands, feet. Contact frostbite occurs when bare skin is cooled quickly by contact with an extremely cold object; frostbite can also occur instantaneously when skin contacts super-cooled liquids that do not freeze at 32°F — gasoline, petroleum products, antifreeze, and alcohol (including alcohol-based wipes). **Superficial frostbite:** the affected area feels "wooden"; skin initially turns red, later pale gray or waxy white; the affected skin moves freely over pliable underlying layers. **Deep frostbite:** skin is cold and firm; it will not move over underlying layers; at a minimum the upper layers of skin are involved and it can include all tissues down to and including bone. The **rebound method** is the field-expedient test for superficial versus deep frostbite (press and release the skin and observe whether it rebounds). **Treatment:** all frostbite cases must be evaluated by professionals in a medical facility; treat hypothermia or other life-threatening injuries first. **Once a tissue has thawed, it must never re-freeze** — thawing and re-freezing causes additional damage.

**3.6. Weapons considerations.** Weapons function under extreme cold if given proper care. **Sluggishness:** lubricants used in temperate conditions — cleaning, lubricating, and preservative compound (CLP) — thicken in the cold and cause stoppages or sluggish action; **CLP freezes at −35°F (−37°C)**. **Visibility:** firing in still air below **−30°F (−34°C)** creates ice fog — hot propellant gases condense water vapor, which freezes into a cloud that hangs over the weapon, follows the projectile's path, obstructs the gunner's vision, and reveals his position; fire at a slower rate and/or relocate to an alternate firing position. For detailed technical issues, consult FM 9-207 and the applicable technical manuals. **Condensation:** when weapons move from the cold into a warmer environment they "sweat"; if returned to the cold without removing the condensation, the sweat turns to ice and causes stoppages. Leave weapons outside when temperatures are below freezing, sheltered but readily accessible. **Fouling from snow and ice:** use muzzle caps or improvise with plastic bags, tape, or condoms; close ejection port covers; carry a de-icer; cycle the weapon periodically to keep parts from freezing. **Breakage and malfunctions:** extreme cold makes metal and plastic more brittle; breakage generally occurs early when a cold weapon is fired — begin firing small arms at a slow rate of fire in extreme cold if the tactical situation permits.

**3.7. Communications and information systems equipment.** Cold affects radios, batteries, and cables — batteries lose capacity in the cold (keep them warm, carry spares inside clothing); cables stiffen and break; antenna systems ice up. Acoustic sensors are affected by cold air's better sound transmission (sounds carry farther, which aids detection but also aids the enemy).
## 4. Movement and Maneuver in Cold Regions
**4.1. Movement by individuals.** Individual snow movement techniques are the most reliable form of transportation in the cold region environment. Three major techniques: **snowshoeing, skiing, and skijoring**. In snow less than **12 inches (30 cm)** deep, personnel generally need no special equipment; deeper than 12 inches, leaders consider skis and snowshoes. **Snowshoes** are the easiest to use and most easily trained — the minimum skill all soldiers and Marines should possess; use them in rough terrain, thick brush, or when there is no time or equipment to become proficient with skis. **Skis** move soldiers quickly into remote areas vehicles cannot access — much quicker infiltration and exfiltration than snowshoes; best for raids and reconnaissance and surveillance; the disadvantage is training time — the Northern Warfare Training Center estimates **40 to 50 hours** for most soldiers to attain proficiency with the standard combat load, and skiing expertise is maintained in only a few Army schools. **Skijoring** is pulling individuals on skis with a snowmobile or small unit support vehicle (SUSV) — very little energy to hold the rope and be pulled along; moves personnel and equipment to a staging area quickly; soldiers carry only uniform and assigned weapon while skijoring (all other equipment goes in the vehicle); speeds up to **15 mph (24 kph)** — cover all exposed skin.

**4.2. Route planning for individual movement.** Conduct a map reconnaissance — going around terrain features may be faster than going over them; select the flattest route with the least ascending and descending. Decide skis or snowshoes based on individual proficiency and the route's terrain.

**4.3. Rates of march.** The normal planning rate for troops on hard-packed, gently rolling terrain is **2.5 mph (4 kph)**; crossing snow or hilly terrain, the rate decreases (Table 4-1 in the manual gives rates by snow depth and slope). Terrain angle affects mobility: moving uphill, add **one hour for every 1,000 feet (304 m)** increase in elevation; moving downhill, add **one hour for every 1,600 feet (488 m)** decrease — downhill is counterintuitively slower, because troops have problems descending. Broken and unbroken trail have specific meanings: in undisturbed (unbroken) snow greater than 12 inches, the lead two or three personnel pack the trail for those behind; a broken trail is one already packed by passage.

**4.4. Movement by vehicles.** Route selection in the cold must account for freeze and thaw cycles — a route trafficable when frozen can become impassable when it thaws, and traversing terrain during freeze–thaw cycles is a distinct planning problem. Vehicle types in the cold include wheeled, tracked, and over-snow vehicles (SUSV and snowmobiles); each has different snow-depth, slope, and temperature limits (Table 4-2 in the manual). Rotary-wing: CH-47 Chinook (normal maximum **27 passengers**; authorized cargo load 27 passengers, or 27 passengers and 3 ahkios, or 24 passengers and 4 ahkios; for each snowmobile, delete 2 ahkios or 6 passengers) and UH-60 Blackhawk (normal maximum **11 passengers**; 11 passengers, or 7 passengers and 1 ahkio). Fixed-wing: runways can be built by grading and compacting snow; aircraft with skis need about **15 percent more** landing and takeoff space than wheeled aircraft; airfields can be built on frozen lakes and rivers after engineers complete an ice reconnaissance. **Movement over water:** frozen lakes and rivers become routes when the ice is thick enough — ice thickness must be verified before crossing with troops or vehicles. **Movement by air:** aviation assets effectively overcome cold-region terrain and weather challenges, but they are fragile and leaders must not rely solely on aviation.
## 5. Sustainment in Cold Regions
**5.1. The problem.** Sustainment is the related tasks and systems that provide support and services to ensure freedom of action, extend operational reach, and prolong endurance. The ability to engage and destroy an enemy is only as great as the unit's ability to resupply and sustain itself.

**5.2. Sustainment plans.** Plans must account for the cold's effect on every class of supply: fuel consumption rises (heating, idling, slower movement); food consumption rises (the body burns more calories to stay warm); batteries fail in the cold; water must be obtained by melting snow or ice; ammunition, construction materials, and heaters must be pushed forward before an attack (if a hasty defense is anticipated, push construction materials, heaters, and additional ammunition to the forward troops immediately). **Movement requirements:** sustainment movement must be planned around the same freeze–thaw and visibility constraints as tactical movement, with alternate routes and prepositioned stocks. **Movement requirements:** sustainment movement must be planned around the same freeze–thaw and visibility constraints as tactical movement, with alternate routes and prepositioned stocks. **Personnel services:** mail, pay, and personnel support continue but take longer and depend on the same constrained movement. **Medical plans:** cold-weather injuries are a planning factor — the medical plan must account for hypothermia, frostbite, and immersion syndrome as routine casualties, with warming shelters, litter evacuation routes, and medical facilities that can receive cold casualties.
## 6. Applying Combat Power in Cold Regions
**6.1. Movement and maneuver.** Applying combat power in the cold starts with movement — the force that cannot move cannot fight. Terrain and weather considerations dominate the plan: snow depth, ice, visibility, and temperature determine what is possible and what is not. **Intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance** in the cold must account for sensor degradation (optics fogging, batteries failing, acoustic sensors behaving differently in cold air) and for the enemy's signatures in snow (tracks, ice fog, heat signatures against a cold background). **Command and control** is harder in the cold — radios fail, batteries die, and leaders must plan for degraded communications, including wire, runners, and visual signals.

## Operating Rules

1. **The cold is the second enemy.** It can make a unit combat ineffective without a shot fired.
2. **Wet cold kills faster than dry cold.** Water conducts heat 25 times faster than air — stay dry first, stay warm second.
3. **Buddy teams are the safety system.** No one watches his own face for frostbite.
4. **Weapons outside, dry, and on LAW.** CLP freezes at −35°F; condensation ices a weapon brought inside and taken out again.
5. **Move on the cold's terms.** 2.5 mph on hard-packed rolling ground, slower in snow; add an hour per 1,000 feet up and per 1,600 feet down.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-90.96 (2025) supersedes ATTP 3-97.11 where they conflict; ATTP 3-97.11 is the compact joint TTP reference, not the authority.
