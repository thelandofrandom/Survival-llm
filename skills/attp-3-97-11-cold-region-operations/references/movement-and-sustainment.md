# Movement and Sustainment in Cold Regions — ATTP 3-97.11

## Movement by Individuals

Individual snow movement techniques are the most reliable form of transportation in the cold region environment. Ideally, soldiers and Marines who deploy to a cold region should be familiar with and trained in the equipment that facilitates individual movement.

**Three major techniques:** snowshoeing, skiing, and skijoring. Each has its own planning considerations, advantages, and disadvantages. In snow less than **12 inches (30 cm)** deep, personnel generally need no special equipment; when snow cover is deeper than 12 inches, leaders consider special cold-region movement techniques using skis and snowshoes. The Army has fielded special kits to aid movement in cold and mountainous regions — units that need them should contact the Army Mountain Warfare School, Northern Warfare Training Center, or Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center, since the kits continually evolve.

**Snowshoes:** the easiest to use and most easily trained method — the minimum skill all soldiers and Marines should possess. Use snowshoes in rough terrain, in thick brush, or when there is no equipment or time to become proficient with skis. Units can easily implement a snowshoe training plan.

**Skis:** enable soldiers to move quickly into remote areas vehicles cannot access — much quicker infiltration and exfiltration than snowshoes. Best for raids and reconnaissance and surveillance. Most individuals will not require skis; specialized units (Rangers, Special Forces, Scouts) have the most utility for them. The disadvantage is training time: the Northern Warfare Training Center estimates **40 to 50 hours** for most soldiers to attain proficiency using skis with the standard combat load, and skiing expertise is maintained in only a few Army schools. Commanders who deem it crucial should contact the Northern Warfare Training Center, the Army's Mountain Warfare School, 10th Special Forces, or the Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center.

**Skijoring:** pulling individuals on skis with a snowmobile or small unit support vehicle (SUSV). It takes very little energy to hold the rope and be pulled along, and it moves personnel and equipment to a staging area quickly. Depending on the SUSV model, all equipment normally carried goes in the vehicle — soldiers carry only uniform and assigned weapon while skijoring. Like skiing, skijoring is training-intensive. Personnel must cover all exposed skin — they travel up to **15 mph (24 kph)** while skijoring.

**Methods to use equipment:** the manual covers the use of sleds (ahkios and pulks) for hauling loads over snow — pulling a sled is more efficient than carrying a heavy rucksack in deep snow, and the load can be larger.

## Route Planning for Individual Movement

- Conduct a map reconnaissance. Going around terrain features may be faster than going over them; check the contour and select the flattest route with the least ascending and descending.
- Decide if personnel will use skis or snowshoes. Check the proficiency of individuals. Determine which route (and its terrain) individuals can best negotiate.
- Decide if personnel will carry heavy rucksacks or pull sleds. Check the predicted temperatures during movement.
- Determine if and how soldiers will camouflage their tracks. In barren areas or above the treeline, tracks may be difficult or impossible to conceal. In open terrain, break only one set of tracks — aircraft can more easily spot several tracks than a single set.
- Decide if the route is feasible during conditions of limited visibility, which often occur in cold regions.
- Check if the route crosses any potential avalanche areas. As far as avalanche-prone slopes are concerned, avoid them at all costs.
- Identify potential obstacles: have streams and other bodies of water frozen sufficiently to support troops and vehicles? What is the water level in streams? Can individuals negotiate high snow banks? Do plowed roads perpendicular to the route have high banks of plowed snow?

**Rates of march:** the normal planning rate for troops on hard-packed, gently rolling terrain is **2.5 mph (4 kph)**. When crossing snow or hilly terrain, this rate decreases (Table 4-1 in the manual gives rates by snow depth and slope, calculated with personnel wearing the typical combat load). Terrain angle affects mobility: moving uphill, add **one hour for every 1,000 feet (304 m)** increase in elevation; moving downhill, add **one hour for every 1,600 feet (488 m)** decrease — downhill is counterintuitively slower, because troops have problems descending. **Broken and unbroken trail:** when moving through undisturbed (unbroken) snow greater than 12 inches (30 cm), the lead two or three personnel pack the trail for those behind; a broken trail is one already packed by passage — movement on a broken trail is much faster, so the lead element rotates to spread the work.

## Movement by Vehicles

**Route selection:** must account for freeze and thaw cycles — a route trafficable when frozen can become impassable when it thaws. **Traversing terrain during freeze and thaw cycles** is a distinct planning problem: the same ground changes character daily or even hourly, and a route that was passable in the morning may be a bog by afternoon.

**Vehicle types:** wheeled, tracked, and over-snow vehicles (SUSV and snowmobiles). Each has different snow-depth, slope, and temperature limits (Table 4-2 in the manual). **Vehicular considerations:** cold thickens lubricants, reduces battery output, stiffens tires and tracks, and makes metal brittle — winterize vehicles, carry recovery equipment, and plan for reduced speeds and increased fuel consumption. **Movement over water:** frozen lakes and rivers become routes when the ice is thick enough — ice thickness must be verified before crossing with troops or vehicles, and the manual gives the reconnaissance and marking procedures. Vehicles should cross ice one at a time, spaced apart, with the route marked.

## Movement by Air

Aviation assets effectively overcome cold-region terrain and weather challenges, but they are fragile and leaders must not rely solely on aviation for support. Many factors can limit aviation's effectiveness and the amount of combat force it can support.

**Rotary-wing:** the CH-47 Chinook and the UH-60 Blackhawk. **CH-47:** normally transports a maximum of **27 passengers**; authorized cargo load (ACL): 27 passengers maximum; 27 passengers and 3 ahkios; 24 passengers and 4 ahkios; for each snowmobile, 2 ahkios or 6 passengers are deleted from the maximum load. **UH-60:** normally transports **11 passengers**; ACL: 11 passengers maximum, or 7 passengers and 1 ahkio. **Fixed-wing:** many potential landing sites exist in cold regions; runways can be built by grading and compacting snow; aircraft equipped with skis require about **15 percent more** landing and takeoff space than wheeled aircraft; airfields can be built on frozen lakes and rivers after engineers complete a suitable ice reconnaissance. **Ice fog:** fixed-wing aircraft can create an ice fog that covers an entire runway, reducing visibility so other aircraft cannot take off or land if the wind is calm — the ice fog also draws attention to the airfield location.

**Fire support:** aviation fire support planning in the cold does not differ in general from temperate environments, but tactical air support takes on more importance since other weapon platforms are limited by the lack of road networks and terrain. Planners consider additional assets to compensate. Rarely use door gunners in cold-region operations — with open doors, gunners, flight crew, and passengers risk frostbite too quickly; attack helicopters provide the suppressive fires. Units may eliminate door gunners at other times to increase ground combat power when limited lift is available or distances are greatly extended.

## Sustainment in Cold Regions

Sustainment is the related tasks and systems that provide support and services to ensure freedom of action, extend operational reach, and prolong endurance (the Marine Corps lists logistics instead of sustainment as a warfighting function). The ability to engage and destroy an enemy is only as great as the unit's ability to resupply and sustain itself.

**Planning considerations:** sustainment plans must account for the cold's effect on every class of supply. Fuel consumption rises (heating, idling, slower movement). Food consumption rises (the body burns more calories to stay warm). Batteries fail in the cold. Water must be obtained by melting snow or ice. Ammunition, construction materials, and heaters must be pushed forward before an attack — if a hasty defense is anticipated, push construction materials, heaters, and additional ammunition to the forward troops immediately. **Sustainment plans:** build the plan around the movement constraints — preposition stocks forward, use aircraft for resupply where the ground cannot be trusted, and plan for the interruption of resupply by weather. **Movement requirements:** sustainment movement must be planned around the same freeze–thaw and visibility constraints as tactical movement, with alternate routes and prepositioned stocks. **Personnel services:** mail, pay, and personnel support continue but take longer and depend on the same constrained movement. **Medical plans:** cold-weather injuries are a planning factor — the medical plan must account for hypothermia, frostbite, and immersion syndrome as routine casualties, with warming shelters, litter evacuation routes, and medical facilities that can receive cold casualties.
