# Cold Injuries and Weapons in the Cold — ATTP 3-97.11

## Personnel Considerations and Risk Factors

Leaders must know their soldiers' and Marines' cold-weather experience, physical condition, and equipment state before committing them. Acclimatization to cold takes time; a unit arriving from a temperate climate is not a cold-weather unit on day one. Buddy teams are the basic unit of cold-weather survival — each man watches his buddy for the signs of cold injury.

**Risk factors:**
- **Environmental:** temperature, wind, wetness, exposure time.
- **Mission:** exertion level (sweating then chilling), exposure duration, rest opportunities.
- **Individual:** fitness, body composition, clothing, experience. Personnel with low body fat and a high surface-area-to-mass ratio cool faster; personnel who have not eaten in over 24 hours are more susceptible, as are those fatigued by physical exhaustion or sustained operations.

**Frostbite risk and wind chill:** the manual's wind-chill chart gives frostbite times by shading — light grey means frostbite in **30 minutes**, medium grey **10 minutes**, dark grey **5 minutes**. The wind-chill formula is: Wind Chill (°F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75(V^0.16) + 0.4275T(V^0.16), where T is air temperature (°F) and V is wind speed (mph). **Wet skin significantly decreases the time for frostbite to occur** — wet skin plus wind creates a wet-skin wind chill worse than the dry chart (Table 3-2 in the manual). Rotor wash can create dangerously low wind-chill temperatures; troops exposed in uncovered or drafty vehicles face the same risk. **Skin cannot freeze (so frostbite cannot occur) at temperatures above 32°F (0°C), even when the equivalent wind-chill temperature is below 32°F.**

## Hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when core body temperature falls below **95°F (35°C)**. Classification by core temperature:
- **Mild:** 90–95°F (32–35°C).
- **Moderate:** 80–89°F (27–32°C).
- **Severe:** below 80°F (27°C).

Rectal temperature measurement provides the only accurate core body temperature — in the field, diagnose from obvious signs and symptoms. All levels are potentially life-threatening medical emergencies requiring immediate care in a medical facility.

**Warning signs:** as core temperature falls, shivering is the most noticeable symptom. Shivering alone does not indicate hypothermia, but it indicates the body is having a problem with cold stress.

**Mild hypothermia symptoms:** confusion, sleepiness, slurred speech, shallow breathing, weak pulse, change in behavior or appearance, intense shivering, stiffness in the arms or legs, poor control over body movements, slow reactions, abnormal heart rhythms (usually at the lower end of the 90–95°F range).

**Response to mild hypothermia:** exchange wet clothing for dry, insulated clothing; conduct physical exercise; hydrate with warm liquids; add heat (shelter, sleeping bag, blankets, or a combination). In all circumstances, evacuate the individual to a medical facility. Medical personnel must monitor the heart; if abnormal heart rhythms are detected, the patient must not exercise or assist in the evacuation — prepare for litter evacuation.

**Moderate and severe hypothermia symptoms:** dilated pupils, lack of shivering, low blood pressure, unconsciousness, absent or abnormal heart rhythms. As core temperature falls, it may be difficult or impossible to detect signs of life. Medical personnel have revived victims with core temperatures as low as **60°F (16°C)** — hence the adage, "A person is not dead until they are warm and dead." Treatment requires immediate hospitalization; there is a high risk of heart failure. Rescuers carefully prepare the casualty for litter evacuation (gently placed in a sleeping bag on a sleeping pad or equivalent, then into the litter), keep the casualty quiet and supine, do not jar or move the patient suddenly, do not let the casualty assist in the evacuation. For prolonged litter evacuations, a full body-vapor barrier system may be appropriate.

## Immersion Syndrome (Nonfreezing Cold Injury)

Immersion syndrome is a nonfreezing cold injury developed by tissues exposed to cold-wet conditions — usually between **32°F and 60°F (0°C and 16°C)** — for prolonged periods (about **4 to 5 days minimum**). It can occur in any tissue but occurs most often in the feet. In extreme cold region environments, individuals who wear vapor barrier boots for long periods with wet socks often develop it. Feet initially appear swollen and red with a feeling of numbness; tissue may become pale in more serious injuries; aches, increased pain sensitivity, and infections accompany it.

**Treatment:** prevent further exposure; remove wet or constrictive clothing; wash and dry extremities gently; elevate and cover limbs with layers of loose, warm, dry clothing; evacuate to a medical facility; touch affected areas carefully — do not pop blisters, apply lotions, massage, expose to extreme heat, or have the individual walk.

**Prevention:** keep feet warm and dry; change wet socks as soon as possible; as mission permits, remove boots and allow feet to dry for **2 to 3 hours minimum per day**; wipe the inside of vapor barrier boots dry at least once per day or more often as feet sweat; place boots in the sleeping bag at night so body heat dries them and keeps them pliable; place an absorbent synthetic chamois towel or paper towels in boots overnight to draw out moisture. These techniques work with either leather boots or the vapor barrier boot.

**Immersion chart (Table 3-4)** — allowable exposure time for the body core temperature to fall to 95.9°F (35.5°C), for average personnel:

| Water temp (°F) | Ankle-deep | Knee-deep | Waist-deep | Neck-deep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50–54 | 7 hrs (3.5 if raining) | 5 hrs (2.5) | 1.5 hrs (1) | 5 min |
| 55–59 | 8 hrs (4) | 7 hrs (3.5) | 2 hrs (1.5) | 5 min |
| 60–64 | 9 hrs (4.5) | 8 hrs (4) | 3.5 hrs (2.5) | 10 min |
| 65–69 | 12 hrs (6) | 12 hrs (6) | 6 hrs (5) | 10 min |
| >70 | No limit | No limit | No limit | 30 min |

Some soldiers cool faster than the table predicts (low body fat, high surface-area-to-mass ratio, not eaten in over 24 hours, fatigue). Time limits when immersed to the neck are very short to avoid drowning.

## Frostbite

Frostbite is frozen body tissue. Ambient air temperature must be below **32°F (0°C)** for it to occur; if ambient temperature is above 32°F but wind chill is below, frostbite cannot occur. It generally occurs on exposed skin or extremities — nose, ears, cheeks, hands, feet. Contact frostbite occurs when bare skin is cooled quickly by contact with an extremely cold object. Frostbite can also occur instantaneously when skin contacts super-cooled liquids that do not freeze at 32°F — gasoline, petroleum products, antifreeze, and alcohol (including alcohol-based wipes). Four degrees of frostbite exist by tissue involvement (only a medical doctor can diagnose the degree); for field diagnosis and treatment, frostbite is classified as superficial or deep.

**Signs:** the first sign is numbness. As skin cools, individuals feel tingling, burning, aching, sharp pain, and decreased sensation, replaced by numbness as the skin freezes. **Superficial frostbite:** the affected area feels "wooden"; skin initially turns red, later pale gray or waxy white (in dark-skinned individuals, the skin often remains red); the affected skin moves freely over pliable underlying layers. **Deep frostbite:** skin is cold and firm; it will not move over underlying layers; at a minimum the upper layers of skin are involved and it can include all tissues down to and including bone. The **rebound method** is the field-expedient test for superficial versus deep frostbite (press the skin and watch whether it rebounds).

**Treatment:** professionals in a medical facility must evaluate all frostbite cases. Treat hypothermia or other life-threatening injuries first. Superficial frostbite can be re-warmed at room temperature or with skin-to-skin contact — cover cheeks or nose with warm hands, place uncovered fingers under opposite armpits, place bare feet against the belly of a companion. Deep frostbite: move the patient to a warm shelter and keep warm for the duration of the evacuation; re-warm with the same techniques or the warm-water bath technique described in the manual. Insulate and protect thawed injuries from re-freezing during evacuation — **once a tissue has thawed, it must never re-freeze**; thawing and re-freezing causes additional damage.

**Prevention:** buddy teams must watch each other closely for signs and symptoms — soldiers may not even be aware they are developing frostbite, especially on the face. Always use contact gloves to handle equipment — never allow bare hands to contact equipment. Always use approved gloves to handle fuel and petroleum, oil, and lubricant products.

## Weapons Considerations

Weapons function under extreme cold if given proper care. Units should consult FM 9-207 and applicable technical manuals for a more detailed listing of technical issues.

**Sluggishness:** lubricants used in temperate conditions — cleaning, lubricating, and preservative compound (CLP) — thicken in cold regions and cause stoppages or sluggish weapon action. **CLP freezes at −35°F (−37°C).** To eliminate the problem, strip the weapon completely, thoroughly clean it, and lubricate with lubricating oil arctic, weapons (LAW); at a minimum, lightly oil the camming surfaces of the bolt with LAW and leave the rest dry. LAW is available in one-quart containers, not the refillable half-ounce bottles normally found in weapons cleaning kits. Without LAW, use a dry graphite lubricant or fire the weapon dry.

**Condensation:** condensation ("sweating") forms on weapons when they move from the cold into a warmer environment. If weapons return to the cold without removing the condensation, the sweat turns to ice and causes stoppages. Leave weapons outside when temperatures are below freezing — readily accessible but sheltered, so ice and snow do not get into the working parts (sights, barrel). If weapons must be taken inside for cleaning, they will continue to sweat for approximately **one hour** after coming into a warm shelter — wait until the sweating stops before cleaning thoroughly. If weapons are kept in heated shelters, keep them near but not on the floor to minimize condensation.

**Fouling from snow and ice:** use muzzle caps (expendable — request from the unit armorer) or improvise with plastic bags, tape, or condoms. Close ejection port covers. Carry something to de-ice a weapon if part of it becomes frozen — windshield wiper fluid in a small bottle, aircraft de-icer, or antifreeze all work. Cycle the weapon periodically to keep parts from freezing and to identify icing issues.

**Visibility issues:** firing in still air below **−30°F (−34°C)** creates ice fog — hot propellant gases condense water vapor, which freezes into a cloud that hangs over the weapon, follows the projectile's path, obstructs the gunner's vision, and reveals his location. Fire at a slower rate and/or relocate to an alternate firing position. Tests have shown that even in warmer temperatures, fog develops around the gun — hot gases and the gunner's breath create it, making it difficult to observe the strike of rounds; for crew-served weapons, the assistant gunner may need to take a position further left or right to help with adjustments; for individual weapons, change position frequently. Optics: never breathe on the sight (condensation clouds it); allow space between the eye and the sight (the warmth of the face can cloud it); when moving optics from cold to warm, allow them to adjust slowly to avoid cracking the lens.

**Breakage and malfunctions:** extreme cold makes metal and plastic more brittle. Breakage generally occurs early when a cold weapon is fired — the metal heats and rapid, unequal expansion of parts occurs. Begin firing small arms at a slow rate of fire in extreme cold if the tactical situation permits; the slower rate greatly reduces potential malfunctions. Freezing of moisture from sweating or accumulated snow and ice also causes malfunctions and stoppages. After firing, the heat generated can melt snow or ice the weapon touches; the water then re-freezes and may cause a malfunction — use de-icer and cycle the weapon periodically. Unit armorers need to carry extra parts to overcome these problems.

**Considerations for specific weapons systems:** the manual gives cold-weather considerations for specific systems (individual weapons, crew-served weapons, mortars, antitank systems, air defense, and others) — consult the system technical manual and FM 9-207 for the details of each.

## Communications and Information Systems Equipment

Cold affects radios, batteries, and cables: batteries lose capacity in the cold (keep them warm, carry spares inside clothing against the body); cables stiffen and break; antenna systems ice up (ice loading changes antenna characteristics and can break the antenna). **Acoustic sensors** are affected by cold air's better sound transmission — sounds carry farther, which aids detection but also aids the enemy's detection of friendly movement. Shelter requirements: personnel and equipment shelters must be heated, ventilated, and protected from wind — see Appendix B for compatible heaters and tents, and Appendix A for the uniform and equipment posture by temperature category.
