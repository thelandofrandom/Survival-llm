# Cold Region Environment — ATTP 3-97.11

## Defining Cold Regions

For military purposes, cold regions are any regions where cold temperatures, unique terrain, and snowfall significantly affect military operations for **one month or more each year**. About one quarter of the earth's land mass is severely cold: mean annual air temperatures stay below freezing, maximum snow depths exceed **60 centimeters**, and ice covers lakes and rivers for more than **180 days each year**. Another quarter of the earth is moderately cold — mean temperatures during the coldest month are below freezing, including most of the United States and Eurasia.

## Subregions

Cold regions divide into three subregions (loosely derived from Bailey's ecoregions classification; see AR 70-38 for detail):

**Arctic:** south limit at the Arctic Circle (latitude **66°32' N**). Above it the sun never sets on the summer solstice and never rises on the winter solstice. Long, severe winters and short, cool summers; the mean monthly temperature of the warmest month hovers between **32°F and 50°F (0°C and 10°C)**. Annual precipitation less than **8 inches (200 mm)**, but low evaporation makes the climate humid. Vegetation is low-growing grasses, lichens, mosses, and brush on treeless plains. Soils are poorly developed with a permanently frozen sub-layer (permafrost) that may thaw seasonally at the surface; drainage is poor, creating muddy summertime conditions. Coastal plains, low-interior and high-interior plains, and lesser areas of low and high-relief mountains. Most development and infrastructure of military interest centers on ports and areas with valuable natural resources. Few roads or towns exist.

**Subarctic:** between latitude **50°N** and the Arctic Circle. At least one month of the year has a mean monthly temperature above **50°F (10°C)**. Precipitation falls mostly in the summer months. Vegetation consists primarily of needle-leaf forests and open woodlands. Soils are acidic, seasonally frozen, and contain discontinuous permafrost. Numerous lakes, ponds, peat bogs, and swamps exist because of poor subsurface drainage. Coastal plains, high-relief and low-relief mountains, lesser areas of low-interior and high-interior plains, and extensive areas of rock and non-cohesive sand. More infrastructure than the arctic, but vast undeveloped areas with few roads dominate. The largest temperature range in the world exists here. Snow cover exists for at least **6 months** in the lowlands; surrounding mountains can have perennial snow cover.

**Temperate:** varies greatly — maritime and continental zones, heavily forested areas, mountain ranges, deserts, and plains. The effects of cold are generally short-term, but they can be catastrophic for unprepared units.

All three subregions can include mountains, which significantly complicate operations. **All mountain areas that receive snowfall are considered cold regions.** The most significant factor to affect individual performance in mountains is altitude — performance starts to degrade after personnel ascend above **5,000 feet (1,500 meters)**. For mountain-specific information, the manual refers to FM 3-97.6 and FM 3-97.61.

## Oceans, Land Masses, and Terrain

Large bodies of water (maritime zones) and inland areas (continental zones) have the greatest overall effect on climate. Maritime zones have milder temperatures and more precipitation; continental zones have greater temperature extremes and less precipitation.

**Terrain characteristics:** boreal forests dominate the subarctic; the arctic is treeless plains and coastal plains; the temperate zone varies. Terrain analysis in the cold must account for:
- **Observation and fields of fire:** snow and low vegetation change both — a dark object on snow is visible for miles; low scrub and snowdrifts provide concealment that is absent in summer.
- **Avenues of approach:** frozen rivers and lakes become routes; deep snow, swamps, and overflow ice block them. Snow fills in ditches and vehicle tracks; on rolling ground, snow tends to flatten the terrain.
- **Cover and concealment:** snow cover changes what conceals and what exposes. Tracks in snow are a signature — a unit's movement can be read from its tracks long after it has passed. On the downwind side of every obstacle, tree, house, and bush, a hollow always exists — an excellent observation point or firing position. Wind builds long, wavy snowdrifts that create almost natural snow trenches; when deep enough, soldiers can use them as an approach to the objective.
- Frozen streams or sunken riverbeds are another means of advance — often a longer but safer route — though overflow ice must be considered, and swampy areas that do not freeze solidly in the coldest periods (hidden under snow) must be avoided and bypassed without attempting to cross.

## Cold Temperature Categories

The Army and Marine Corps use the same categories; the Army adds one more because it is designed to operate in colder temperatures:

| Category | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Wet cold | +39°F to +20°F (4°C to −6°C) |
| Dry cold | +19°F to −4°F (−7°C to −20°C) |
| Intense cold | −5°F to −25°F (−20°C to −32°C) |
| Extreme cold | −25°F to −40°F (−32°C to −40°C) |
| Hazardous cold (Army only) | −40°F (−40°C) and below |

**Wet cold** occurs when wet snow and rain often accompany the cold. It is often more dangerous than colder, dry cold environments: the ground becomes slushy and muddy, clothing and equipment become perpetually wet and damp, and water conducts heat **25 times faster than air** — core body temperatures can quickly drop if troops become wet and then catch a blowing wind. Wet cold can quickly lead to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot. The ground alternates between freezing and thawing (temperatures fluctuate above and below freezing), which makes planning problematic — areas trafficable when frozen can become severely restricted when the ground thaws.

**Dry cold** is easier to live in than wet cold. Low humidity and permanently frozen ground mean people and equipment are not subject to the freeze–thaw cycle, and precipitation is generally dry snow. Wind chill is the complicating factor. Of the four cold-weather categories, dry cold best enables troops to survive.

**Intense cold** (−5°F to −25°F) affects the mind as much as the body: rapid numbing effect, simple tasks take longer and require more effort, quality of work degrades as attention to detail diminishes, clothing becomes bulkier and dexterity is lost. Commanders must consider these factors when planning operations and assigning tasks.

**Extreme cold** (below −25°F) makes survival paramount: individuals can easily prioritize physical comfort above all else, withdraw into themselves, and adopt a cocoon-like existence. Leaders must expect and plan for weapons, vehicles, and munitions failures.

**Hazardous cold** (below −40°F): commanders and planners assume a great deal of risk if they choose to engage in operations at these temperatures. Units must have extensive training before undertaking an operation in these extremes.

## Snow Categories

For military purposes, snow is categorized as light, moderate, or heavy — each classification affects visibility and ground movement due to accumulation:

| Category | Visibility in falling snow | Accumulation |
|---|---|---|
| Light snow | Equal to or greater than 5/8 mile (1,000 m) | Trace to 1 inch (2.5 cm) per hour |
| Moderate snow | 5/16 mile to 1/2 mile (500–900 m) | 1–3 inches (2.5–7.6 cm) per hour |
| Heavy snow | Less than 1/4 mile (400 m) | 3 or more inches per hour |

## Weather Phenomena

Weather information can be hard to come by in cold regions — observatories make generalized forecasts for large, unpopulated areas that may or may not be accurate for the area of operations. For specific information on weather observation and forecasting, refer to FM 3-97.61. Unique phenomena:

- **Ice fog:** occurs when three things exist — temperature of **−30°F (−34°C) or colder**, a vapor source, and still air. Moisture from combustion crystallizes into very small ice particles. Firing a weapon creates heat and a lasting ice-fog signature that pinpoints the weapon's position — alternate firing positions are required for both target acquisition and cover and concealment. Stationary running vehicles also produce ice fog noticeable for miles.
- **Blizzard:** sustained winds or frequent gusts to **35 mph (56 kph)** or greater; considerable falling, blowing, or falling and blowing snow; reduced visibility to a quarter-mile or less — for **three or more hours**. Ground blizzards involve winds moving snow already on the ground; they often occur in the arctic and subarctic and can last for days.
- **Whiteout:** sunlight diffused through an unbroken cloud layer onto an unbroken snow surface. The horizon effectively disappears; individuals experience a loss of depth perception and an inability to distinguish irregularities in terrain. Whiteout, often referred to as flat light, creates difficult and dangerous travel — units should restrict or cease movement until the condition clears.
- **Grayout:** over a snow-covered surface during twilight or when the sun is close to the horizon. An overall grayness to surroundings causes a loss of depth perception; the horizon is indistinguishable, making travel difficult and dangerous.
- **Temperature inversions:** normally as elevation increases, temperature decreases; in mountainous areas inversions trap cold air in valleys with warmer air above, affecting visibility, sound transmission, and the performance of weapons and sensors.
- **Looming:** refraction that makes distant objects appear closer, higher, or displaced — a navigation and observation hazard.
- **Chinook winds:** warm, dry winds descending the lee side of mountains, rapidly raising temperatures and melting snow — they can change ground conditions in hours.
- **Aurora borealis:** can affect communications and navigation equipment.
- **Light data:** in the arctic and subarctic, daylight varies from continuous to absent by season — plan movement, reconnaissance, rest, and security around the light available.
