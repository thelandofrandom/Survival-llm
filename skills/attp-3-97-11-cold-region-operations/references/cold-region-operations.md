# Cold Region Operations — ATTP 3-97.11

## Full Spectrum Operations

ATTP 3-97.11 addresses the operations process in cold regions — the same process as elsewhere, but every step takes longer and costs more, and the staff must account for the cold's effect on every warfighting function.

## Offensive Operations

Offensive operations are combat operations conducted to defeat and destroy enemy forces and seize terrain, resources, and population centers. The four types are movement to contact, attack, exploitation, and pursuit. Offense is the decisive element in full spectrum operations and the preferred posture of the Army. The most important element of a successful operation is the ability to maneuver to defeat or destroy the enemy — and that is the hardest part in cold regions, where terrain and climate are not conducive to maneuvering.

**Conducting the offense in bad weather:** if combat operations are decided upon during snowstorms or high winds, leaders keep **compact formations, simple plans, detailed instructions, and limited objectives**, and use positive means of identification. Units can conduct close reconnaissance and attack under the cover such conditions afford. Successful operations require soldiers and Marines experienced in cold conditions. If they have little or no cold-region experience, leaders should not engage in offensive operations in inclement weather if possible; if a mission must be undertaken, commanders use compact formations to maximize command and control. After seizing an objective, assaulting troops are often fatigued and overheated from the exertion — leaders make provisions to prevent cold and heat casualties, and integrate exploitation forces into the plan. Once dislodged from defensive positions, enemy forces are at the mercy of the extreme cold and difficult terrain — an opportunity for follow-on forces to exploit, forcing surrender or destruction.

**Characteristics of the offense in the cold** (surprise, concentration, tempo, audacity — FM 1-02/MCRP 5-12A):
- **Surprise:** local surprise is harder when snow is present — the enemy can easily see moving objects on the snow and hear movement (better sound transmission in cold air). Surprise may come only from limited visibility, or from suddenly increasing the tempo of an initially slow attack when over-snow transportation is available. Execute assaults at night or during periods of low visibility where possible — though in the arctic and subarctic, darkness depends on the season. Considering the importance of surprise, it may be preferable to deliver the assault **without field artillery preparation fires**.
- **Concentration:** overwhelming combat power at critical points is extremely difficult with mobility restrictions and periods of limited visibility — all units must be equipped, trained, and experienced in moving under such conditions.
- **Tempo:** the relative speed and rhythm of operations over time with respect to the enemy. Units build tempo by careful planning for the avenue of attack, the battle in depth, a quick transition to other phases, and the concentration of forces. In cold regions, key planning factors are time, speed, and mobility: planners need additional time for troop-leading procedures and preparation; speed and mobility for tactical movement by forward troops and the reserve; additional lead time for reconnaissance; and sustainment augmentation (prepositioning forward, aircraft resupply) to maintain tempo.
- **Audacity:** weather conditions can be exploited — blizzards, falling snow, fog, low cloud cover, and natural night illumination. But severe weather restricts aviation support, degrades sensors, and shortens reconnaissance patrols. Commanders weigh the advantage against losing command and control, and use detailed plans to mitigate the risk. If attacking during a blizzard or blowing snow, **attack downwind or at a slight angle**, forcing the enemy to face the full force of the storm.

**Types of offensive operations:** the same four basic types as in temperate regions, with emphasis on easy transition between them to capitalize on gains. **Movement to contact:** as mobility decreases, use a smaller force to establish contact; if mobility varies with snow depth or mud, adjust the deployment location; search-and-attack by cold-region-skilled elements can deny large areas and collect information. Before a meeting engagement, consider options for subsequent actions: if a hasty or deliberate attack is anticipated, proceed on multiple avenues or at reduced intervals; if a hasty defense is anticipated, push supplies immediately (construction materials, heaters, additional ammunition) to the forward troops. **Attack:** as temperature drops, terrain-oriented objectives become more likely than force-oriented ones, since mobility worsens and the enemy seeks sheltered defensive positions. **Exploitation and pursuit:** a dislodged enemy in extreme cold is vulnerable — follow-on forces press the advantage before the enemy can reorganize in shelter.

**Forms of maneuver:** the same forms as in temperate operations (envelopment, turning movement, infiltration, penetration, frontal attack), applied with the cold's constraints on mobility and visibility — the manual discusses each form's cold-region application and the need for detailed planning and rehearsal.

**Tactical considerations:** every tactical decision in the cold must account for the reduced mobility, the limited visibility, the effect of cold on weapons and equipment, and the physical condition of the troops. **Patrol base operations** in the cold require heated shelters or snow shelters, security that functions in limited visibility, and a sustainment plan that accounts for the fuel and food consumption of a cold, static force.

## Defensive Operations

Defensive operations in the cold use the same types as elsewhere (area defense, mobile defense, retrograde as a form of defense), with cold-specific adaptations: obstacles constructed from snow and ice (snow walls, ice obstacles, frozen water as a barrier) supplement wire and mines; positions are dug into frozen ground where possible and built up with snow where digging is impossible; shelters and warming areas are part of the defensive position, not an afterthought. **Constructing obstacles:** snow and ice obstacles are built with the same principles as other obstacles — they must be covered by fire, and they must be maintained, since warming temperatures and traffic degrade them.

## Retrograde Operations

Retrograde operations (withdrawal, delay, retirement) must account for the slow movement of a withdrawing force and the risk of abandoning equipment that cannot be moved. A withdrawing force in the cold is vulnerable to exposure and to an enemy exploiting the same weather that covers the withdrawal — plan the route, the warming areas, and the transport for the slowest element, not the fastest.

## Stability Operations and Civil Support Operations

Stability operations in cold regions must plan for the population's own cold-weather needs — shelter, heat, and food become mission factors, not background conditions. A force that cannot keep itself warm cannot keep a population warm. Civil support operations (disaster relief, humanitarian assistance) in cold regions face the same problem at larger scale: the cold is the disaster's force multiplier, and the response must bring heat, shelter, and medical care for cold injuries as its first priority.

## Applying Combat Power in Cold Regions

**Movement and maneuver:** applying combat power in the cold starts with movement — the force that cannot move cannot fight. Terrain and weather considerations dominate the plan: snow depth, ice, visibility, and temperature determine what is possible and what is not.

**Terrain and weather considerations:** the same analysis as in Chapter 1, applied to the mission — which ground is trafficable, which is not, and how the weather will change the answer during the operation.

**Conducting intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance:** ISR in the cold must account for sensor degradation — optics fogging, batteries failing, acoustic sensors behaving differently in cold air (sound carries farther, which aids detection but also aids the enemy) — and for the enemy's signatures in snow: tracks, ice fog, and heat signatures against a cold background. Reconnaissance patrols are shorter in severe weather, and the information they bring back is correspondingly thinner — plan for it.

**Command and control:** harder in the cold — radios fail, batteries die, and leaders must plan for degraded communications, including wire, runners, and visual signals. The chapter's through-line: every warfighting function is degraded by the cold, and the plan must compensate for that degradation in advance rather than discovering it in contact.
