# Visual Signals

## Purpose

TC 3-21.60, Visual Signals (U.S. Army, March 2017, 96 pages), distilled into a field-usable catalog. It standardizes the signals a unit uses when radios and digital systems are inadequate, unavailable, or forbidden: hand-and-arm signals for dismounted troops, vehicles, and aircraft; flag signals; ground-to-air panel signals; and pyrotechnics. The manual's own framing is the point — visual signals are any means of communication that require sight and can transmit prearranged messages rapidly over short distances, and efficient combat operations depend on clear, accurate, and secure communication among ground units, Army aviation, and supporting Air Force elements.

The most common types are hand-and-arm, flag, pyrotechnic, and ground-to-air signals — but Soldiers are not limited to them. Chemical light sticks, flashlights, and other items can be used provided their use is standardized within the unit and understood by everyone working in the area. The manual is a guide, not an exhaustive list: it covers the commonly used signals, and signals for specific equipment or operations live in the manuals for those operations.

## 1. The rules that govern every signal

**How signals are drawn and read.** Signals illustrated with a **single arrowhead** are not continuously repeated, but may be repeated at intervals until acknowledged or the desired action is executed. Signals with **double arrowheads** are repeated continuously until acknowledged or the action is taken. Signals are illustrated as normally seen by the viewer; some are shown in oblique, right-angle, or overhead views for clarity.

**Context decides meaning.** Visual signals are generally contextual. The hand-and-arm signal for "take cover" and the one for "slow down" are similar in movement, but the situation in which each is given is completely different. Hand-and-arm signals are not exclusive to dismounted or mounted formations — as long as the message is sent and received accurately, the only limit is the Soldier's initiative and creativity.

**Limitations — know them cold:**
- Range and reliability collapse in poor visibility and when terrain restricts observation.
- Signals may be misunderstood — acknowledge every signal.
- Signals are vulnerable to enemy interception and may be used for deception. Never transmit a signal the enemy can read and act on without considering that he is watching.

**Weapon control.** Unless otherwise depicted, the individual weapon has been removed from the figures for clarity. Soldiers maintain positive control of their weapon at all times. If the proper signal cannot be performed while maintaining positive control, use an alternate signal with the non-firing hand. If both hands are needed, move the weapon to the hang or sling-arms position. → `references/dismounted-signals.md`

## 2. Dismounted hand and arm signals (Chapter 1)

The dismounted set is the one every Soldier must know cold — it controls formations, movement, contact, and crew-served weapons when silence is survival.

**Formations.** Disperse: either arm extended horizontally from the shoulder, waved repeatedly to the front and to the side in a sweeping motion, palm toward ground. Assemble or rally: arm raised vertically overhead, palm to the front, waved in large horizontal circles (normally followed by pointing to the assembly or rally site). Join me / follow me / come forward: point toward the person or unit, then beckon with the arm held horizontally to the front, palm up, motioning toward the body. Increase speed / double time / rush: fist raised to the shoulder, thrust upward to the full extent of the arm and back to shoulder level, several times rapidly. Wedge: arms extended downward and to the sides at 45° below the horizontal, palms to the front — or, with the non-firing hand, index finger and pinky pointed to the ground, other fingers curled. Vee: arms raised and extended 45° above the horizontal — or index and pinky pointed up. Line: arms extended parallel to the ground. Echelon left: right arm raised 45° above the shoulder, left arm 45° below the horizontal pointing toward the ground. Echelon right: the mirror image. Staggered column: upper arms parallel to the ground, forearms perpendicular, arms raised fully extended above the head, repeated. Column: arm raised and extended overhead, moved right and left until the formation is executed — or, alternately, non-firing hand touching the rim of the headgear directly in front of the face, fingers extended and joined. Herringbone: arms parallel to the ground, forearms bent perpendicular, repeated.

**Patrolling signals.** Contact left: left arm parallel to the ground, forearm bent perpendicular, repeated. Contact right: the same with the right arm. Air attack: arms bent at 45°, forearms crossed, repeated. CBRN attack: arms and fists extended, arms bent to the shoulders, repeated. Fix bayonets: simulate removing the bayonet from the scabbard and fixing it on the rifle. Enemy in sight: hold the rifle in the ready position at shoulder level and point it at the enemy — or, with the non-firing hand, index finger at the enemy and thumb pointing down, other fingers curled. Quick time: arm extended horizontally sideward, palm to the front, waved slightly downward several times, arm straight, never above the horizontal. Take cover: arm at 45° above the horizontal, palm down, then lowered to the side. Map check: point at the palm of one hand with the index finger of the other. Halt: hand raised to head level, fingers extended and joined. Take a knee: from the halt, lower the hand to waist level, palm down. Move to the prone: from halt or take a knee, lower the arm past waist level, palm down, then move the forearm from waist level to below waist level repeatedly. Pace count: tap the heel of the boot repeatedly with an open hand. RTO forward: hand raised to the ear with thumb and little finger extended. Head count: tap the back of the helmet repeatedly with an open hand. Danger area: right hand, palm down, drawn across the neck in a throat-cutting motion, left to right. Freeze: fist raised to head level. Move the platoon leader to the front: non-firing hand, index finger to the rim of the headgear. Move the platoon sergeant to the front: non-firing hand grabs the collar (or simulates it). Move the squad leader forward: non-firing hand, finger on the upper arm — the number of fingers is the squad number (two fingers = second squad leader). Numbers: all number signals use the non-firing hand; numbers above nine are given as individual digits (27 = 2, then 7). Stop, look, listen, smell (SLLS): open palm of the non-firing hand raised to ear level. Message acknowledged: fist held out, thumb up.

**Crew-served weapons signals.** Commence firing / change rate of fire: arm extended in front of the body, palm down, moved through a wide horizontal arc several times — for machine guns, moving the arm faster means the next higher rate of fire; slower means slow the rate. Change direction or elevation: move the hand and arm in the new direction and indicate the amount by the number of fingers shown — each finger is 1 mil or 1 meter of change; extend the hand repeatedly to total the change (e.g., up eight meters = arm upward with five fingers, then again with three). Move over / shift fire: raise the hand on the side toward the new direction, move it across the body to the opposite shoulder, palm to the front, then swing the arm in a horizontal arc, extending it to point in the new direction. Cease firing: hand raised in front of the forehead, palm to the front, swung up and down several times in front of the face. Out of action: strike the fist of one hand several times in rapid succession against the palm of the other hand. → `references/dismounted-signals.md`

## 3. Vehicle hand and arm signals (Chapter 2)

**Mechanized movement techniques.** Wedge, vee, and line are the same as the dismounted versions. Coil: one arm raised above the head and rotated in a small circle. Echelon left/right: as dismounted. Staggered column: as dismounted. Column: as dismounted. Herringbone: as dismounted. Traveling: arm extended overhead and swung in a circle from the shoulder. Traveling overwatch: both arms extended and raised up and down. Bounding overwatch: one arm at a 45° angle, bent, tapping the helmet, repeated. Fire: drop the arm sharply from the vertical position to the side (usually from the "are you ready" position); to fire a single weapon, point at that weapon with the arm extended, then drop the arm sharply. Move to the left/right: extend the arm to that side and raise it up and down. Advance / move out / follow me: face the direction of movement, hold the arm extended to the rear, swing it overhead and forward in the direction of movement, holding it at the horizontal, palm down. Dismount: arms extended, two or three movements up and down, hands open toward the ground. Stop: hands clasped together, palms facing, at chin level. Button up: both hands, one on top of the other, palms down, on top of the helmet; unbutton is the same signal, then separate the hands to each side in a slicing motion, repeated. Open up: arms extended overhead, palms inward, then slowly lowered to the horizontal. Close up: both arms parallel to the ground, palms up, then moved upward and inward toward the head.

**Guiding drivers and crews.** These are the ground-guide signals used to direct vehicles, with flashlights at night (blue filters whenever possible to preserve the driver's night vision; chemical lights have less effect on night vision). Attention: arm extended sideways, slightly above horizontal, palm to the front, waved to and from the head several times. I am ready / are you ready: arm extended toward the person being signaled, then raised slightly above horizontal, palm outward. Mount: two or three upward movements with the open hand, palm up. Disregard previous command / remain in place: both arms raised, wrists crossed above the head, palms facing out. I do not understand: both arms sideward to the horizontal, elbows bent, both hands across the face, palms to the front. Start engine / prepare to move: daylight — simulate cranking by moving the fist in a circular motion at waist level; night — move a light to describe a horizontal figure 8 in a vertical plane in front of the body. Halt or stop: daylight — hand raised upward to the full extent of the arm, palm to the front, held until understood; night — move a light horizontally back and forth several times across the path of approaching traffic. Increase speed: daylight — fist to shoulder level, thrust upward to the full extent of the arm and back rapidly several times; night — move a light vertically several times in front of the body. Right or left turn: daylight — arm extended horizontally to the side, palm outward; night — rotate a light to describe a 12- to 18-inch circle in the direction of the turn. Slow down: arm extended horizontally sideward, palm down, waved slightly downward several times, arm straight, never above the horizontal. Move forward: hands and forearms moved backward and forward, palms toward the chest. Move in reverse: face the vehicle, raise hands to shoulder level, palms to the front, move hands forward and backward; night — hold a light at shoulder level and blink it several times toward the vehicle. Close distance between vehicles and stop: forearms extended to the front, palms inward and shoulder-width apart; bring the palms together as the vehicle approaches — the vehicle must stop when the palms come together. Stop engines: arm parallel to the ground, hand open, moved across the body in a throat-cutting action. Neutral steer (track vehicles): wrists crossed at the throat, index finger pointing in the direction of steer, other hand in a fist.

**A note on exposure:** signals illustrated from the hatch or the top of a tactical vehicle are for illustration or for use while the vehicle is stationary. Per AR 385-10, personnel do not expose more than head and shoulders (name-tag defilade) except when actively engaging targets with vehicle-mounted weapons.

**Traffic control.** Used by military police and personnel at traffic control points; at night, given with a flashlight or lighted wand. Left and right traffic stop: stand facing traffic, arms raised, palms open, in the same plane as the shoulders. Front traffic stop: stand facing traffic, arm raised, palm open. Rear traffic stop: stand with the back to traffic, arm raised, palm open, rotating the upper body so the palm faces traffic. Traffic from the right, GO: right side facing traffic, left arm extended, palm open; right arm parallel to the ground, bent, palm at shoulder level. Traffic from the left, GO: the mirror image.

**Convoy control.** Signals are given by the convoy commander out of the passenger-side window or relayed to personnel operating a mounted weapon system; the driver is the last resort for relaying signals, for safety. Open up / increase distance between vehicles: left arm extended horizontally to the side, palm to the front, then moved downward to 45° below horizontal, repeated several times. Close up / decrease distance: left arm sideward to the horizontal, palm up, raised to the vertical, repeated. Pass and keep going: left arm horizontally to the side, palm to the front, describing large circles to the front by rotating the arm clockwise from the elbow. Move in reverse: face the unit, raise the hand to shoulder level in front of the body, palm to the front, extend the arm forward to its full extent in a pushing motion, palm to the front.

**Flag signals.** Each combat vehicle carries a flag set: one red, one yellow, one green flag. Flags mark vehicle positions (a quartering party marks assembly-area positions), identify disabled vehicles, warn friendly elements of an advancing enemy (an OP marks the platoon to move to fighting positions), and control movement as an extension of hand-and-arm signals when distances between vehicles grow too great. **Alone, the colors mean: red — DANGER or ENEMY IN SIGHT; green — ALL CLEAR, READY, or UNDERSTOOD; yellow — DISREGARD or VEHICLE OUT OF ACTION.** Flag signals, when understood, are repeated and executed at once. In limited visibility, flashlights with colored filters or colored chemical lights may be substituted for flags. **Firing-range flags:** a red flag at the control point means firing may be conducted; a green flag means it may not. → `references/vehicle-and-convoy-signals.md`

## 4. Aircraft signals, panels, and emergency codes (Chapter 3)

**Ground-to-air hand and arm signals.** When radios and digital systems cannot be used, ground forces must be able to direct helicopters and fixed-wing aircraft. The signalman's position when directing a taxiing helicopter: slightly to the right, in full view of the pilot, at a safe distance of **no less than 40 meters** (no closer than **20 meters** during slingload operations), day or night. The signalman never stands in front of an armed helicopter or fixed-wing aircraft.

- Assume guidance: arms extended above the head in a vertical position, palms facing forward.
- Cut engine / stop rotor: either arm, shoulder height, palm down, drawn across the neck in a throat-cutting motion; to shut down a specific engine or rotor, point at it with the other hand.
- Emergency: hand and arm moved in a horizontal "infinity" symbol, palm facing the aircraft.
- Proceed right to next signalman: left arm held down, right arm extended across the body toward the next signalman. Proceed left: the mirror image.
- Depart: overhead circular motion with the right hand, ending in a throwing motion in the direction of lift-off.
- Go around / do not land: arms crossed repeatedly overhead.
- Land: crossed arms extended downward in front of the body.
- Stop: arms crossed above the head, palms forward.
- Spot turn: one hand moved upward and backward from a horizontal position to indicate the direction of tail movement; the other hand points toward the center of the spot turn; the signalman stays in full view of the pilot.
- Move right: left arm extended horizontally to the side in the direction of movement; right arm swung over the head in the same direction, repeated. Move left: the mirror image.
- Move ahead: arms slightly away from the sides, palms to the rear, repeatedly moved upward and backward from shoulder height — used for short distances.
- Move rearward: arms by the sides, palms to the front, swept forward and upward repeatedly, level with the shoulders.

**Sling-load signals.** Load has not been released: left arm and fist bent horizontally across the chest (knuckles down), open right hand pointing up to the center of the left fist. Hook up complete: fist moved up and down, making contact with the other fist, which is stationary on top of the helmet. Release: left arm extended horizontally with the fist toward the load while the right arm makes a horizontal slicing motion under the left arm, palm down. Move downward: arms extended horizontally to the sides, beckoning downward, palms down. Move upward: the same, beckoning upward, palms up. Hover: arms extended horizontally to the sides, palms down — when guiding a landing helicopter, do not give this until the helicopter is at normal hover height above the ground and just short of the desired landing point.

**Ground-to-air panel system.** Two types of panels: **marking and identifying panels** in fluorescent colors ("Panel Marker, Aerial, Liaison" through the supply system), used to mark positions and identify friendly units; and **black-and-white panel sets**, arranged on light or dark terrain backgrounds to transmit brief messages or identify a unit, using the combined panel system and the panel recognition code in the unit's communications-electronics operating instructions. Locally constructed panels should be at least **6 feet long and 2 feet wide**, with maximum color contrast between symbol and background. Lay out on a relatively flat, clear area about **40 by 130 feet** — large enough to display messages and special signs, and clear of obstacles for message drop and pickup (aircraft flying into the wind at reduced airspeed and low altitude). One panel is the **base panel**: place base panels first and keep them in place while signaling; distance between panels is one panel length throughout, when space allows; the **index panel** is the first removed and the last laid out when the display changes; remove all panels not used for a particular display from view.

**Special panel signals.** Wind-T: a T, two panels wide and two panels long, representing an aircraft flying into the wind — it shows wind direction. Message pickup (figure 8 / H with the wind-T centered below): the crossbar of the H is not placed until the message is ready to be picked up; pickup poles are placed one panel-length from the corner of the nearest panel. Message not found: a symbol displayed in the drop area. Enemy aircraft near: two panels at right angles to a third, on the axis of any base panel — always means enemy aircraft near, even if other parts of the display remain. Direction: an arrow made with not less than four panels means "in this direction," used alone or with the pattern preceding it.

**Acknowledgment.** A pilot indicates ground signals are understood by rocking the wings laterally, flashing a green signal lamp, or any prearranged signal; not understood by a 360-degree turn to the right, flashing a red signal lamp, or a prearranged signal. Each panel display is acknowledged. A pilot requests a unit's identification code by a prearranged signal — and **a unit never displays an identification code until the aircraft has been identified as friendly**.

**Emergency signals and codes.** Two methods, understood by allied nations: **body signals** — the individual stands in an open area, ensures the background (as seen from the air) is not confusing, goes through the motions slowly, and repeats each signal until understood; and **ground codes** — symbols constructed from any available material that contrasts with the background (strips of parachute canopy, undershirts torn into wide strips, rocks, sticks, stripped foliage), semipermanent once laid out. The code meanings are in the manual's figures and the unit SOI. **Mirrors:** the MK 3 signal mirror gets an aircraft pilot's attention during the day (needs good visibility and little or no cloud cover to reflect the sun); instructions for its use are printed on the back of the mirror. → `references/aircraft-panels-pyrotechnics.md`

## 5. Pyrotechnics (Chapter 4)

Pyrotechnics produce either smoke or light and are consumed in the process. They supplement or replace normal communications and can signal a large number of troops or isolated units quickly: friendly-unit identification, maneuver-element control, fire-support control, target marking, location reports. **When pyrotechnics are used, the signal and its meaning go in the command-and-signal portion of the operation order and in the unit's communications-electronics operating instructions.** Warning, verbatim in spirit: **do not discharge pyrotechnics in the vicinity of aircraft flying in the area.**

**Handheld signals** are rocket-propelled, fin-stabilized, and consist of three concentric tubes: the outer tube is the container, the next is the launcher, and inside the launcher is the fin-stabilized tube containing the rocket propellant and signal element. When fired, the fin-stabilized tube is lifted about **50 feet** in the air, the signal element is expelled, and it burns **4 to 42 seconds** (depending on type: cluster or parachute). Types: **star clusters** — five free-falling pyrotechnic stars, in green, red, and white, for signaling and illuminating; **star parachutes** — a single parachute-suspended illuminant star, in green, red, and white; **smoke parachutes** — a perforated canister, parachute-suspended, for signaling only, in green, yellow, and red smoke. The 40-mm rifle-mounted launcher can also fire pyrotechnic rounds (TM 3-22.31).

**Ground smoke** is used for ground and ground-to-air signaling; white and colored smoke are both used, and smoke is most visible against a terrain background of contrasting color. Smoke marks unit flanks, positions of lead elements, targets, drop zones, tactical landing areas, and MEDEVAC landing sites. Smoke signals are **not suitable for messages** — they communicate by prearranged signal only. Smoke can be observed by the enemy: consider secrecy before disclosing a position or an intention. **M8 white smoke hand grenade:** burning-type, for signaling and smoke screens; dense white smoke for **105–150 seconds**; will not normally injure exposed troops, but in heavy concentrations troops should wear the field protective mask — and the mask will not protect against heavy concentrations in enclosed spaces because of oxygen depletion and carbon monoxide buildup. **M18 colored smoke grenade:** same appearance, top painted the color of the smoke it produces; filler is a burning-type mixture containing dye; four standard colors — red, green, violet, yellow; igniting-type fuse; burns **50–90 seconds**. → `references/aircraft-panels-pyrotechnics.md`

## Output Contract

When asked for a signal, give the exact body movement (which arm, which angle, palm direction, repetition) and the figure/paragraph reference (e.g., TC 3-21.60 para 1-25). Distinguish the dismounted, vehicle, and aircraft versions of signals that share a name — "halt" for a patrol is not "halt" for a convoy, and "stop" for a vehicle is not "stop" for a helicopter. State the limitations every time the answer matters: visibility, terrain, enemy observation. For panels and pyrotechnics, always note that meanings are prearranged in the OPORD and SOI — a color means nothing until the unit has agreed what it means.

## Operating Rules

1. **Acknowledge every signal.** Single-arrowhead signals repeat at intervals until acknowledged; double-arrowhead signals repeat continuously until acknowledged or executed.
2. **Context decides meaning.** Similar movements mean different things in different situations — read the situation, not just the arm.
3. **Never lose positive control of your weapon to signal.** Use the non-firing hand, or sling the weapon first.
4. **The enemy can see you signal.** Visual signals are interceptable and can be used for deception — against you as well as by you.
5. **A color means nothing until the unit agrees what it means.** Flags, smoke, and pyrotechnics are prearranged in the OPORD and SOI.
6. **Never display an identification code until the aircraft is identified as friendly.**
