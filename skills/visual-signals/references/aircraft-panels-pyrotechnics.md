# Aircraft Signals, Panels, and Pyrotechnics — TC 3-21.60, Chapters 3–4

## Ground-to-air hand and arm signals (paras 3-1 to 3-24, Figures 3-1 to 3-22)

Ground personnel and aviation assets must communicate even when radios and digital systems cannot be used. Pathfinders are often unavailable, so the responsibility to guide aircraft falls on ground forces.

**Signalman position (para 3-3):** when directing a taxiing helicopter, stand slightly to the right, in full view of the pilot, at a safe distance of **no less than 40 meters** — no closer than **20 meters** during slingload operations. Day and night. **Never stand in front of an armed helicopter or fixed-wing aircraft.**

| Signal | Movement (para) |
|---|---|
| Assume guidance (3-4) | Extend arms above the head in a vertical position, palms facing forward |
| Cut engine / stop rotor (3-5) | Either arm, shoulder height, palm down; draw the extended hand across the neck in a throat-cutting motion. To shut down a specific engine/rotor, point at it with the other hand |
| Emergency (3-6) | Hand and arm moved in a horizontal "infinity" symbol, palm facing the aircraft |
| Proceed right to next signalman (3-7) | Left arm held down; right arm extended across the body to indicate the direction to the next signalman |
| Proceed left to next signalman (3-8) | Right arm held down; left arm extended across the body to indicate the direction |
| Depart (3-9) | Overhead circular motion with the right hand, ending in a throwing motion in the direction of lift-off |
| Go around / do not land (3-10) | Arms crossed repeatedly overhead |
| Land (3-11) | Crossed arms extended downward in front of the body |
| Stop (3-12) | Arms crossed above the head, palms forward |
| Spot turn (3-13) | Move one hand upward and backward from a horizontal position to indicate the direction of tail movement; point the other hand toward the center of the spot turn; remain in full view of the pilot |
| Move right (3-14) | Left arm extended horizontally to the side in the direction of movement; right arm swung over the head in the same direction. Repeat |
| Move left (3-15) | Right arm extended horizontally to the side; left arm swung over the head in the same direction. Repeat |
| Move ahead (3-16) | Arms slightly away from the sides, palms to the rear, repeatedly moved upward and backward from shoulder height. For short distances |
| Move rearward (3-17) | Arms by the sides, palms to the front; swept forward and upward repeatedly, level with the shoulders |

## Sling-load signals (paras 3-18 to 3-24, Figures 3-17 to 3-22)

| Signal | Movement (para) |
|---|---|
| Load has not been released (3-19) | Bend the left arm and fist horizontally across the chest (knuckles down); point the open right hand up to the center of the left fist |
| Hook up complete (3-20) | Move the fist up and down, making contact with the other fist, which is stationary on top of the helmet |
| Release (3-21) | Extend the left arm horizontally with the fist toward the load while the right arm makes a horizontal slicing motion under the left arm, palm down |
| Move downward (3-22) | Arms extended horizontally to the sides, beckoning downward, palms down |
| Move upward (3-23) | Arms extended horizontally to the sides, beckoning upward, palms up |
| Hover (3-24) | Arms extended horizontally to the sides, palms down. When guiding a landing helicopter, do not give this until the helicopter is at normal hover height above the ground and just short of the desired landing point |

## Ground-to-air panel system (paras 3-25 to 3-36, Figures 3-23 to 3-29)

**Two types of panels (para 3-25):**
- **Marking and identifying panels** — fluorescent colors, used to mark positions and identify friendly units. Ordered through the supply system as "Panel Marker, Aerial, Liaison."
- **Black-and-white panel sets** — arranged on light or dark terrain backgrounds to transmit brief messages or identify a unit, using the combined panel system and the panel recognition code in the unit's communications-electronics operating instructions (SOI).

**Construction and layout (paras 3-26 to 3-28):**
- Locally constructed panels: at least **6 feet long and 2 feet wide**, with as much color contrast as possible between symbol and background.
- Lay out on a relatively flat, clear area about **40 by 130 feet** — large enough for messages and special signs, and clear of obstacles for message drop and pickup (aircraft flying into the wind at reduced airspeed and low altitude).
- One panel is the **base panel**: place base panels first and keep them in place while signaling. Distance between panels is one panel length throughout, when space allows. The **index panel** is the first removed and the last laid out when the display changes. Remove all panels not used for a particular display from view.

**Special panel signals:**
- **Wind-T (para 3-32):** a T, two panels wide and two panels long, representing an aircraft flying into the wind — it indicates wind direction.
- **Message pickup (para 3-33):** the figure 8 (H) with the wind-T centered below it. The crossbar of the H is not placed until the message is ready to be picked up. Pickup poles are placed one panel-length away from the corner of the nearest panel.
- **Message not found (para 3-34):** a symbol displayed in the drop area.
- **Enemy aircraft near (para 3-35):** two panels placed at right angles to a third, on the axis of any base panel — always means enemy aircraft near, even if other parts of the display remain in place.
- **Direction (para 3-36):** an arrow made with not less than four panels means "in this direction," used alone or with the pattern preceding it.

**Acknowledgment and identification (paras 3-29 to 3-30):** the unit's SOI assigns vocabulary, receipting, acknowledging, and identification procedures; code meanings are based on the SOI with local amplification, and the numbers are changed periodically to prevent compromise. A pilot indicates ground signals are **understood** by rocking the wings laterally, flashing a green signal lamp, or a prearranged signal; **not understood** by a 360-degree turn to the right, flashing a red signal lamp, or a prearranged signal. Each panel display is acknowledged. A pilot requests a unit's identification code by a prearranged signal — **a unit never displays an identification code until the aircraft has been identified as friendly**.

## Emergency signals and codes (paras 3-37 to 3-40, Figures 3-30 to 3-32)

Aviators have developed two methods of transmitting emergency messages once a pilot's attention has been obtained; they are typically understood by all allied nations.

- **Body signals (para 3-38):** the individual stands in an open area, ensures the background (as seen from the air) is not confusing, goes through the motions slowly, and repeats each signal until understood (Figure 3-30).
- **Ground codes (para 3-39):** symbols constructed from any available material that contrasts with the background — strips of parachute canopy, undershirts torn into wide strips, rocks, sticks, foliage stripped from trees. Once laid out, these signals are semipermanent (Figure 3-31). The code meanings are in the manual's figures and the unit SOI.
- **Mirrors (para 3-40):** used to get an aircraft pilot's attention during the day; they require good visibility and little or no cloud cover to reflect the sun. Mirrors can also transmit messages if signals have been arranged. The **MK 3 signal mirror** is designed for this purpose; instructions for its use are printed on the back of the mirror.

## Pyrotechnics (Chapter 4, paras 4-1 to 4-6)

**General (para 4-1):** pyrotechnics produce either smoke or light and are consumed in the process. Prearranged or prescribed signals are developed based on the color and characteristics of the device. They supplement or replace normal communications and can signal a large number of troops or isolated units quickly: friendly-unit identification, maneuver-element control, fire-support control, target marking, location reports. **The signal and its meaning are included in the command-and-signal portion of the operation order and in the unit's communications-electronics operating instructions.**

> **WARNING: Do not discharge pyrotechnics in the vicinity of aircraft flying in the area.**

**Description (para 4-2):** pyrotechnics are usually issued as complete rounds. Two common types for signaling: handheld devices and ground smoke. The 40-mm rifle-mounted launcher can fire pyrotechnic rounds (TM 3-22.31).

**Handheld signals (paras 4-3 to 4-4):** rocket-propelled, fin-stabilized, three concentric tubes — the outer tube is the container, the next is the launcher, and inside the launcher is the fin-stabilized tube containing the rocket propellant and signal element. When fired, the fin-stabilized tube is lifted about **50 feet** in the air, the signal element is expelled, and it burns **4 to 42 seconds** (depending on type: cluster or parachute).

| Type | Effect | Colors | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Star clusters | Cluster of five free-falling pyrotechnic stars | Green, red, white | Signaling and illuminating |
| Star parachutes | Single parachute-suspended illuminant star | Green, red, white | Signaling and illuminating |
| Smoke parachutes | Perforated canister, parachute-suspended | Green, yellow, red smoke | Signaling only |

All are issued in an expendable launcher consisting of a launching tube and a firing cap.

**Ground smoke (paras 4-5 to 4-6):** smoke is used for ground and ground-to-air signaling, white and colored. It is most visible against a terrain background of contrasting color. Uses: marking unit flanks, positions of lead elements, targets, drop zones, tactical landing areas, MEDEVAC landing sites. **Smoke signals are not suitable for messages** — they communicate by prearranged signal only. Smoke can be observed by the enemy: consider secrecy before disclosing a position or an intention.

| Grenade | Type | Burn time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| M8 white smoke | Burning-type; signaling and smoke screens | **105–150 seconds** of dense white smoke | Will not normally injure exposed troops; in heavy concentrations wear the field protective mask — but the mask will not protect against heavy concentrations in enclosed spaces (oxygen depletion, carbon monoxide buildup) |
| M18 colored smoke | Burning-type with dye filler; top painted the color of the smoke it produces | **50–90 seconds** | Four standard colors: red, green, violet, yellow. Igniting-type fuse |
