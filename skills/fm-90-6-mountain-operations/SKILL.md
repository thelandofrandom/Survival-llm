# FM 90-6 Mountain Operations

## Purpose

FM 90-6, *Mountain Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 30 June 1980, 152 pages) — the Army's Cold War mountain operations manual, written for commanders, staffs, and subordinate leaders conducting operations in mountainous terrain. It is the predecessor of FM 3-97.6 and the first Army manual to treat mountain operations as a complete combined-arms subject: the environment and its effects on personnel and equipment, preparation and training, the conduct of mountain operations, combat support, combat service support, and appendices on clothing and equipment, employment of mountaineers, Army aviation, NBC considerations, avalanches, and the principal mountains of the world. Its material is applicable to both nuclear and non-nuclear warfare.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 90-6 was superseded by **FM 3-97.6 (November 2000)**, which in turn has been superseded in spirit by **TC 3-97.61, *Military Mountaineering* (2025)** and **ATP 3-90.97** — those are the current references, and where they disagree with FM 90-6 on a procedure or a number, follow the current manual. FM 90-6 remains valuable for three reasons: its Chapter 1 is the clearest statement of how the mountain environment degrades people and equipment; its Appendix F is the most detailed avalanche treatment in any Army manual of the period, with the historical record of avalanche disasters; and its Appendix B preserves the Cold War cold-wet, cold-dry, and mountain uniform and equipment type loads. The durable core is the environment chapter, the acclimatization and cold-injury doctrine, and the avalanche appendix — the weapons, communications, and organizational details are dated, and some medical figures (for example its salt guidance) reflect 1980 practice.

## 1. The Environment and Its Effects on Personnel

**1.1. Terrain and climate.** Mountains present broken, steep ground, thin air, intense solar radiation, and violent, rapidly changing weather. Maximum cloudiness and precipitation generally occur near 1,800 meters. Temperature falls with height; wind increases; snow and ice persist at altitude in every season. The environment affects every tactical function — objectives, mobility, maneuver, observation and fields of fire, reconnaissance and security, cover and concealment, assembly areas and bivouac sites, and command and control.

**1.2. Conditioning and acclimatization.** If soldiers are subjected to heavy work following rapid transport from sea level to an elevation of 4,200 meters, it is likely that **60 percent of them will become ill and may become ineffective**. After months in a high-altitude environment, **70 percent of sea-level work capacity** may be attained by acclimatized troops. **For operations above 2,500 meters, acclimatization is required.** A training program of graduated physical exercises — marches and climbs requiring increased stamina and endurance — is mandatory. There are two different types of acclimatization in mountain environments, psychological as well as physiological: complete acclimatization occurs only when personnel realize their own limitations and those of their equipment.

**1.3. Behavioral effects and psychological adjustment.** High altitude often affects people mentally. In unacclimatized personnel: increased errors in performing simple mental arithmetic, decreased ability for sustained concentration, deterioration of memory, decreased vigilance, increased irritability — and self-evaluation is also impaired, so during the first few days at high altitude leaders will find it difficult to maintain coordination and discipline. Errors in judgement that would never occur at lower altitudes are common. Many of the psychological adjustments that must be made are due to preconceived notions about the supposedly harmful effects of high altitudes; they are made easier by a well-planned period of acclimatization and conditioning, coupled with educational programs on high-altitude effects. → `references/environment-and-personnel.md`

**1.4. Sun, snow, and wind.** Ultraviolet exposure at high elevations is greater than at sea level because of the clear atmosphere and reflection from glaciers and snowfields; the nose, cheeks, neck, and ears are most frequently sunburned, and sunburn can result in painful blistering. Prevention requires frequent and liberal application of Army-issued sunscreen lotion — but sweating and wiping tend to remove it. **Snow reflects about 75 percent of the sun's rays**, and snow blindness can result from excessive exposure; during the actual period of exposure there is no sensation other than brightness to warn the soldier, but when symptoms occur, moving and blinking the eyes is extremely painful and exposure to normal light may also cause pain. A severe case may be completely disabling for several days. Prevention: consistent use of proper sunglasses or goggles, large and sufficiently curved to block most reflected light from below and from the sides — aviator sunglasses are not adequate. Improvised slitted glasses can be made from cardboard, thin wood, tree bark, or any other available material.

**1.5. Frostbite and windchill.** Frostbite — the freezing of some part of the body by exposure to extreme temperatures — is a constant hazard when the wind is strong; high altitude increases the chance of frostbite by reducing blood flow to the extremities. Soldiers must wear proper clothing for protection against cold and wind, keep clothing and bodies dry, and avoid excessive perspiration (for heavy work, remove outer layers and replace them as soon as work stops). The face especially must be protected in high winds and when exposed to aircraft propeller or rotor blast. The **buddy system is one of the prime preventers of frostbite**: soldiers must watch each other for signs of frostbite and provide mutual aid if frostbite occurs. The windchill chart in Chapter 1 shows the coupling effect of wind and temperature on the human body: at high wind speeds flesh may freeze within 1 minute, and in the greatest-danger zone within 30 seconds. Commanders at all levels should take this effect into consideration when planning or conducting operations under such adverse conditions.

**1.6. Nutrition, dehydration, and salt.** Success in mountains is affected by proper nutrition: at least one hot meal per day should be served, and an increase in rations may be desirable — palatable foods such as soup, fruits, fruit juice, sweetened beverages, and candy, with dehydrated soups as an excellent ration supplement especially in cold/wet climates. Loss of weight during the first few days is characteristic because of loss of appetite, dehydration, and changes in metabolism. Below 4,500 meters, carbohydrate foods are preferred to noncarbohydrate foods. **Dehydration:** thirst is not an adequate warning — the sensation may not be felt until there is a body deficiency of 1 to 2 quarts of water, and it may be blunted at high elevation; very dark urine is often a warning. Soldiers may require encouragement or coercion to drink more than they think necessary (**1 quart every 3 hours**). A loss of 2 quarts of body fluid (2.5 percent of body weight) decreases efficiency by 25 percent, and a loss of 12 quarts (15 percent of body weight) is usually fatal. All water must be potable, no matter how pure or clear snow or water appears — purification must be accomplished. **Salt:** the issue ration has enough salt for a soldier drinking up to 4 quarts of water per day; unacclimatized soldiers need additional salt during their first few days of exposure, and all soldiers need additional salt when sweating heavily. Extra salt should only be taken in proportion to available extra water, and the quantity must be strictly controlled according to medical advice — salt in excess of body requirements may cause increased thirst, sickness, and can be dangerous. → `references/environment-and-personnel.md`

**1.7. Hygiene and sanitation.** Weekly bathing is desirable; in freezing conditions latrines cannot be dug and sanitation discipline must be improvised. Cold, wet, and dirty clothing invites frostbite and lice; foot care is a daily command responsibility.

**1.8. Effects on equipment.** Vehicles lose power and suffer thickened lubricants; batteries lose capacity in the cold; communications, fire control, and other precision equipment are degraded by cold, moisture, and altitude. Clothing and equipment must be selected for the cold-wet or cold-dry condition (Appendix B). Acclimatization to the cold, extensive training, and disciplined maintenance are the counters.

## 2. Preparation for Mountain Operations

**2.1. Factors to be considered.** Commanders must answer: by what date must the unit be ready, are predeployment training areas available, what training is necessary, what training aids are needed, and how will the environment affect the acclimatization period. Preparation is a deliberate, time-consuming process — it cannot be compressed without casualties.

**2.2. Training requirements.** Training requirements are divided into those for all personnel and those for leaders, staff, and specialists. All personnel need acclimatization, weapons training, NBC training, basic military mountaineering (basic movement skills), language training where applicable, and driver training. Specialists need advanced military mountaineering, and selected soldiers need assault-climber and installation-team training. The manual's training matrix (Chapter 2) makes mountaineering a graded skill: basic movement for everyone, advanced techniques for specialists, and expert qualification for the few who will lead on technical ground.

**2.3. Mountaineers.** Appendix C defines the employment of mountaineers: basic mountaineering training, advanced mountaineering training, and the capabilities of climbers — installation party members, fixed alpine path builders, guides, observers and scouts, patrols, and assault climbers. Assault climbers are the spearhead on near-vertical ground; installation parties build the fixed ropes and ladders the rest of the force uses; guides and observers exploit the mountaineer's ability to move and see where ordinary soldiers cannot.

## 3. Mountain Operations

**3.1. How the environment affects tactical operations.** Objectives in mountains are usually terrain features — passes, ridges, dominating heights — rather than towns or road junctions. Mobility is restricted and canalized; maneuver forces are small and dispersed; observation and fields of fire are short and interrupted by intervening ground; reconnaissance and security are harder and more important; cover and concealment are plentiful in rock and forest but scarce above the tree line; assembly areas and bivouac sites must be chosen for shelter, water, and protection from avalanche and rockfall; command and control is degraded by terrain masking of radios and the difficulty of moving messengers.

**3.2. The enemy in mountain operations.** The manual studies how an enemy attacks and defends in mountains: enemy attacks exploit covered approaches, night movement, and envelopment around dominating ground; enemy defenses hold key terrain with mutually supporting positions, use obstacles and demolitions in defiles, and rely on the terrain to canalize the attacker into killing zones. The summary lesson: in mountains the defender has the advantage of ground, and the attacker must win it by maneuver — envelopment, infiltration, and the seizure of dominating heights — not by frontal assault.

**3.3. How to attack.** Fundamentals of offensive operations in the mountains: seize the high ground first, move by bounds from one covered position to the next, use assault climbers to open routes the enemy considers impassable, and concentrate firepower on the few positions that dominate the approach. Forms of maneuver and types of offensive operations are the same as elsewhere, but their execution is slower and more deliberate. The manual's attack example works through a battalion attack on a ridge position, showing the sequencing of reconnaissance, installation of fixed ropes, assault climber action, and the main attack.

**3.4. How to defend.** Fundamentals of defensive operations: hold the dominating ground, organize the defense in depth along successive ridges, prepare demolitions and obstacles in the defiles, and keep a mobile reserve that can move along the ridge line faster than the attacker can climb. Organization of the defense, preparation (including fortification in rock and frozen ground), and a defense example complete the chapter. → `references/operations.md`

## 4. Combat Support

**4.1. Field artillery.** Fire support, fire planning, fire control, observation of fire, targets, and positioning are all complicated by altitude (changed ballistics), terrain masking, and the difficulty of moving guns and ammunition. Observation of fire is the hardest problem — observers must be on dominating ground, and even then the enemy is visible only in glimpses.

**4.2. Tactical air support.** Counterair, tactical air reconnaissance, air interdiction, close air support, and tactical airlift are all limited by mountain weather, the scarcity of landing zones, and the altitude penalty on aircraft performance. Air reconnaissance gains value because ground reconnaissance is so slow.

**4.3. Air defense artillery.** Terrain analysis, employment, target acquisition, and data transfer must cope with radar masking by ridges and the difficulty of siting weapons on high ground.

**4.4. Engineers.** Engineer augmentation and employment, mobility operations (route construction and maintenance), countermobility (obstacles and demolitions in defiles), survivability (fortification in rock and frozen ground), and general engineering. In mountains, engineers are a combat arm, not a support afterthought.

**4.5. Communications.** Communication means and techniques for radio operation in mountainous environments, antennas and grounds. Terrain masking, cold effects on batteries, and the need for relay stations and redundant means are the recurring themes.

## 5. Combat Service Support

**5.1. General.** Preparation, the support base, trains, security of main supply routes, supply, maintenance, transportation, and health services. Main supply routes are few and exposed; their security is a combat mission, not a routine one. Supply must be planned for the altitude and the season; maintenance is harder in cold and altitude; transportation mixes wheeled and tracked vehicles, helicopters, pack animals, and man-carry; health services emphasize evacuation over treatment forward, because many mountain illnesses are treated by descent.

## 6. Avalanches (Appendix F)

**6.1. The historical record.** The danger of avalanches must be carefully considered when military operations are conducted in snow-covered mountains. In World War I, at least **40,000 troops died as a result of avalanches** in the province of Tyrol in western Austria and northern Italy; during fighting on the Austro-Italian front in 1916, as many as **9,000 to 10,000 troops were killed in a single avalanche** of 2 days' duration. Avalanches are a military disaster, not a mountaineering curiosity.

**6.2. Causes.** There are only two basic causes of avalanches: large amounts of snow, and steep slopes. Acting together, they can produce an avalanche independent of other factors. Terrain modifies the risk: a broken, serrated, or boulder-strewn surface anchors the snowpack, while smooth, even slopes of bare earth, solid rock, or shale favor massive ground-level avalanches. Gullies collect and channelize descending snow and must be avoided; ridges parallel to the slide path are relatively secure. **Convex slopes** are dangerous because the snow layers are under tension and fracture at the sharpest point of the curve, reaching full speed instantly; **concave slopes** are safer because the snow is under compression and the anchorage at the toe is better. The **minimum angle favorable to avalanches is 25 degrees**; slopes from 25 to 35 degrees may avalanche, especially if disturbed by artillery fire or other factors; **the critical zone lies above 35 degrees**.

**6.3. Cornices, vegetation, exposure, climate.** Cornices build up on the lee side of crests and ridges at right angles to the wind; their falling blocks are large enough to be dangerous by themselves and may release avalanches on the slopes below. Vegetation of any kind except grass restrains avalanches — heavy forest cover indicates slides are rare — but scattered timber is no particular deterrent, and slopes where timber has been destroyed by fire offer good potential for snowslides. Sun-facing slopes favor avalanches produced by thawing; lee slopes are the most probable locations for overloads of wind-driven snow and slab formation. Storms depositing up to 1 inch (2.5 cm) of new snow per hour are not uncommon; temperature fluctuations greatly affect snow cohesion — a rise weakens bonds, a fall retards settlement and increases slab brittleness and tension.

**6.4. Triggers and types.** Every avalanche must have a trigger: overloading (new snow piling up until cohesion fails), temperature change, and the manual lists further triggers including thunder, shock waves from high-speed aircraft, explosions, earthquakes, vibrations from tanks, bulldozers, or heavy machinery, and blast waves from nuclear weapons. A loose snow slide usually occurs during or immediately after a storm; a slab avalanche may come as a delayed action. → `references/avalanches.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a mountain operations question from FM 90-6, give the environment effect first (altitude, cold, terrain), then the numbers (acclimatization threshold 2,500 m, 60 percent casualty risk on rapid ascent to 4,200 m, water 1 quart every 3 hours, 25-degree avalanche minimum, 35-degree critical zone), then the tactical implication. State the 1980 vintage of the advice and where current doctrine (TC 3-97.61, ATP 3-90.97, FM 3-97.6) differs. Cite the chapter or appendix the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **Acclimatize above 2,500 meters.** Rapid transport to 4,200 meters with heavy work makes 60 percent of soldiers ill — train up gradually.
2. **The buddy system prevents frostbite.** Soldiers watch each other; no one self-diagnoses cold injury reliably.
3. **Drink on schedule, not on thirst.** 1 quart every 3 hours; 2 quarts lost costs 25 percent efficiency; 12 quarts is usually fatal.
4. **Respect the slope angle.** 25 degrees can slide; above 35 degrees is the critical zone; convex slopes fracture without warning.
5. **History is the warning.** 40,000 avalanche dead in the Tyrol in World War I — avalanche terrain is a tactical decision, not bad luck.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** FM 3-97.6 (2000), TC 3-97.61 (2025), and ATP 3-90.97 supersede FM 90-6 where they conflict; FM 90-6 is the historical and avalanche reference, not the authority.
