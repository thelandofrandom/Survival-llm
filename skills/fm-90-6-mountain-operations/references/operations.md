# Operations — FM 90-6

## Preparation and Training

Commanders must answer: by what date must the unit be ready, are predeployment training areas available, what training is necessary, what training aids are needed, and how will the environment affect the acclimatization period. Preparation is deliberate and time-consuming — it cannot be compressed without casualties.

Training requirements are divided into those for all personnel and those for leaders, staff, and specialists. All personnel need acclimatization, weapons training, NBC training, basic military mountaineering (basic movement skills), language training where applicable, and driver training. Specialists need advanced military mountaineering, and selected soldiers need assault-climber and installation-team training. Mountaineering is a graded skill: basic movement for everyone, advanced techniques for specialists, expert qualification for the few who will lead on technical ground.

## Employment of Mountaineers (Appendix C)

- **Basic mountaineering** — movement skills for all personnel operating in mountains.
- **Advanced mountaineering** — technical climbing, rope work, and fixed installations for specialists.
- **Installation party members** — build fixed ropes, ladders, and fixed alpine paths.
- **Fixed alpine paths** — prepared routes with fixed ropes and aids that let ordinary troops cross ground they could not climb.
- **Guides** — lead units through difficult terrain.
- **Observers and scouts** — exploit the mountaineer's ability to move and see where ordinary soldiers cannot.
- **Patrols** — small, self-sufficient teams operating in compartmented terrain.
- **Assault climbers** — the spearhead on near-vertical ground, opening routes the enemy considers impassable.

## How the Environment Shapes Tactics

Objectives in mountains are usually terrain features — passes, ridges, dominating heights — rather than towns or road junctions. Mobility is restricted and canalized; maneuver forces are small and dispersed; observation and fields of fire are short and interrupted by intervening ground; reconnaissance and security are harder and more important; cover and concealment are plentiful in rock and forest but scarce above the tree line; assembly areas and bivouac sites must be chosen for shelter, water, and protection from avalanche and rockfall; command and control is degraded by terrain masking of radios and the difficulty of moving messengers.

## The Enemy in Mountain Operations

Enemy attacks exploit covered approaches, night movement, and envelopment around dominating ground. Enemy defenses hold key terrain with mutually supporting positions, use obstacles and demolitions in defiles, and rely on the terrain to canalize the attacker into killing zones. The summary lesson: in mountains the defender has the advantage of ground, and the attacker must win it by maneuver — envelopment, infiltration, and the seizure of dominating heights — not by frontal assault.

## How to Attack

Fundamentals: seize the high ground first, move by bounds from one covered position to the next, use assault climbers to open routes the enemy considers impassable, and concentrate firepower on the few positions that dominate the approach. Forms of maneuver and types of offensive operations are the same as elsewhere, but their execution is slower and more deliberate. The manual's attack example works through a battalion attack on a ridge position, showing the sequencing of reconnaissance, installation of fixed ropes, assault climber action, and the main attack.

## How to Defend

Fundamentals: hold the dominating ground, organize the defense in depth along successive ridges, prepare demolitions and obstacles in the defiles, and keep a mobile reserve that can move along the ridge line faster than the attacker can climb. Organization of the defense, preparation (including fortification in rock and frozen ground), and a defense example complete the treatment.

## Combat Support

**Field artillery** — fire support, fire planning, fire control, observation of fire, targets, and positioning are all complicated by altitude (changed ballistics), terrain masking, and the difficulty of moving guns and ammunition. Observation of fire is the hardest problem.

**Tactical air support** — counterair, tactical air reconnaissance, air interdiction, close air support, and tactical airlift are all limited by mountain weather, the scarcity of landing zones, and the altitude penalty on aircraft performance. Air reconnaissance gains value because ground reconnaissance is so slow.

**Air defense artillery** — terrain analysis, employment, target acquisition, and data transfer must cope with radar masking by ridges and the difficulty of siting weapons on high ground.

**Engineers** — mobility operations (route construction and maintenance), countermobility (obstacles and demolitions in defiles), survivability (fortification in rock and frozen ground), and general engineering. In mountains, engineers are a combat arm.

**Communications** — means and techniques for radio operation in mountainous environments, antennas and grounds. Terrain masking, cold effects on batteries, and the need for relay stations and redundant means are the recurring themes.

**Army aviation (Appendix D)** — operational considerations (attack helicopter, command and control, intelligence, mobility, logistics) and environmental considerations (terrain, weather). Lift efficiency falls with density altitude.

**NBC (Appendix E)** — nuclear, biological, and chemical considerations requiring increased emphasis in mountains: detection is harder, decontamination is harder in snow and cold, and protective equipment is harder to wear and use at altitude.

## Combat Service Support

Preparation, the support base, trains, security of main supply routes, supply, maintenance, transportation, and health services. Main supply routes are few and exposed; their security is a combat mission. Supply must be planned for the altitude and the season; maintenance is harder in cold and altitude; transportation mixes wheeled and tracked vehicles, helicopters, pack animals, and man-carry; health services emphasize evacuation over treatment forward, because many mountain illnesses are treated by descent.
