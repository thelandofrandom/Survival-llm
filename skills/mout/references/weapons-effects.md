# Weapons Effects, Fighting Positions, and Chechnya (MCRP 12-10B.1, Appendices B, D–J)

## Appendix B — employment and effects of weapons

| Weapon | Effect against structures |
|---|---|
| M16 rifle / M249 SAW | Effective against personnel; penetrates interior walls — know what is behind your target |
| Medium/heavy machine guns, sniper rifles (7.62 mm, .50 cal) | Penetrate most interior walls and many exterior walls; .50 cal defeats light masonry and cinder block |
| Grenade launchers, 40 mm (M203, MK19) | Effective against personnel in the open and in light structures; may not penetrate reinforced walls |
| Light/medium recoilless weapons (AT4, SMAW) | Breach walls, destroy positions inside buildings; **dangerous backblast — never fire from a small enclosed room without accounting for it** |
| Antitank guided missiles | Effective against structures and armor; need clear line of sight and a trained operator |
| Flame weapons | Devastating in enclosed spaces; restricted by policy and law |
| Hand grenades | The urban infantryman's most-used weapon: fragmentation clears rooms; concussion (where authorized) reduces fragmentation risk to the thrower |

Rules: know what your weapon does to the wall, and what is on the other side of the wall. Overpenetration kills friendlies and civilians; backblast kills the firer.

## Appendix D — fighting positions

- Select a room with good fields of fire and multiple exits.
- Reinforce walls and floors; create loopholes (firing ports) in walls.
- Protect against grenades (overhead cover, grenade sumps) and indirect fire.
- Plan a withdrawal route. A position that cannot be abandoned is a trap.

## Appendix E — subterranean operations

Basements, sewers, subways, tunnels: how to enter, clear, and defend them. Special dangers: flooding, gas, collapse, limited communications, and the fact that the enemy knows the system better than you do. Never enter a subterranean system without a map, a guide, or a plan for getting out.

## Appendix F — obstacles, mines, and demolitions

- Employ obstacles and mines in the defense of a built-up area; breach them in the offense.
- Demolitions create mouseholes — holes breached between rooms or through walls — so the attacker moves through walls rather than through defended doorways and hallways.
- Engineers assist in breaching or bypassing minefields and in assault breaching into buildings.

## Appendix G — NBC considerations

Nuclear, biological, and chemical weapons in a city: contamination in enclosed spaces, difficulty of decontamination, protection of the population.

## Appendix H — MOUT under limited visibility

Night operations: advantages of night vision and thermal optics; difficulty of navigation; increased risk of fratricide when units cannot see each other.

## Appendix I — urban building analysis

Analyze a building before attacking it: construction type, materials, layout, entry points, fields of fire, vulnerabilities. Building analysis is intelligence preparation of the battlefield for the urban fight.

## Appendix J — lessons from Russian operations in Chechnya, 1994–1996

The Russian army entered Grozny in December 1994 expecting to take the city in days. Instead, Chechen defenders — small, mobile, well-armed teams fighting from prepared positions in buildings — destroyed Russian armored columns in the streets, inflicted heavy casualties, and forced a prolonged, grinding battle.

Lessons:
1. Never send armor into a city without infantry.
2. Never underestimate a defender fighting on his own ground.
3. Never assume a quick victory in a city.
4. Never enter a city without detailed intelligence on its buildings, its streets, and its people.
5. Small, decentralized teams with light anti-armor weapons and intimate knowledge of the terrain can destroy a much larger, heavier force in urban terrain.
6. The battle for the population and the battle for the buildings are fought together; losing one loses the other.
