# Military Operations on Urbanized Terrain (MOUT)

## Purpose

MCRP 12-10B.1 *Military Operations on Urbanized Terrain (MOUT)* (April 2018, 371 pages) is the Marine Corps' manual for fighting in cities — where most of the world's people live, and where most future fights will happen. It covers the nature of modern urban warfare, offense and defense in built-up areas, combat support and logistics, noncombatants and the rules that govern operations among them, and a long set of appendices on the practical skills: how a squad is organized for urban combat, how weapons actually perform against walls, how to attack and clear buildings, how to build fighting positions, subterranean operations, obstacles and demolitions, and the lessons of the Russian military operations in Chechnya 1994–1996 (Appendix J) — a case study in how a modern army can be bled dry in a city it expected to take in days.

Who it's for: infantry leaders at every level from fire team to battalion who must plan or execute operations in a built-up area — and, for the survival-LLM reader, anyone who needs to understand how a city changes every rule of open-terrain fighting: ranges collapse to tens of meters, every window is a potential firing position, the vertical dimension (rooftops, basements, sewers) matters as much as the horizontal, and the civilian population is not a backdrop but a central factor in every decision. The core idea: urban terrain multiplies the defender's advantage, fragments command and control, and devours ammunition, casualties, and time — so urban operations must be planned deliberately, fought methodically building by building, and always with the population and the rules of engagement in mind.

## 1. Modern Urban Warfare

**Importance of urban areas.** Cities are where people, government, industry, communications, and transportation concentrate. Controlling a city means controlling the population, the infrastructure, and the political center of gravity. An enemy who holds a city cannot be ignored, and a city cannot be bypassed cheaply — it anchors lines of communication and dominates the surrounding terrain.

**The Marine Corps role in urban warfare.** The Marine Corps fights as an expeditionary force, often arriving by sea or air with limited heavy equipment, and urban operations are a core part of that mission: seizing ports, airfields, and cities; clearing built-up areas; and conducting stability operations among a population. The Corps' bias is toward the infantry and combined arms at small-unit level, which is exactly what urban combat demands.

**Distinguishing features of urbanized terrain.** The manual lists the features that make cities different from open terrain:
- *Three-dimensional battlefield.* Combat happens at street level, above (rooftops, upper floors), and below (basements, sewers, subways, tunnels). The defender uses all three; the attacker must clear all three.
- *Short ranges and restricted fields of fire.* Engagement ranges collapse to 50 meters or less in streets and to room distance inside buildings. Long-range weapons lose their advantage; short-range weapons (rifles, grenades, shotguns, submachine guns) dominate.
- *Cover and concealment everywhere — for both sides.* Every building is cover; every window, doorway, and rubble pile is concealment. The defender is hard to find and hard to hit; the attacker is exposed in the street.
- *Fragmented command and control.* Buildings block radio signals and line of sight. Units lose contact with each other and with higher headquarters. Small units fight semi-independently, and the initiative of junior leaders decides outcomes.
- *High ammunition expenditure and casualty rates.* Urban fighting consumes ammunition, grenades, and demolitions at rates far above open-terrain norms, and casualties are heavy on both sides.
- *Civilian presence.* Noncombatants are intermingled with combatants, in buildings that are also fighting positions. Every fire mission, every building entry, and every movement must account for civilians.
- *Infrastructure as a weapon and a target.* Water, power, sewage, communications, and transportation can be destroyed, denied, or used by either side. The attacker who destroys the city's infrastructure inherits the problem of restoring it.
- *Restricted maneuver.* Streets canalize movement into predictable routes — ideal for ambush. Vehicles are confined to streets and vulnerable from above and from the flanks.

**Modern battles for urbanized terrain.** The manual surveys the recent history: Stalingrad, Seoul, Hue, Beirut, Mogadishu, Grozny, Fallujah. The pattern is consistent: the attacker enters expecting a quick victory and is drawn into a slow, grinding, building-by-building fight; the defender, fighting on familiar ground with prepared positions, inflicts disproportionate casualties; and the battle is decided as much by logistics, morale, and the population as by firepower.

**Modern urban battle analysis and observations.** The key observations: urban battles are won by the side that controls the tempo — that moves deliberately, clears methodically, and maintains supply; technology helps (night vision, breaching tools, precision fires) but does not abolish the infantry fight; the defender's advantage is real but not absolute — it can be overcome by isolation, by firepower applied precisely, and by attacking from unexpected directions (through walls, from rooftops, from below); and the battle for the population's support is fought alongside the battle for the buildings.

**Implications and key insights.** Urban operations require: more infantry, more engineers, more demolitions, more grenades, and more medical support than open-terrain operations of the same scale; decentralized command with clear intent (because communications will fail); detailed intelligence on the urban environment (building construction, street layout, subterranean systems); and a plan for the population from the start — not as an afterthought. Necessity for preparation: urban skills atrophy fast. Units that do not train in built-up areas will relearn them at the cost of lives.

## 2. Offense

**Planning considerations.** The commander's estimate for an urban attack weighs METT-T (mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available, time available) with urban specifics: the type of built-up area (village, town, city), building construction (masonry, concrete, wood — each resists weapons differently), street patterns, the subterranean dimension, and the population. The attack is phased: isolate the objective, gain a foothold, seize the objective, consolidate and reorganize. (The rifle squad manual's version of this cycle — reconnoiter, isolate, gain a foothold, seize, consolidate and reorganize — is the small-unit expression of the same idea.)

**Phases of the attack.** Isolation (cut the objective off from reinforcement and resupply — seize key terrain, block routes, establish a cordon); assault (the deliberate attack on the objective, building by building); and exploitation/consolidation (secure the ground taken, reorganize, prepare for counterattack or the next objective). The phases overlap; isolation begins before the assault and continues throughout.

**Warfighting functions in the offense.** Command and control (decentralized, with clear intent and robust communications plans — runners and visual signals back up radios); fires (direct and indirect, with tight control to avoid fratricide and civilian casualties — see Chapter 4); maneuver (small-unit infiltration, movement down streets, building-to-building); logistics (ammunition, water, and casualty evacuation dominate — see Chapter 5); intelligence (building analysis, pattern analysis, human sources); and force protection (all-around security, because the threat comes from any direction, including above and below).

**Infantry battalion operations.** The battalion seizes key objectives (a bridge, a government building, a crossroads), conducts infiltration (moving through or around the enemy to attack from an unexpected direction), and provides route security (keeping supply routes open through the city — itself a major operation in urban terrain).

**Rifle company operations.** The company attacks within a built-up area by isolating a block or a building, then clearing it systematically; attacks an enemy outpost; seizes a traffic circle or major intersection (a key piece of terrain that controls movement in all directions); seizes key terrain (high ground, a dominating building); and conducts movement to contact through the city.

**Rifle platoon operations.** The platoon attacks a building (the fundamental urban tactical task — see Appendix C for the drill), moves down a street (using bounding overwatch, building-to-building movement, and crossing open areas by rush), and uses its reserve to exploit success or to respond to a counterattack. At platoon level, the fight is won by fire teams and squads clearing rooms — and by the platoon leader's ability to coordinate fires, movement, and casualty evacuation while out of sight of his own squads.

## 3. Defensive Operations

**Decision to defend.** A commander defends a built-up area to deny it to the enemy, to buy time, to inflict casualties, or to hold key terrain. But the manual is candid about the reasons *not* to defend a built-up area: it ties the force to fixed positions; it invites the attacker to isolate and reduce it; it exposes the defending force to being cut off; and it places the civilian population in the line of fire. The decision to defend a city must weigh the value of the ground against the cost of being trapped in it.

**Defensive planning.** The commander's estimate and METT-T analysis drive the defense: organize the defense in depth (forward positions, main battle area, reserve); prepare fighting positions in buildings (see Appendix D); integrate obstacles, mines, and demolitions (Appendix F); plan fires (direct and indirect, with pre-planned targets on streets, intersections, and approaches); and plan for the subterranean dimension (defend or deny basements, sewers, tunnels).

**Infantry battalion defense.** Defense in sector (holding a defined area of the city); delay in a built-up area (trading space for time, inflicting casualties while withdrawing through prepared positions); defense of a village (a smaller, more contained problem — perimeter, key buildings, and routes).

**Rifle company operations.** Defense of a village; delay in a built-up area; defense of a city block (the company as a strongpoint, with mutually supporting positions in buildings); defense of a traffic circle or key intersection (controlling movement through the position).

**Rifle platoon operations.** Defense of a strongpoint (a single building or group of buildings, prepared for all-around defense); defense against armor (anti-armor weapons, obstacles, and the use of the urban terrain to canalize tanks into killing zones); and the armored ambush (letting enemy armor enter a street, then engaging it from the flanks and above where its armor is thinnest and its weapons cannot elevate or depress enough to reply).

## 4. Combat Support

**Fire support.** Indirect fire (artillery, mortars) is degraded in cities: high buildings mask trajectories, and the risk to civilians and friendly forces is high. Mortars are more useful than artillery in the close fight (high angle, short range, responsive). Naval surface fire support and naval gunfire are available in coastal cities but face the same masking and collateral problems. Aviation is constrained by the need for precise target identification and by the threat from small arms and man-portable air defense from rooftops. Armored vehicles provide direct fire support and breaching capability but are vulnerable without infantry protection — a tank without infantry in a city is a target. Antiarmor weapons (shoulder-fired rockets, guided missiles) are the infantry's answer to enemy armor, and snipers are disproportionately effective in urban terrain: a single sniper can halt a street, dominate an intersection, and force the enemy to change his plan.

**Other combat support.** Engineers breach obstacles and buildings, clear mines, and construct fighting positions — in urban operations, engineers are a maneuver arm, not a support afterthought. Assault support helicopters move troops and supplies over the city, bypassing streets entirely. Reconnaissance forces (including unmanned systems) provide the intelligence the urban fight demands. Military police control the population, manage detainees, and secure routes. Communications are the perennial problem: buildings block radios, so relay stations, retransmission, and runners are planned from the start. Sensors (acoustic, seismic, thermal, unmanned aerial) help find an enemy who is hard to see. Air defense protects against enemy air and drone threats.

## 5. Logistics and Combat Service Support

Urban operations consume supplies at extraordinary rates: ammunition (especially grenades, demolitions, and small-arms ammunition), water (the city's supply may be cut or contaminated), food, medical supplies, and fuel. Resupply is the central logistics problem: streets are canalized and ambushed, so resupply moves in protected convoys or by air, and forward stockpiles are built inside secured buildings. Maintenance and repair are done forward — a vehicle that must be evacuated to a rear depot is a vehicle lost for the battle. Replacements arrive as individuals and small groups, and must be integrated into units that are fighting room to room. Critical classes of supply in urban operations: Class I (food and water), Class III (fuel), Class V (ammunition — especially grenades and demolitions), and Class VIII (medical). Health service support is forward and heavy: casualty collection points in buildings, evacuation routes through the city, and a medical system that expects high casualty rates. The manual's point is blunt: logistics wins or loses urban battles. A unit that runs out of grenades, water, or stretchers stops fighting, however good its tactics are.

## 6. Noncombatant Considerations

Noncombatants are not an obstacle to urban operations; they are a central factor. The manual covers their impact (they restrict fires, they require protection and support, they can be used by the enemy as shields or as sources of intelligence), command authority (who is responsible for civilian affairs and under what legal authority), source utilization (civilians as sources of information), health and welfare (food, water, medical care, shelter), law and order (preventing looting and violence, restoring civil authority), public affairs and media relations (the urban battle is fought on television as well as in the streets), civil affairs and psychological operations (communicating with the population, countering enemy propaganda), and refugee control (managing the movement of displaced civilians so they do not block military operations or become casualties). The commander's legal authority and responsibilities are defined by the law of war, the rules of engagement, and the mission — and the manual stresses that how a force treats the population determines whether the population helps it, tolerates it, or fights it. A city taken with the population's hostility is a city that must be garrisoned indefinitely.

## 7. The Urban Environment and Restrictions to Operations

**Military operations other than war (MOOTW).** Urban operations frequently occur outside declared war: peacekeeping, humanitarian assistance, counterterrorism, evacuation of noncombatants. The manual reviews types of MOOTW, recent experiences and lessons, and urban terrorism — the use of the city itself as a weapon, by an enemy who hides among the population and strikes from it.

**Restrictive conditions.** Rules of engagement (ROE) are tighter in urban operations than anywhere else, because civilians are present and the political consequences of error are immediate. Planning under restrictive ROE means: positive identification of targets before firing; disciplined fires; coordination with civilian authorities; and acceptance that the enemy will exploit the restrictions — using civilians as shields, firing from protected structures, and counting on your restraint. The manual does not offer a way around this; it offers the discipline to fight effectively within it.

## 8. Appendix A — Organization for Urban Combat and Fundamental Combat Skills

This appendix is the practical core of the manual for the small unit, and it is worth studying in full. Its sections:

**Organization.** The rifle squad and fire team are reorganized for urban combat: assault, support, and security elements, with breaching and clearing teams designated. The organization is ad hoc and task-specific — a squad that clears buildings needs different teams than one that defends a strongpoint.

**Weapons handling and firing techniques.** Weapons carries (how to carry a rifle in a building so it does not snag or point at a teammate); firing techniques (short bursts, aimed fire, firing from the shoulder, hip, and around corners; the danger of overpenetration through interior walls).

**Movement.** Crossing a wall; observation around corners (using a mirror, a camera, or a quick "slice of the pie" look — never stepping into the open); movement past windows (stay below the sill, move fast, do not silhouette); use of doorways (never stand in a doorway — it is a fatal funnel); movement parallel to buildings (hugging the wall, using the building as cover); crossing open areas (by rush, in small groups, with overwatch and smoke); assault element employment; and movement inside a building (along walls, stairwells, hallways).

**Entry techniques.** Upper building levels (entering from the roof down is preferred — the defender expects attack from below); use of ladders; use of a grappling hook; scaling walls; rappelling; entry at lower levels (breaching the exterior wall is the best way in — a door is a known, defended point); hand grenades (used to clear a room before entry, with the thrower moving away from any wall that might be penetrated by fragments).

**Clearing techniques.** Clearing a room from stacked positions (door open or closed); clearing a room from split positions; covering team members used to support clearing; clearing a room through a mousehole; clearing L-shaped and T-shaped hallways; clearing a stairwell. The common principles: dominate the room on entry, cover the corners, never enter alone, never cross in front of a teammate's muzzle, and never stop in the doorway.

**Firing positions.** Hasty firing positions (quickly prepared, using available cover); prepared firing positions (built up with sandbags, rubble, and reinforcement); target acquisition in the urban environment; firefighting planning (the risk of fire in buildings and the need for a plan); employment of snipers.

**Advancing and patrolling along city streets.** Movement along streets (one side at a time, using buildings as cover); crossing a street intersection (the most dangerous point in urban movement — cross at the narrowest point, with overwatch and smoke, in small groups); reacting to enemy contact (return fire, take cover in a building, report, maneuver).

**Navigation in built-up areas.** Military maps and aerial photographs — street names, building numbers, and prominent features become the navigation system, because compass and GPS are degraded among buildings.

**Camouflage.** Application (face paint, uniform, equipment); use of shadows (stay in them, avoid skylining); color and texture (match the urban background — concrete, brick, rubble — not the forest).

## 9. Appendix B — Employment and Effects of Weapons

Weapons behave differently against buildings than against open terrain, and the manual gives the data:
- The M16 rifle and M249 squad automatic weapon are effective against personnel but penetrate interior walls — know what is behind your target.
- Medium and heavy machine guns and sniper rifles (7.62 mm and .50 caliber) penetrate most interior walls and many exterior walls; .50 caliber defeats light masonry and cinder block.
- Grenade launchers (40 mm M203 and MK19) are effective against personnel in the open and in light structures, but their rounds may not penetrate reinforced walls.
- Light and medium recoilless weapons (shoulder-fired rockets such as the AT4 and SMAW) can breach walls and destroy positions inside buildings, but they have a dangerous backblast — never fire a recoilless weapon from a small enclosed room without accounting for the backblast, which can kill the firer and his team.
- Antitank guided missiles are effective against structures and armor but require a clear line of sight and a trained operator.
- Flame weapons are devastating in enclosed spaces but are restricted by policy and law.
- Hand grenades are the urban infantryman's most-used weapon: fragmentation grenades clear rooms; concussion grenades (where authorized) reduce fragmentation risk to the thrower.
The rule that runs through the appendix: know what your weapon does to the wall, and what is on the other side of the wall.

## 10. Appendices C–K — The Working Details

- **Appendix C, Attacking and Clearing Buildings.** The deliberate procedure: isolate the building, gain a foothold (usually by breaching an exterior wall or entering from the roof), clear from the top down (so the defender is pushed downward into the assault element's fires, and so cleared floors stay clear), and consolidate. Clearing is systematic: room by room, floor by floor, with security posted on cleared areas and a plan for casualties and prisoners.
- **Appendix D, Fighting Positions.** How to build a fighting position in a building: select a room with good fields of fire and multiple exits; reinforce walls and floors; create loopholes (firing ports) in walls; protect against grenades and indirect fire; and plan a withdrawal route. A position that cannot be abandoned is a trap.
- **Appendix E, Subterranean Operations.** Basements, sewers, subways, and tunnels: how to enter, clear, and defend them; the special dangers (flooding, gas, collapse, limited communications, and the fact that the enemy knows the system better than you do). Never enter a subterranean system without a map, a guide, or a plan for getting out.
- **Appendix F, Obstacles, Mines, and Demolitions.** How to employ obstacles and mines in the defense of a built-up area, and how to breach them in the offense — including the use of demolitions to create mouseholes (holes breached between rooms or through walls, so the attacker moves through walls rather than through defended doorways and hallways).
- **Appendix G, Nuclear, Biological, and Chemical Considerations.** The special problems of NBC weapons in a city: contamination in enclosed spaces, the difficulty of decontamination, and the protection of the population.
- **Appendix H, MOUT Under Limited-Visibility Conditions.** Night operations in a city: the advantages of night vision and thermal optics, the difficulty of navigation, and the increased risk of fratricide when units cannot see each other.
- **Appendix I, Urban Building Analysis.** How to analyze a building before attacking it: construction type, materials, layout, entry points, fields of fire, and vulnerabilities. Building analysis is intelligence preparation of the battlefield for the urban fight.
- **Appendix J, Lessons Learned from Russian Military Operations in Chechnya 1994–1996.** The cautionary case study. The Russian army entered Grozny in December 1994 expecting to take the city in days. Instead, Chechen defenders — small, mobile, well-armed teams fighting from prepared positions in buildings — destroyed Russian armored columns in the streets, inflicted heavy casualties, and forced a prolonged, grinding battle. The lessons: never send armor into a city without infantry; never underestimate a defender fighting on his own ground; never assume a quick victory; and never enter a city without detailed intelligence on its buildings, its streets, and its people. The manual includes this appendix because it expects its readers to remember it.
- **Appendix K, Glossary; Appendix L, References.**

## Operating Rules

1. **The city is three-dimensional.** Clear up and down, not just across. The defender uses rooftops, upper floors, basements, and sewers.
2. **Streets are killing zones.** Move building to building; cross open areas and intersections by rush, with overwatch and smoke, in small groups.
3. **Never stop in a doorway.** Doorways are fatal funnels — pre-aimed, pre-ranged, and covered. Breach a wall instead when you can.
4. **Clear top-down.** Enter from the roof or an upper floor when possible; push the defender down; keep cleared floors clear.
5. **Know your weapon's effect on the wall — and what is behind it.** Overpenetration kills friendlies and civilians; backblast kills the firer.
6. **Armor without infantry in a city is a target.** Tanks need infantry protection; infantry needs armor's firepower. Neither works alone.
7. **Comms will fail.** Plan runners, visual signals, and clear intent — junior leaders will fight semi-independently.
8. **The population is the terrain.** How you treat civilians determines whether they help, tolerate, or fight you — and whether you must garrison the city forever.
9. **Logistics decides urban battles.** Grenades, demolitions, water, and medical support run out first. Stockpile forward.
10. **Remember Grozny.** Never assume a quick victory in a city; never send armor in unsupported; never underestimate a defender on his own ground.
