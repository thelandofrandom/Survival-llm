# Tactical Operations in Snow and Extreme Cold — FM 70-15

## Influencing Factors

Tactical operations in snow and extreme cold do not vary in principle from operations under any other conditions, but their application differs.

- Deep snow stops cavalry and wheeled vehicles and prevents movement of troops on foot as well as in armored vehicles other than those of special design.
- Snow acts as either an obstacle or an avenue of approach, depending on the equipment and training of the command.
- **Frozen ground makes passageways, instead of obstacles, of swamps, streams, and lakes.**
- Deep snowdrifts and icy slopes restrict movement. Emplacements and field works are difficult to construct, necessitating thawing or explosives.
- Tracks and installations are more apparent to aerial observers, and camouflage is both more difficult and of greater importance than usual.
- Unless wheeled vehicles are supplanted or supplemented by oversnow vehicles, supply routes are canalized to roads and trails. Some canalization occurs in any case, because trails must be used for oversnow equipment, snowshoers, and skiers if speed of movement is to be maintained.

## Training

For troops who have completed unit and combined training, considerable training in snow and cold is necessary before a unit is proficient; during training, cross-country movements without shelter in buildings during halts must be practiced. On the march, individuals should be equipped to be self-sustaining, carrying their own food and shelter.

## The Enemy Suffers Too

While friendly personnel are weakened in strength and morale by cold, fatigue, and hunger, this is equally true of the enemy: whenever possible, deny him towns or buildings, use patrols, aircraft, and harassing fire against him in bivouacs, and raid his supply system — deprive him of food, rest, and shelter, and expect corresponding efforts against your own troops.

## Organization, Leadership, and Operations

Chapter 11 covers, in order: organization and equipment for winter operations; the arms and services; leadership and command; reconnaissance and security; movements; offensive operations; defensive operations; retrograde movements; special operations; airborne operations; and supply and evacuation — all adapted to the same tactical principles as temperate operations, with the movement, shelter, and equipment constraints of this manual applied throughout.

- **Organization:** units are reorganized and re-equipped for winter conditions, with oversnow transport and cold-weather clothing and equipment added and unsuitable vehicles and weapons eliminated.
- **Leadership and command:** commanders must account for the slower rates of movement, the greater bulk of supply, and the rapid loss of effectiveness when men are cold, tired, or hungry; orders must allow the extra time winter operations demand.
- **Reconnaissance and security:** tracks in snow betray movement to aerial and ground observation, so camouflage and track discipline are both more difficult and more important than usual.
- **Movements:** rates of travel are greatly altered by winter conditions; movement is canalized to cleared roads and trails unless troops are equipped for oversnow movement.
- **Offensive and defensive operations:** the same principles as elsewhere, with the added weight of cold on both sides — the side that keeps its men warm, fed, and moving retains the initiative.
- **Retrograde, special, and airborne operations:** all demand the same cold-weather preparation, with particular attention to shelter and supply for troops cut off from their base.
- **Supply and evacuation:** bulk is greater (fuel, rations, forage), means of handling it narrower, and evacuation slower — planning must allow for both.
