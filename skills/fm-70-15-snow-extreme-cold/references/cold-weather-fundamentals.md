# Cold-Weather Fundamentals — FM 70-15

## The Four Major Problems

1. **Keeping men and animals warm.**
2. **Moving troops across snow and ice.**
3. **Transporting and preserving supplies and equipment.**
4. **Preventing the malfunction of weapons and equipment.**

**Keeping warm.** Men are subject to chilling, frostbite, freezing, and snow blindness. When cold, they are less alert; without proper shelter and covering they lose sleep and weaken; fingers may become numb from cold, resulting in poor handling of weapons; they seek sheltered locations and huddle together for warmth. Violent exertion followed by inactivity can leave men overheated and then freezing. When the air is still and dry, men are likely to consider the temperature higher than it actually is and become careless in their precautions against frostbite. Animals suffer from cold and frosted lungs, may require hot food, warmed water, prepared beds and shelter (sled dogs survive without these), and eat more; horses need nose shields and frequent rub-downs.

**Moving across snow and ice.** Deep snow and drifts impede progress both on and off roads; movement of even a few feet from cleared areas becomes impossible without snow-crossing equipment. Ordinary wheeled vehicles are useless, and armored track-laying vehicles experience difficulty, in snow **over 18 inches deep**. Troops unable to cross snow and ice are caught in a continuous defile on cleared roads or trails. Snowshoes, skis, crampons or ice creepers, and oversnow vehicles — snow tractors and motorized toboggans — are essential.

**Supplies.** Supplies and equipment assume relatively greater importance in cold than normally, as life is more immediately dependent on their adequacy and uninterrupted delivery. The bulk of supplies, particularly fuel, rations, and forage, becomes greater in cold weather, while the means of handling the bulk are more narrowly limited. Certain items of supply freeze in extreme cold; means of prevention must be devised.

**Malfunction.** Motors require special starting and maintenance techniques; lubricants stiffen; gasoline and chemicals vaporize less readily; condensation of water occurs in fuel tanks and carburetors; oil and fuel fail to flow through pipes; water jackets freeze; springs become brittle; steel breaks more easily; frost fogs windshields and optical lenses; rubber sometimes cracks; batteries fail; ice collects in weapon bores; improperly lubricated mechanisms do not function.

## Rations and Nutrition

- Troops lose military efficiency when uncomfortably cold. Regular sustaining hot food and drink are prime factors in maintaining body warmth. More food is required in extreme cold than in a temperate climate: more energy to maintain normal body warmth; more energy from increased activity; more energy because of the additional weight of arctic clothing and equipment; more energy to overcome the increased obstacles of snow and ice.
- **Protein** (meats, fish, eggs, milk) supplies heat and energy in about the same amount as carbohydrate, but is used primarily as builder and repairer of muscle tissue, needed in about the same amount as in temperate climates.
- **Carbohydrate** (sugars, cereals, fruits, vegetables) produces energy speedily and is required in increased quantities in extreme cold.
- **Fat** (meat, milk, butter, lard, vegetable oils) takes longer to digest but gives off **about twice as much heat** as carbohydrate or protein; it is extremely important in the arctic diet because when stored in the body it can be used as future heat and energy, it insulates, and it delays the sensation of hunger.
- **Deficiency diseases:** scurvy and beriberi may develop after a number of months if not guarded against. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is the specific for prevention and cure of scurvy, Vitamin B1 (thiamin) for beriberi; when these vitamins are lacking from the diet, synthetic substitutes should be provided.

## Water

- Obtaining sufficient water for cooking and drinking is an important consideration in cold regions. Running water is used whenever available; glacier water is usually clean enough if sediment is given time to settle.
- Frequently snow or ice must be melted: **ice is preferred because it is denser than snow and yields more water for a given volume**. When snow must be used, wet or frozen snow is better than powder snow.
- Melt it properly or the bottom of the container may burn: pack about **½ inch of snow** in the bottom of the vessel and melt it completely, then add additional snow in amounts small enough that the contents are always slush.
- Water from running streams, lakes, and ponds may ordinarily be considered safe, but if any doubt exists — or river water is used — make it safe by boiling or treating with halazone or iodine.
- **The canteen:** water is rarely carried in the canteen much below freezing. When carried, fill the canteen only **three-fourths full**, leaving room for the water to expand without breaking the canteen if it freezes solid; wrap the canteen in the sleeping bag or a garment for insulation.
- **Eating snow:** in the absence of drinking water, small quantities of snow may be eaten while troops are warm or on the move: place a small piece of ice or compressed snow in the mouth rather than sucking it (otherwise the lips may become chapped); if the soldier is cold, or hot and tired, he should not eat snow. **Snow should never be swallowed in lumps** — it may chill the stomach — and never eaten in large quantities, as it will materially reduce body temperature and may cause severe chafing of the lips. It is generally better to eat a little snow often than to wait until thirsty and eat too much.

## Cooking

- Cooking should always be done in as large a group as the situation permits, for convenience and fuel conservation; pooling stoves melts snow quickly at first, then each stove prepares part of the meal or stands ready as a substitute if one fails.
- Thaw frozen meat before cooking (partly frozen meat may cook outside while the center stays raw); cook vegetables only until tender, in a small amount of water, and drink the cooking water or use it in soup; heat canned foods only enough to warm them; dip frozen potatoes in hot water and scrape off the skins; clean mess gear thoroughly in boiling water — unclean gear may cause diarrhea.
- One-burner gasoline cooking stoves are issued for mobile troops; at extremely low temperatures, solid fuel or a primer-type gasoline stove is desirable, and at outposts where fires are not permissible, thermos containers are advantageous.

## Clothing — Insulation Is Trapped Air

- Suitable cold-weather clothing is not warm in itself; it is merely a good insulator and a poor conductor of heat. The heat of the body is held in by the clothing and prevented from escaping into the atmosphere.
- Since **still air is an excellent insulator**, the best cold-weather clothes are those that entrap a considerable amount of air: the warmth of a woolen sweater lies mainly in the thousands of tiny air cells between the woolen fibers; fur is warm because of the air trapped among the hairs.
- **Several thin layers of cloth are better than one thick, heavy, matted piece**, because air pockets form between the layers.
- **Perspiration is the enemy:** clothing that has become damp is a poor insulating agent, and physical exercise causes perspiration even in the coldest weather. To prevent excessive perspiration, which results in damp clothing and consequent chilling and frostbite, troops must remove or loosen outer garments at the start of exercise; the garments are kept dry in the packs and put on again during breaks and at the end of the exercise.
- Complete information on the use, care, and maintenance of cold-weather clothing is in TM 10-275.
