# Shelter, Heat, and Camps — FM 70-15

## What Shelter Must Do

Cold-weather shelter should provide an adequate windbreak, insulation against loss of heat from the interior, a source of heat where possible, and drying racks for clothing and equipment. Shelters should be sited to protect personnel and equipment from drifting snow and prevailing winds. They may be improvised from snow, logs, boughs, sod, or any material at hand in sufficient quantity, or transported — usually tents, but also prefabricated houses or huts, which should be light, compact, durable, and easily erected and disassembled by unskilled workers. Where buildings are available, use them to the greatest extent possible; isolated heated buildings are valuable for drying wet or sweaty clothing and footgear.

## Tents

- Medium-sized tents are better than small ones, since the body heat of a number of men together raises the inside temperature.
- A six-man tent may be improvised from double-ended shelter halves, with space near the peak for hanging clothing to dry. Floor cloths keep bedding off the ground.
- The two-man mountain tent, with a fixed floor cloth, is very satisfactory and quickly erected.
- The pyramidal tent normally issued is not entirely satisfactory in cold weather because of its weight.
- Prefabricated shelters of composition board, plywood, or galvanized steel usually accommodate six to eight men and may be heated by wood, coal, or oil stoves.

## Shelter in Timber

A bivouac in timber gives protective concealment (unless the timber stands alone so obviously as to indicate the bivouac's location). Timber with a high percentage of evergreen trees is most desirable — the trees provide maximum shelter and the best source of building materials, fuel, and bedding. Cut only lower branches, or small branches stripped from boughs too large for bedding; where boughs cannot safely be removed, straw, hay, newspapers, or pasteboard from ration and ammunition containers can serve as bedding.

### Snow shelter trench

Excavated below the surface of the snow but not to the ground, larger than the sleeping bag; a bough bed several inches thick after compression, thick enough that the sleeping bag or equipment never touches snow; boughs covered by a shelter half, extra clothing laid evenly on top, sleeping bag on the clothing; an access pit prevents loose snow from being knocked into the bag on entering or leaving. It may be elaborated with a bough roof or an improvised stove at one end.

### Lean-to

The standard timber shelter, of considerable value when open fires are permitted; built in various sizes for two or more men, with the small lean-to warmer than a large one. Snow is tramped down or cleared; two large uprights with a transverse piece, back pieces at intervals, cross members, and boughs hooked on by their branches.

## Shelter Without Timber

In open country, snow itself is the building material — snow houses (igloos) are covered in section III of the chapter and in Appendix I. Dry powdery snow is the best insulator; compact wet or wind-hardened snow is best for water supply.

## Camp Siting

- Water supply is particularly important because of the extreme dehydration of men after several days' operations in snow and because dehydrated foods need unusual amounts of water for cooking; since ice yields more water than snow, camp near a source of ice if possible — streams are sometimes open even at 60° below zero.
- Place camps on the **downwind side of hills, about halfway or two-thirds up the slope**, since cold lies at the bottom of valleys.
- Place camps in timber when possible for windbreak and insulation.
- In hilly or mountainous terrain, examine snow on slopes above for avalanche danger.
- Pitch camp in the deepest snow available to use snow's insulating properties.
- Plan camps in advance: designate and mark unit areas, garbage and latrine sites, and command-post locations, and tape paths before construction begins.
- **Halt troops before darkness and before they are exhausted** — tired troops making camp in darkness and extreme cold neglect measures necessary for their safety, leading to a gradual reduction in effective personnel strength.
- Firewood should be at or near the camp site.

## Making Camp

On arrival, cooks immediately begin preparing hot food. The remaining men are divided into groups and put to work making the camp habitable: gathering boughs for beds (which must be quite thick to be effective), erecting shelter frameworks, supplying timber, wattling and thatching shelters and banking them with snow. No one does detailed work on individual shelters until all shelters are completed; the hot meal is served, boughs for bedding drawn, and adequacy of all shelters checked before dismissal. A standing operating procedure greatly facilitates speedy, orderly completion.

## One-Man Bivouacs

One-man bivouacs are often necessary for small-unit harassing operations. When the mountain tent is not issued, the snow shelter trench is the most satisfactory form — but individuals should have cold-weather training and experience before being sent out alone.
