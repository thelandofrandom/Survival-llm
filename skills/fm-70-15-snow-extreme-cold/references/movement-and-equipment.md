# Movement and Equipment — FM 70-15

## Movement Over Snow and Ice

- **Snowshoes** (chapter 5) and **skiing** (chapter 6) are the individual means of crossing snow. In open terrain and under favorable snow conditions, **infantry on skis has greater mobility than foot troops operating on roads**. On the march, individuals should be equipped to be self-sustaining, carrying their own food and shelter.
- **Oversnow vehicles** (chapter 7): oversnow motorized vehicles (snow tractors, motorized toboggans) and nonmotorized vehicles (sleds). Oversnow vehicles properly used give units greater cross-country mobility than they would otherwise possess — but supply routes remain canalized to roads and trails, since trails must be used for oversnow equipment, snowshoers, and skiers if speed is to be maintained.
- **Animal transport** (chapter 8): types of animals, their uses and equipment, and their care and management. Animal food consumption rises in the cold; horses need nose shields and frequent rub-downs; sled dogs survive without prepared beds and shelter.
- **Equipment** (chapter 9): packs, rucksacks, packboards, sleeping equipment, and miscellaneous equipment — the load-carrying systems that let a man be self-sustaining on the march in snow.

## Special Care of Weapons and Equipment in the Cold

### Temperature ranges

Most equipment functions satisfactorily down to **20° below zero** with lubricants and preservatives prescribed for +32° to 0°. Below that temperature all materiel requires winterization and special precautions. Temperatures of **20° to 60° below zero** are what the manual means by "extreme cold"; temperatures of 60° to 90° below zero, though common in some localities, are relatively infrequent.

### Condensation — the hidden enemy

When warm air meets cold metal or glass, moisture deposits on the cold materiel: a rifle or telescope brought indoors condenses on exposed surfaces, and equipment assembled warm then moved outdoors condenses inside its air spaces. If not wiped off, condensation causes rust and corrosion, or freezes when moved outdoors and makes mechanisms inoperative; it renders glass opaque, puts water in closed containers such as fuel drums, and emulsifies lubricants.

**Prevention: keep arms, ammunition, and equipment at the same temperature as the air in which they are to be operated.**

- Unheated anterooms or porches should be used for storing weapons of men living in heated shelters.
- Muzzle and breech covers should be installed and kept securely fastened so wind cannot drive snow under them.
- Bores should be cleaned frequently; if ice has formed, hot oil may melt it for removal before cleaning.
- After firing, clean weapons while still warm — small-arms bores with rifle bore cleaner protected from freezing; large-caliber weapons with warm water to make the standard soda-ash solution, treated with alcohol, glycerine, or antifreeze compound if the gun has become cold.

### Vehicles and optics

- Vehicles should not be repeatedly moved between heated garages and cold outside air; abrupt temperature changes of any kind should be avoided.
- Fuel tanks should be kept filled; the effect of condensation in fuel tanks may be neutralized by adding **½ pint of ethyl alcohol at each refueling** to absorb the water.
- Glass-enclosed electric defrosters should be used on windshields not equipped with defrosting equipment connected to the vehicle heating system; cellophane taped to the windshield may serve as a temporary expedient.
- Optical instruments should not be brought directly into warm quarters when not in use and should be protected from ice and snow in transport; lenses fogged by condensation are cleaned with lens tissue paper, and ice on lenses outdoors is removed with lens tissue paper wetted with alcohol.
