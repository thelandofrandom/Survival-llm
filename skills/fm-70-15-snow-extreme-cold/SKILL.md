# FM 70-15 Operations in Snow and Extreme Cold

## Purpose

FM 70-15, *Operations in Snow and Extreme Cold* (War Department Basic Field Manual, 264 pages) — the WWII Army manual for operating in snow and extreme cold. It covers rations and their preparation, cold-weather clothing, shelter and heat, snowshoes, skiing, oversnow vehicles, animal transport, equipment, special care of weapons and equipment, and tactical operations in snow and extreme cold, with appendices on stream crossing and related subjects.

> **HISTORIC WWII-ERA DOCTRINE — READ THIS FIRST.** This is a 1940s manual. Its equipment (one-burner gasoline stoves, animal transport, oversnow tractors and motorized toboggans, 1940s lubricants and winterization practice) and its medical and nutritional practice are dated. Its durable value is in principles and fieldcraft: how cold changes the body's needs, how insulation actually works, how to shelter, move, and fight in deep snow, and how cold breaks weapons and vehicles. Where a current manual governs, it governs: the current cold-weather references are **ATP 3-90.96, *Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations*** and **TC 21-3, *Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas*** — ATP 3-90.96 is already a skill in this vault, and the vault also holds FM 31-70, *Basic Cold Weather Manual*. Use the current manuals as the authority and FM 70-15 as the deep historical field reference.

## 1. The Four Major Problems

Chapter 1 names four major problems in operations in snow and extreme cold:

1. **Keeping men and animals warm.**
2. **Moving troops across snow and ice.**
3. **Transporting and preserving supplies and equipment.**
4. **Preventing the malfunction of weapons and equipment.**

**Keeping warm.** Men are subject to chilling, frostbite, freezing, and snow blindness. When cold, they are less alert; without proper shelter and covering they lose sleep and weaken; fingers may become numb from cold, resulting in poor handling of weapons; they seek sheltered locations and huddle together for warmth. Violent exertion followed by inactivity can leave men overheated and then freezing. When the air is still and dry, men are likely to consider the temperature higher than it actually is and become careless in their precautions against frostbite. Animals suffer from cold and frosted lungs, may require hot food, warmed water, prepared beds and shelter (sled dogs survive without these), and eat more; horses need nose shields and frequent rub-downs.

**Moving across snow and ice.** Deep snow and drifts impede progress both on and off roads; movement of even a few feet from cleared areas becomes impossible without snow-crossing equipment. Ordinary wheeled vehicles are useless, and armored track-laying vehicles experience difficulty, in snow **over 18 inches deep**. Troops unable to cross snow and ice are caught in a continuous defile on cleared roads or trails. Snowshoes, skis, crampons or ice creepers, and oversnow vehicles — snow tractors and motorized toboggans — are essential.

**Supplies.** Supplies and equipment assume relatively greater importance in cold than normally, as life is more immediately dependent on their adequacy and uninterrupted delivery. The bulk of supplies, particularly fuel, rations, and forage, becomes greater in cold weather, while the means of handling the bulk are more narrowly limited. Certain items of supply freeze in extreme cold; means of prevention must be devised.

**Malfunction.** Motors require special starting and maintenance techniques; lubricants stiffen; gasoline and chemicals vaporize less readily; condensation of water occurs in fuel tanks and carburetors; oil and fuel fail to flow through pipes; water jackets freeze; springs become brittle; steel breaks more easily; frost fogs windshields and optical lenses; rubber sometimes cracks; batteries fail; ice collects in weapon bores; improperly lubricated mechanisms do not function. → `references/cold-weather-fundamentals.md`

## 2. Rations and Water — Eat More, Drink on Schedule

**2.1. Food is fuel for warmth.** Troops lose military efficiency when uncomfortably cold. Regular sustaining hot food and drink are prime factors in maintaining body warmth. More food is required in extreme cold than in a temperate climate, for four reasons: more energy to maintain normal body warmth; more energy from increased activity; more energy because of the additional weight of arctic clothing and equipment; more energy to overcome the increased obstacles of snow and ice.

**2.2. The three nutrients.** Protein (meats, fish, eggs, milk) supplies heat and energy in about the same amount as carbohydrate, but is used primarily as builder and repairer of muscle tissue, needed in about the same amount as in temperate climates. Carbohydrate (sugars, cereals, fruits, vegetables) produces energy speedily and is required in increased quantities in extreme cold. Fat (meat, milk, butter, lard, vegetable oils) takes longer to digest but gives off **about twice as much heat** as carbohydrate or protein; it is extremely important in the arctic diet because when stored in the body it can be used as future heat and energy, it insulates, and it delays the sensation of hunger.

**2.3. Deficiency diseases.** Scurvy and beriberi may develop after a number of months if not guarded against. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is the specific for prevention and cure of scurvy, Vitamin B1 (thiamin) for beriberi; when these vitamins are lacking from the diet, synthetic substitutes should be provided.

**2.4. Water.** Obtaining sufficient water for cooking and drinking is an important consideration in cold regions. Running water is used whenever available; glacier water is usually clean enough if sediment is given time to settle. Frequently snow or ice must be melted: **ice is preferred because it is denser than snow and yields more water for a given volume**. When snow must be used, wet or frozen snow is better than powder snow. Melt it properly or the bottom of the container may burn: pack about **½ inch of snow** in the bottom of the vessel and melt it completely, then add additional snow in amounts small enough that the contents are always slush. Water from running streams, lakes, and ponds may ordinarily be considered safe, but if any doubt exists — or river water is used — make it safe by boiling or treating with halazone or iodine.

**2.5. The canteen and eating snow.** Water is rarely carried in the canteen much below freezing. When carried, fill the canteen only **three-fourths full**, leaving room for the water to expand without breaking the canteen if it freezes solid; wrap the canteen in the sleeping bag or a garment for insulation. In the absence of drinking water, small quantities of snow may be eaten while troops are warm or on the move: place a small piece of ice or compressed snow in the mouth rather than sucking it (otherwise the lips may become chapped); if the soldier is cold, or hot and tired, he should not eat snow. **Snow should never be swallowed in lumps** — it may chill the stomach — and never eaten in large quantities, as it will materially reduce body temperature and may cause severe chafing of the lips. It is generally better to eat a little snow often than to wait until thirsty and eat too much.

**2.6. Cooking.** Cooking should always be done in as large a group as the situation permits, for convenience and fuel conservation; pooling stoves melts snow quickly at first, then each stove prepares part of the meal or stands ready as a substitute if one fails. Practical hints: thaw frozen meat before cooking (partly frozen meat may cook outside while the center stays raw); cook vegetables only until tender, in a small amount of water, and drink the cooking water or use it in soup; heat canned foods only enough to warm them; dip frozen potatoes in hot water and scrape off the skins; clean mess gear thoroughly in boiling water — unclean gear may cause diarrhea. One-burner gasoline cooking stoves are issued for mobile troops; at extremely low temperatures, solid fuel or a primer-type gasoline stove is desirable, and at outposts where fires are not permissible, thermos containers are advantageous. → `references/cold-weather-fundamentals.md`

## 3. Clothing — Insulation Is Trapped Air

**3.1. The principle.** Suitable cold-weather clothing is not warm in itself; it is merely a good insulator and a poor conductor of heat. The heat of the body is held in by the clothing and prevented from escaping into the atmosphere. Since **still air is an excellent insulator**, the best cold-weather clothes are those that entrap a considerable amount of air: the warmth of a woolen sweater lies mainly in the thousands of tiny air cells between the woolen fibers; fur is warm because of the air trapped among the hairs. **Several thin layers of cloth are better than one thick, heavy, matted piece**, because air pockets form between the layers.

**3.2. Perspiration is the enemy.** Clothing that has become damp is a poor insulating agent, and perspiration is a common source of moisture — physical exercise causes perspiration even in the coldest weather. To prevent excessive perspiration, which results in damp clothing and consequent chilling and frostbite of specific areas, troops must remove or loosen outer garments at the start of exercise; the garments are kept dry in the packs and put on again during breaks and at the end of the exercise. Complete information on the use, care, and maintenance of cold-weather clothing is in TM 10-275. → `references/cold-weather-fundamentals.md`

## 4. Shelter, Heat, and Camps

**4.1. What shelter must do.** Cold-weather shelter should provide an adequate windbreak, insulation against loss of heat from the interior, a source of heat where possible, and drying racks for clothing and equipment. Shelters should be sited to protect personnel and equipment from drifting snow and prevailing winds. They may be improvised from snow, logs, boughs, sod, or any material at hand in sufficient quantity, or transported — usually tents, but also prefabricated houses or huts, which should be light, compact, durable, and easily erected and disassembled by unskilled workers. Where buildings are available, use them to the greatest extent possible; isolated heated buildings are valuable for drying wet or sweaty clothing and footgear.

**4.2. Tents.** Medium-sized tents are better than small ones, since the body heat of a number of men together raises the inside temperature. A six-man tent may be improvised from double-ended shelter halves, with space near the peak for hanging clothing to dry. Floor cloths keep bedding off the ground. The two-man mountain tent, with a fixed floor cloth, is very satisfactory and quickly erected. The pyramidal tent normally issued is not entirely satisfactory in cold weather because of its weight. Prefabricated shelters of composition board, plywood, or galvanized steel usually accommodate six to eight men and may be heated by wood, coal, or oil stoves.

**4.3. Shelter in timber.** A bivouac in timber gives protective concealment (unless the timber stands alone so obviously as to indicate the bivouac's location). Timber with a high percentage of evergreen trees is most desirable — the trees provide maximum shelter and the best source of building materials, fuel, and bedding. Cut only lower branches, or small branches stripped from boughs too large for bedding; where boughs cannot safely be removed, straw, hay, newspapers, or pasteboard from ration and ammunition containers can serve as bedding.

- **Snow shelter trench:** excavated below the surface of the snow but not to the ground, larger than the sleeping bag; a bough bed several inches thick after compression, thick enough that the sleeping bag or equipment never touches snow; boughs covered by a shelter half, extra clothing laid evenly on top, sleeping bag on the clothing; an access pit prevents loose snow from being knocked into the bag on entering or leaving. It may be elaborated with a bough roof or an improvised stove at one end.
- **Lean-to:** the standard timber shelter, of considerable value when open fires are permitted; built in various sizes for two or more men, with the small lean-to warmer than a large one. Snow is tramped down or cleared; two large uprights with a transverse piece, back pieces at intervals, cross members, and boughs hooked on by their branches.

**4.4. Shelter without timber.** In open country, snow itself is the building material — snow houses (igloos) are covered in section III of the chapter and in Appendix I. Dry powdery snow is the best insulator; compact wet or wind-hardened snow is best for water supply.

**4.5. Camp siting.** Water supply is particularly important because of the extreme dehydration of men after several days' operations in snow and because dehydrated foods need unusual amounts of water for cooking; since ice yields more water than snow, camp near a source of ice if possible — streams are sometimes open even at 60° below zero. Place camps on the **downwind side of hills, about halfway or two-thirds up the slope**, since cold lies at the bottom of valleys. Place camps in timber when possible for windbreak and insulation. In hilly or mountainous terrain, examine snow on slopes above for avalanche danger. Pitch camp in the deepest snow available to use snow's insulating properties. Plan camps in advance: designate and mark unit areas, garbage and latrine sites, and command-post locations, and tape paths before construction begins. **Halt troops before darkness and before they are exhausted** — tired troops making camp in darkness and extreme cold neglect measures necessary for their safety, leading to a gradual reduction in effective personnel strength. Firewood should be at or near the camp site.

**4.6. Making camp.** On arrival, cooks immediately begin preparing hot food. The remaining men are divided into groups and put to work making the camp habitable: gathering boughs for beds (which must be quite thick to be effective), erecting shelter frameworks, supplying timber, wattling and thatching shelters and banking them with snow. No one does detailed work on individual shelters until all shelters are completed; the hot meal is served, boughs for bedding drawn, and adequacy of all shelters checked before dismissal. A standing operating procedure greatly facilitates speedy, orderly completion. One-man bivouacs are often necessary for small-unit harassing operations; when the mountain tent is not issued, the snow shelter trench is the most satisfactory form — but individuals should have cold-weather training and experience before being sent out alone. → `references/shelter-heat-and-camps.md`

## 5. Movement Over Snow and Ice

**5.1. Snowshoes and skis.** Chapter 5 covers snowshoes and chapter 6 covers skiing. In open terrain and under favorable snow conditions, **infantry on skis has greater mobility than foot troops operating on roads**. On the march, individuals should be equipped to be self-sustaining, carrying their own food and shelter.

**5.2. Oversnow vehicles.** Chapter 7 covers oversnow motorized vehicles (snow tractors, motorized toboggans) and nonmotorized vehicles (sleds). Oversnow vehicles properly used give units greater cross-country mobility than they would otherwise possess — but supply routes remain canalized to roads and trails, since trails must be used for oversnow equipment, snowshoers, and skiers if speed is to be maintained.

**5.3. Animal transport.** Chapter 8 covers types of animals, their uses and equipment, and their care and management. Animal food consumption rises in the cold; horses need nose shields and frequent rub-downs; sled dogs survive without prepared beds and shelter.

**5.4. Equipment.** Chapter 9 covers packs, rucksacks, packboards, sleeping equipment, and miscellaneous equipment — the load-carrying systems that let a man be self-sustaining on the march in snow. → `references/movement-and-equipment.md`

## 6. Special Care of Weapons and Equipment in the Cold

**6.1. Temperature ranges.** Most equipment functions satisfactorily down to **20° below zero** with lubricants and preservatives prescribed for +32° to 0°. Below that temperature all materiel requires winterization and special precautions. Temperatures of **20° to 60° below zero** are what the manual means by "extreme cold"; temperatures of 60° to 90° below zero, though common in some localities, are relatively infrequent.

**6.2. Condensation — the hidden enemy.** When warm air meets cold metal or glass, moisture deposits on the cold materiel: a rifle or telescope brought indoors condenses on exposed surfaces, and equipment assembled warm then moved outdoors condenses inside its air spaces. If not wiped off, condensation causes rust and corrosion, or freezes when moved outdoors and makes mechanisms inoperative; it renders glass opaque, puts water in closed containers such as fuel drums, and emulsifies lubricants. **Prevention: keep arms, ammunition, and equipment at the same temperature as the air in which they are to be operated.** Unheated anterooms or porches should be used for storing weapons of men living in heated shelters. Muzzle and breech covers should be installed and kept securely fastened so wind cannot drive snow under them. Bores should be cleaned frequently; if ice has formed, hot oil may melt it for removal before cleaning. After firing, clean weapons while still warm — small-arms bores with rifle bore cleaner protected from freezing; large-caliber weapons with warm water to make the standard soda-ash solution, treated with alcohol, glycerine, or antifreeze compound if the gun has become cold.

**6.3. Vehicles and optics.** Vehicles should not be repeatedly moved between heated garages and cold outside air; abrupt temperature changes of any kind should be avoided. Fuel tanks should be kept filled; the effect of condensation in fuel tanks may be neutralized by adding **½ pint of ethyl alcohol at each refueling** to absorb the water. Optical instruments should not be brought directly into warm quarters when not in use and should be protected from ice and snow in transport; lenses fogged by condensation are cleaned with lens tissue paper, and ice on lenses outdoors is removed with lens tissue paper wetted with alcohol. → `references/movement-and-equipment.md`

## 7. Tactical Operations in Snow and Extreme Cold

**7.1. Influencing factors.** Tactical operations in snow and extreme cold do not vary in principle from operations under any other conditions, but their application differs. Deep snow stops cavalry and wheeled vehicles and prevents movement of troops on foot as well as in armored vehicles other than those of special design. Snow acts as either an obstacle or an avenue of approach, depending on the equipment and training of the command. **Frozen ground makes passageways, instead of obstacles, of swamps, streams, and lakes.** Deep snowdrifts and icy slopes restrict movement. Emplacements and field works are difficult to construct, necessitating thawing or explosives. Tracks and installations are more apparent to aerial observers, and camouflage is both more difficult and of greater importance than usual. Unless wheeled vehicles are supplanted or supplemented by oversnow vehicles, supply routes are canalized to roads and trails.

**7.2. Training and the enemy.** For troops who have completed unit and combined training, considerable training in snow and cold is necessary before a unit is proficient; during training, cross-country movements without shelter in buildings during halts must be practiced. While friendly personnel are weakened in strength and morale by cold, fatigue, and hunger, this is equally true of the enemy: whenever possible, deny him towns or buildings, use patrols, aircraft, and harassing fire against him in bivouacs, and raid his supply system — deprive him of food, rest, and shelter, and expect corresponding efforts against your own troops.

**7.3. Organization, leadership, and operations.** Chapter 11 covers organization and equipment for winter operations, the arms and services, leadership and command, reconnaissance and security, movements, offensive and defensive operations, retrograde movements, special and airborne operations, and supply and evacuation — all adapted to the same principles as temperate operations, with the movement, shelter, and equipment constraints of this manual applied throughout. → `references/tactical-operations.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Warmth is the mission.** Cold degrades alertness, sleep, weapon handling, and morale before it kills — prevent it, don't react to it.
2. **Insulation is trapped air.** Layers beat bulk; damp clothing is failed clothing; ventilate before you sweat.
3. **Eat more, drink on schedule.** Fat is the cold-weather fuel; ice beats snow for water; never eat snow in lumps or in large quantities.
4. **Keep equipment at operating temperature.** Condensation is the silent killer of weapons, optics, and vehicles.
5. **Halt before dark and before exhaustion.** A camp built by tired men in the dark is a casualty list.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-90.96 and TC 21-3 govern modern cold-weather operations; FM 70-15 is the deep historical field reference.
