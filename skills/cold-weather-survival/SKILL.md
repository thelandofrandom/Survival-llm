# Cold-Weather Survival

## Purpose

TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas, U.S. Army, 30 September 2025, 136 pages — the individual Soldier's handbook for operating and surviving in the cold. It supersedes TC 21-3 of 17 March 1986, and it is the most current cold-weather doctrine in the vault. It covers what the cold does to men, weapons, and missions; how to dress, eat, drink, and sleep in it; how to move over snow; how to occupy a patrol base in it; and how to survive in it with nothing but what you carry.

The manual's governing fact is stated in Chapter 1 and repeated in Chapter 4: cold injury can disable a Soldier like an enemy bullet. The cold is not weather. It is a weapon, and it fires every minute you are exposed to it. The handbook is the countermeasure.

## 1. The cold and what it does (Chapter 1)

**Different types of cold conditions.** The manual divides cold into zones by temperature, and the division matters because the countermeasures change:
- **Wet cold:** temperatures above **19°F (−7°C)**. A freezing-and-thawing cycle, often with rain or wet snow. Hard to dress for, because the elements keep changing and keeping clothing dry is a constant battle. Wet cold is the most dangerous zone for its temperature, because wet clothing steals heat faster than dry cold air does.
- **Dry cold:** temperatures consistently below **19°F (−7°C)**. The ground is frozen and the snow is dry. Colder, but easier to stay warm — dry snow brushes off, and dry clothing insulates.
- Below dry cold, the zones are **intense, extreme, and hazardous** cold — also dry in nature, and Soldiers dress for the dry-cold environment in all of them.

**Effects of cold weather on military operations.** The cold slows everything: men move slower, weapons malfunction more, batteries die, fuel thickens, metal becomes brittle, and the simplest task takes twice as long with mittens on. The manual's point is that the cold is a planning factor, not an afterthought — a mission planned for warm weather will fail in the cold unless the plan is rebuilt for it. **Wind chill** is the complicating factor that turns a survivable temperature into a lethal one (Chapter 4). → `references/cold-injuries-and-care.md`

## 2. Personal clothing and equipment (Chapter 2)

**The layering principle.** Several layers provide more insulation and movement than one heavy garment, even if the heavy garment is as thick as the combined layers. Air pockets trapped between the layers provide insulation, warmth, and ventilation. The four rules that make layering work are the mnemonic **C-O-L-D** (Figure 2-2):
- **C — Keep it Clean.** Dirty clothes are cold clothes. Dirt and grease clog the air spaces in clothing, reducing insulation and ventilation.
- **O — Avoid Overheating.** Do not overdress. In cold weather it is better to be a little cool than to risk overheating — sweat is the enemy, because wet clothing conducts heat away from the body faster than dry clothing.
- **L — Wear it Loose.** All cold-weather uniform items are sized to allow the appropriate number of layers. If the uniform does not fit loosely, the insulation that generates warmth is greatly reduced.
- **D — Keep it Dry.** Moisture soaks into clothing from the inside (sweat) and the outside (melting snow). Guard against both. Always brush snow and frost from clothing before entering any heated shelter or vehicle.

**The Extended Cold-Weather Clothing System, Generation III (ECWCS Gen III)** — seven levels, worn in combination:

| Level | Item | Role |
|---|---|---|
| I | Lightweight cold-weather undershirt and drawers | Base layer — wicks moisture off the skin |
| II | Middleweight cold-weather shirt and drawers | Base layer; also an insulation layer when worn with Level I |
| III | Fleece jacket | Primary insulation layer for moderate to cold conditions |
| IV | Wind cold-weather jacket | Lightweight, windproof, water-repellent shell |
| V | Soft shell cold-weather jacket and trousers | Outer shell layer — highly wind-resistant, breathable |
| VI | Extreme cold/wet weather jacket and trousers | Waterproof outer shell for wet conditions |
| VII | Extreme cold-weather parka and trousers | Superior warmth — the heavy insulation layer for extreme cold |

Gloves and mittens follow the same logic: contact gloves under mittens (the Arctic mitten and intermediate cold-weather gloves, Figure 2-12). Snow sticks to wool gloves and melts — keep a dry pair. **The Level VII parka has a field use worth knowing:** zip it closed and slide it over your feet before getting into the sleeping bag, to keep feet warm (Figure 2-18).

**The Cold-Weather Equipment Modular Sleep System** is rated down to **−20°F (−28.8°C)** with the user wearing Level I or Level II underclothes — and colder when the Level III fleece jacket and Level V soft shell trousers are also worn. Keep the bag dry (water bottles and wet items go outside it or in a waterproof bag; tie the top securely), and never get into it wet. **Camouflage in the snow** follows the terrain: thick woods, low brush, roads and trails, and open snow above the tree line each have their own pattern (Figures 2-13 to 2-16) — white is not always the answer; a white figure against dark trees is a target.

**Packing the rucksack (Figure 2-19):** heavy items high and close to the back, frequently needed items accessible, everything waterproofed. **Load-carrying equipment:** the MOLLE system with skis (Figure 2-20), snowshoes secured two ways (Figure 2-21), ice axe attached to the rucksack (Figure 2-22), snowshoe binding attached to the boot (Figure 2-23). **Moving equipment over snow:** skis, snowshoes, and the ahkio (pulled sled) — the manual covers each, because the wrong transport for the snow wastes the energy you need to survive. → `references/clothing-and-equipment.md`

## 3. Weapons and ammunition (Chapter 3)

**Care, cleaning, and maintenance.** The cold's effects on weapons are specific and preventable: condensation forms when a cold weapon is brought into a warm shelter, then freezes when it goes back out — keep the weapon outside, or keep it at a constant temperature. Lubricants thicken; use the cold-weather lubricant and use less of it. Snow in the barrel is a catastrophic failure waiting to happen — keep the muzzle covered. Fuel, oil, or solvent splashed on skin in the cold can cause frostbite, because the liquid evaporates and supercools the skin.

**Breakage and malfunctions.** Metal becomes brittle in extreme cold; plastic cracks; a hot barrel can melt the lanyards on ski poles, the nylon on gloves and mittens, and the cotton canvas barrel cover is preferred because nylon canvas melts on contact with a hot barrel. The manual's malfunction drills are the standard ones, adapted: do them with mittens on, because you will be wearing mittens.

**Shooting positions** are adapted to the snow: standing on skis and snowshoes (Figure 3-1); firing while kneeling on skis (Figure 3-2) and on snowshoes (Figure 3-3); prone on skis and snowshoes (Figure 3-4); the M240 machine gun in tripod mode (Figure 3-5). The rule: the skis or snowshoes are the platform — never try to shoot from a position the snow will not hold. → `references/clothing-and-equipment.md`

## 4. Optimizing physical care (Chapter 4)

**Eating and drinking.** Most of what humans eat and drink is used to maintain body heat; only a small portion produces energy for physical work. Adequate caloric intake is essential: about **4,600 calories per day** for Soldiers doing physically demanding work in the cold. Efficiency drops rapidly if that level is not maintained. The rations: one MRE provides about **1,300 calories** — **four MREs per day** are needed to meet the requirement, and the downside is that most MRE components freeze. The Meal, Cold Weather (MCW) provides about **1,450 calories per bag**, with pocket items accessible throughout the day; the Modular Operational Ration Enhancement (MORE) packet adds **1,100 calories**; the Unitized Group Ration (UGR) Arctic supplement adds **914 calories**. The MCW is a restricted ration meant for short durations.

**Water.** The body loses liquid quickly in extreme cold, from sweat and from breathing — the exertion of moving on foot, preparing bivouac sites, and manual work in the snow takes a toll. Soldiers need approximately **3.75 to 5.25 quarts (3.5 to 5 liters) of water per day**, including the water present in rations. Insensible fluid loss (breathing and perspiration) is about **1.5 to 2.25 quarts (1.4 to 2.1 liters) per day**. Replace liquids regularly, preferably with hot drinks that provide extra calories when they contain sugar. **Do not eat snow** — it costs more heat to melt than the water is worth, and it lowers core temperature. Melt snow and drink it hot. One MRE has close to 8 ounces (~0.25 L) of liquid; one MCW has close to zero (freeze-dried, it needs a little more than 1 quart (~1 L) of water to reconstitute). A squad needs at least **8 gallons (~30.3 L) of water per day** on MCWs, and almost **12 gallons (~45.5 L)** at the high end — a company needs a HMMWV just for carrying water cans.

**Cleanliness and preventive health.** Keep the body clean — dirt and sweat in clothing destroy insulation. Preventive health covers snow blindness (wear sunglasses or improvised eye protection — Figure 4-1), sunburn (the snow reflects UV), tent eye (irritation from stove smoke in an enclosed tent), heat cramps and heat exhaustion (yes, in the cold — from overdressing and overexertion), hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration, and carbon monoxide poisoning.

**Wind chill (Table 4-1).** Wind chill is the combination of wind and low temperature. At **−20°F (−28.8°C) with calm wind**, there is little danger. At the same temperature with a **20 mph (32 kph) wind**, the equivalent chill temperature is **−63°F (−52.7°C)** — and exposed skin can freeze within **6 minutes**. Walking creates a wind speed of about **5 mph (8 kph)**; running, about **10 mph (16 kph)**. To use the chart: get the ambient air temperature, estimate the wind speed, and **multiply the wind speed by 1.5** to get the true wind speed at 33 feet (10 meters), the world meteorological standard. Find the two numbers on the chart; where they intersect is the wind chill. **Cold injury can disable a Soldier like an enemy bullet.**

**Carbon monoxide poisoning.** Fresh air in living and working quarters is vital. Carbon monoxide is a deadly, odorless gas that displaces oxygen in the bloodstream. It comes from stoves, heaters, and engines in enclosed spaces — the tent, the vehicle, the shelter. Ventilate, or die in your sleep. → `references/cold-injuries-and-care.md`

## 5. Occupy a patrol base (Chapter 5)

**Accomplishing the mission** in the cold means the patrol base is built for the cold: trail plans and occupation (the route in, the security plan, the layout); organizing the tent (Figure 5-1 — the location of the tent is chosen for wind protection, drainage, and concealment). Keep the tent fairly cool inside — if the snow is melting, reduce the heat from the heater system; spread a poncho on the ground; clean snow from boots and equipment before bringing them inside so they do not freeze. The patrol base in the cold is a life-support system, not just a perimeter: the same priorities of work apply (security first), but the cold adds the requirement that the base itself must keep men alive through the night. → `references/movement-shelter-and-survival.md`

## 6. Individual movement (Chapter 6)

**Moving over snow.** Skis and snowshoes are the two ways to move, and the manual covers both in detail — the techniques, the pace, and the energy cost. Moving over snow is the most exhausting movement a Soldier does; the pace is set by the slowest man, and the trail is broken by the lead man in rotation, because breaking trail burns energy twice as fast. **The challenge of land navigation** in the snow: the landscape is white, the landmarks are buried, the compass is affected by the cold and by magnetic anomalies, and dead reckoning is the primary method — pace count, azimuth, and time, checked against every identifiable feature. Snow distorts distance: a white plain looks closer than it is, and a ridge looks farther. Trust the compass and the pace count, not the eye. → `references/movement-shelter-and-survival.md`

## 7. Survival (Chapter 7)

**General emergency solutions.** In the winter it is not possible to stay in the open for long unless moving. Use natural shelters — caves or rock ledges — if available; if not, build a temporary improvised shelter. Comfort depends on initiative and improvisational skills.

**Improvised shelters:**
- **Poncho shelter (Figure 7-1):** the simplest — pull a poncho over a sleeping bag, or build a one-person shelter from one poncho: spread it on the ground, hood side up, close the hood opening by tying the drawstrings, raise the middle of its short dimension to form a ridge, stake the corners and sides, and seal the sides and one end with snow, sod, or branches for heat retention and wind protection.
- **Lean-to (Figures 7-3, 7-4):** built in forested areas from trees and limbs, with a poncho, canvas, or boughs laid like overlapping shingles. A double lean-to is two singles facing each other with the fire between them.
- **Snow trench (Figure 7-6):** provides shelter quickly — burrow into a snowdrift or dig a trench in the snow.
- **Snow cave (Figure 7-7):** for open areas with deep, compacted snow. Locate a snowdrift at least **10 feet (~3 meters) deep**. Make the cave high enough for comfortable sitting space; the sleeping area is on a raised platform, and the roof is about **30 cm (12 inches) thick**. A large cave is more efficient for squad-size groups. Building a snow cave requires practice — and a molded-dome shelter (snow piled into a mound, then hollowed out) is the alternative when no drift is available.

**Food.** The manual covers food sources in the cold — including what is available in the arctic environment (the manual's food section covers fish, small game, and, in the far north, sea mammals, and the snakes of warmer cold-weather regions). The rule that governs all of it: in the cold, food is fuel for heat first and energy second — and a hungry man in the cold is a cold man, because he has nothing to burn.

**Knots (Figures 7-15 to 7-18, paras 7-45 to 7-49):**
- **Overhand knot (7-45):** to make a knotted rope for a hand line, or to temporarily whip the end of a rope.
- **Square knot (7-46):** to tie the ends of two ropes of equal width together.
- **Clove hitch (7-47):** the most useful of the hitches — to tie the end of a rope to a post or eye.
- **Double sheet bend (7-48):** to tie two ropes of different width together.
- **Bowline (7-49):** to form a loop that does not slip on the end of a rope. The lead Soldier on a rope team ties off to the end of the rope with a bowline just under the armpits.

**Appendix B** is Battle Drill 1: React to Direct Fire Contact While Dismounted — Platoon (07-PLT-D9501), the same drill as the Ranger Handbook's Chapter 8. **Appendix C** is Emergency Visual Signals — the ground-to-air signals for a Soldier in distress, the companion to the `visual-signals` skill (TC 3-21.60). **Appendix A** is the U.S. customary to metric conversion table. → `references/movement-shelter-and-survival.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **C-O-L-D: Clean, avoid Overheating, wear it Loose, keep it Dry.** The four rules are the whole clothing system.
2. **Cold injury disables like a bullet.** Treat wind chill, frostbite, and hypothermia as enemy action, not weather.
3. **Sweat is the enemy.** It is better to be a little cool than to overheat — wet clothing steals heat faster than cold air does.
4. **4,600 calories and 3.5–5 liters of water a day.** In the cold, food is fuel for heat first. Do not eat snow — melt it and drink it hot.
5. **Ventilate or die in your sleep.** Carbon monoxide is odorless, and the tent is the most dangerous place in the cold.
6. **Trust the compass and the pace count, not the eye.** Snow distorts distance, buries landmarks, and lies about both.
