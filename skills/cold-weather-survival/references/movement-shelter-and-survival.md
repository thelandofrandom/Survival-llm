# Movement, Shelter, and Survival — TC 21-3, Chapters 5–7 and Appendices

## Chapter 5 — Occupy a patrol base: outline

- Accomplishing the mission; trail plans and occupation; organizing the tent.

## The patrol base in the cold

- **Trail plans:** the route in, the security plan, and the layout are planned for the cold — the base must keep men alive through the night, not just hidden from the enemy.
- **Tent location (Figure 5-1):** chosen for wind protection, drainage, and concealment. The lee side of a ridge or a stand of trees breaks the wind; a hollow collects cold air and meltwater.
- **Organizing the tent:** keep the tent fairly cool inside — if the snow is melting, reduce the heat from the heater system. Spread a poncho on the ground. Clean snow from boots and equipment before bringing them inside so they do not freeze. Water bottles can be kept inside the bag to keep them from freezing, but the top of the bag must be tied securely.
- The patrol base in the cold is a life-support system, not just a perimeter: the same priorities of work apply (security first), but the cold adds the requirement that the base itself must keep men alive.
- **Carbon monoxide:** fresh air in the tent is vital — ventilate when cooking or heating, or the tent becomes the most dangerous place in the cold.

## Chapter 6 — Individual movement: outline

- Moving over snow; the challenge of land navigation.

## Moving over snow

- **Skis and snowshoes** are the two ways to move. The manual covers both in detail — the techniques, the pace, and the energy cost.
- Moving over snow is the most exhausting movement a Soldier does. The pace is set by the slowest man, and the trail is broken by the lead man in rotation, because breaking trail burns energy roughly twice as fast as following in a broken trail.
- **The ahkio** (pulled sled) carries the heavy load so the Soldier does not have to — in deep snow, pulling is more efficient than carrying.
- **Rope teams:** on glaciers and steep snow, the patrol moves roped together. The lead Soldier ties off to the end of the rope with a bowline just under the armpits.

## Land navigation in the snow

- The landscape is white, the landmarks are buried, the compass is affected by the cold and by magnetic anomalies, and **dead reckoning is the primary method** — pace count, azimuth, and time, checked against every identifiable feature.
- Snow distorts distance: a white plain looks closer than it is, and a ridge looks farther. Trust the compass and the pace count, not the eye.
- **Pace count in snow:** the pace is shorter and the count is higher than on dry ground — calibrate the pace count in the snow you are actually moving through, not on a parade ground.
- Navigation checkpoints must be chosen for visibility in snow — a rock outcrop, a tree line, a stream bank — not a feature that disappears under a foot of snow.

## Chapter 7 — Survival: outline

- General emergency solutions; improvised shelters; food; knots.

## General emergency solutions

- In the winter it is not possible to stay in the open for long unless moving. Use natural shelters — caves or rock ledges — if available; if not, build a temporary improvised shelter.
- Building a shelter depends on the equipment and materials available. Some sort of shelter can be built during any season. In open terrain, a shelter can be built with ponchos, canvas, snow blocks, or other materials. Snow caves, snow trenches, snow houses, or snow holes can be built in the winter. When in the woods, a lean-to is usually preferred. Comfort depends on initiative and improvisational skills.

## Improvised shelters

### Poncho shelter (Figure 7-1)

The simplest type: pull a poncho over a sleeping bag. For a one-person shelter from one poncho: spread the poncho on the ground, hood side up; close the hood opening by adjusting and tying the drawstrings; raise the middle of the poncho's short dimension to form a ridge; stake out the corners and sides; seal the sides and one end with snow, sod, or branches for heat retention and wind protection. Various shelters and lean-tos can be made by attaching a poncho to trees, branches, or poles.

### Lean-to (Figures 7-3, 7-4)

- **Single lean-to:** built in forested areas from trees and tree limbs, with a poncho, canvas, or boughs laid like overlapping shingles (shingle-style, so water runs off rather than through).
- **Double lean-to:** two singles facing each other with the fire between them. Any kind of open fire can heat a lean-to; the best kind for a large lean-to is the log fire.

### Snow trench (Figure 7-6)

Provides shelter quickly — burrow into a snowdrift or dig a trench in the snow. The fastest winter shelter in the manual: dig in, cover the top with a poncho or branches, and get out of the wind.

### Snow cave (Figure 7-7, paras 7-17 to 7-21)

- For open areas with deep, compacted snow. Locate the site on the **lee side of a steep ridge or riverbank** where drifted snow collects and becomes wind-packed.
- **The drift must be at least 10 feet (~3 meters) deep.** Test the depth with a sharpened branch or young tree about 13 feet (~4 meters) long, or with a ski or ski pole. Newly fallen, powdery, or loose snow should not be used.
- **Entrance:** chosen so wind does not blow into the cave or block the entrance with drifting snow — about **45 degrees from the downwind side**. Burrow a small tunnel directly into the side of the drift for **3 feet (~1 meter)**, then excavate the chamber to the right and left, so the chamber's length is at right angles to the tunnel entrance.
- **Interior:** make the cave high enough for comfortable sitting space. The sleeping area is on a **higher level than the highest point of the tunnel entrance**, where the air is warmer. The roof is **arched for strength** and so that drops of water forming on the inside follow the curvature and do not fall on the floor — the walls glaze over when frozen. **The roof is about a foot (30 cm) thick.**
- **Size:** the size depends on the number of occupants. A large cave is usually warmer and more practical to build and maintain than several small caves. Short side tunnels are dug for sleeping rooms (one or two Soldiers), storage, a latrine, or kitchen space.
- **Digging:** personnel doing the digging become wet from perspiration and snow — wear a minimum amount of clothing so you have a change of dry clothes when finished. Block the entrance with a rucksack, poncho, or snow block when not in use. Use all available materials (ponchos, cardboard from ration boxes, brush, boughs) for ground insulation.
- **Ventilation:** at least **two ventilator holes** — one in the roof, one in the door. A ski pole can be stuck through the roof ventilator to clear it from the inside. Inspect the holes **every 2 or 3 hours** to ensure they have not clogged with snow or ice. Extra care is taken to keep the air fresh when cooking or heating.
- **Safety:** walking on the roof can cause it to collapse. Mark the entrance by placing a pair of skis, or other equipment, upright on each side of the entryway.
- **Practice:** building a snow cave requires practice. Large shelters are usually more efficient for squad-size and larger units. The most critical decision is when and where to stop and build the shelter.
- **Molded-dome shelter:** snow piled into a mound, then hollowed out — similar in appearance to a snow cave, used when no drift is available.

## Food

- In the cold, food is fuel for heat first and energy second. A hungry man in the cold is a cold man, because he has nothing to burn.
- The manual's food section covers what is available in cold-weather areas — fish, small game, and, in the far north, sea mammals — and the snakes of the warmer cold-weather regions. The through-line: know what is edible in the area you are operating in before you need to eat it, and preserve your carried rations for when there is nothing else.
- **Calories are the metric:** 4,600 per day for demanding work. If you are eating less than that, you are getting colder — not just hungrier.

## Knots (paras 7-45 to 7-49, Figures 7-15 to 7-18)

| Knot | Use (para) |
|---|---|
| Overhand knot (7-45) | Make a knotted rope for a hand line, or temporarily whip the end of a rope |
| Square knot (7-46) | Tie the ends of two ropes of equal width together |
| Clove hitch (7-47) | **The most useful of the hitches** — tie the end of a rope to a post or eye |
| Double sheet bend (7-48) | Tie two ropes of different width together |
| Bowline (7-49) | Form a loop that does not slip on the end of a rope. The lead Soldier on a rope team ties off with a bowline just under the armpits |
