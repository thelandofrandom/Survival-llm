                                                 Chapter 1
                Introduction to Cold-Weather Operations

DIFFERENT TYPES OF COLD CONDITIONS
   1-1. There are different types of cold found throughout the world and each presents its own challenges. For
   Soldiers to be effective in a cold-weather environment, it is essential they first understand the effects of cold
   on the body and equipment. (See ATP 3-90.97 for more information.)
   1-2. Weather is considered to be cold when the temperature registers 39 degrees (°) Fahrenheit (F) or
   4° Celsius (C) above (+) zero and drops to negative (–) 40°F or lower. The Army groups cold temperatures
   into five categories:
        • Temperature zone I (wet cold): +39°F to + 20°F (4°C to –7°C).
        • Temperature zone II (dry cold): +19°F to –4°F (–7°C to –20°C).
        • Temperature zone III (intense cold): –5°F to –25°F (–20°C to –32°C).
        • Temperature zone IV (extreme cold): –25°F to –40°F (–32°C to –40°C).
        • Temperature zone V (hazardous cold): –40°F (–40°C) and below.

         Note. An explanation on how to convert Fahrenheit and Celsius degrees, as well as common
         United States customary and metric conversion units can be found in appendix A.

WET AND DRY ENVIRONMENTS
   1-3. Wet-cold conditions often occur when snow and rain mix with cold weather. This type of environment
   is often more dangerous to troops and equipment than colder, dryer environments due to slushy, muddy
   ground, and wet clothing and equipment. Water conducts heat twenty-five times faster than air, causing core
   body temperatures to quickly drop when Soldiers become wet and caught in blowing wind.
   1-4. Soldiers rapidly become casualties due to weather if not properly equipped and trained for this
   environment. Wet cold can quickly lead to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot. Wet-cold conditions exist
   in many other environments during seasonal transition periods. Under wet-cold conditions, the ground
   alternates between freezing and thawing due to fluctuating temperatures above and below the freezing point.
   This makes planning problematic. For example, areas that are trafficable when frozen could become severely
   restricted if the ground thaws. Waterproof footwear is essential.
   1-5. Dry-cold conditions are easier to live in than wet-cold conditions. Like wet cold, proper equipment,
   training, and leadership are critical for successful dry-cold operations. Wind chill is a complicating factor in
   this type of cold. Of the four cold-weather categories, the dry-cold environment best enables troops to survive
   due to low humidity and permanently frozen ground. As a result, people and equipment are not subject to the
   effects of the thawing and freezing cycle, and precipitation is generally in the form of dry snow.
INTENSE, EXTREME, AND HAZARDOUS CONDITIONS
   1-6. Intense cold exists from –5°F to –25°F (–20°C to –32°C) and can affect the mind as much as the body.
   Intense cold has a rapidly numbing effect. Simple tasks take longer and require more effort than when done
   in warmer temperatures. The quality of work degrades as attention to detail diminishes in this type of
   environment. Clothing becomes bulkier to compensate for the cold, causing Soldiers to lose dexterity.
   Commanders need to consider these factors when planning operations and assigning tasks.
   1-7. Extreme conditions occur when the temperature falls below –25°F (–32°C), and the challenge
   of survival becomes paramount. During extremely cold conditions, individuals can easily prioritize
   physical comfort above all else. Soldiers may withdraw into themselves and adopt a cocoon-like
   existence. Leaders should expect and plan for weapon, vehicle, and munition failures when temperatures
   drop this low. Leadership, training, and specialized equipment are critical for successful operations
   in this harsh environment.

    1-8. During hazardous conditions, commanders and planners assume a great deal of risk if they choose to
    engage in operations when the temperature falls below –40°F (–40°C). Extensive training is required before
    undertaking an operation in such extreme cold.
WIND CHILL
    1-9. Wind chill is a measure of the combined effects of wind and temperature. Temperature alone does not
    give a true indication of the impact of cold weather. To effectively gauge the difference between temperature
    and the impact of cold, a wind-chill scale is used.
    1-10. A simple and practical guide outlining the conditions when cold weather becomes dangerous and
    exposed flesh is likely to freeze can be found in chapter 4 of this publication. Familiarity with the included
    wind-chill chart helps Soldiers recognize these dangers.

EFFECTS OF COLD WEATHER ON MILITARY OPERATIONS
    1-11. With its numbing effect on skin, cold hinders task performance. This fact is considered
    when performing routine tasks such as vehicle maintenance (see TM 4-33.31), and when making and
    striking bivouac areas.
    1-12. When conditions become extreme and the temperature drops, survival becomes critical. Under these
    circumstances, Soldiers may tend to withdraw emotionally, not wanting to leave the warmth and protection
    of their shelter or tent. The remedy is physical activity. Remaining alert and active helps alleviate this
    problem. Execution of all military duties, and the fast and proper performance of group tasks is vital.
    1-13. In addition to emotional withdrawal, many Soldiers, when wearing headgear and several layers of
    clothing, tend to assume a cocoon-like existence. When clothed like this, many Soldiers’ hearing and field of
    vision are restricted; they tend not to notice their surroundings. Thinking and reasoning can become sluggish.
    1-14. This process is characterized by individuals seeking the comfort of sleeping bags, and by the
    group remaining in tents or other shelters. Guard and security measures may be neglected, jeopardizing
    the safety of the unit. Talking or even acknowledging others can become nonexistent.
    1-15. Group hibernation can also occur. It is a process characterized when an entire unit, usually a squad,
    seeks the comfort of sleeping bags and shelter. Mundane tasks such as maintaining equipment and even
    preparing rations are neglected in favor of staying warm. Security postures may also be neglected,
    jeopardizing the safety of the unit.
OVERCOMING COLD
    1-16. It is essential the body stays protected. It is kept clean, dry, and warm, with all normal body processes
    maintained. Rest and nourishment are vital. The right approach to cold-weather living promotes health and
    provides strength and fortitude through challenging times. There are four basic rules for success:
        • Stay in shape.
        • Drink plenty of water.
        • Eat to stay fit.
        • Maintain a good attitude.
    1-17. Cold-weather clothing and equipment is heavy and adds weight to the normal equipment load.
    This hinders movement in snow and uses a lot of physical energy. The importance of being in excellent
    physical condition in this environment cannot be overemphasized. Developing a tough, challenging, training
    program is essential.
    1-18. Water may be hard to procure, causing Soldiers to drink less than needed. This leads to dehydration.
    Drink at least 3.5 quarts (3.3 liters) of water per day to avoid dehydration and fatigue. Do not eat snow as a
    water substitute because it lowers core body temperature. It also contains a lot of air that can cause a very
    uncomfortable stomachache.
    1-19. Regular, nutritious hot food is needed for top performance. Eating, even when not hungry, and calorie
    intake should be a near-continuous activity. Field rations are designed to be portable, pocket-sized, and
    should be used by every Soldier.

   1-20. Maintaining a good attitude when environmental conditions are harsh can be difficult. Aggressive
   leadership actions that overcome the challenges of the environment are essential for mission accomplishment.
POSITIVE LEADERSHIP
   1-21. While living and working in a frigid environment presents many new challenges, personal attitude
   about cold weather tends to reflect from the leader’s point of view. A bad attitude and gloomy outlook can
   spread like wildfire. After 3 to 4 weeks of cold-weather training, most Soldiers can cope with the
   environment. Some Soldiers may not be as skilled as others, but most succeed. As a leader, set an example
   for other Soldiers.
   1-22. Winter conditions can be frightening, especially when trying to meet new challenges. Any mistake
   could create a casualty. There are two adversaries to overcome in cold-weather operations: the enemy soldier
   and the freezing environment. The first step in defeating either problem is having the right attitude. Cold can
   be psychologically debilitating for a poorly prepared unit if leaders are unaware of the symptoms.
   Suggestions on how to defeat the cold include—
       • Soldiers and leaders getting more involved with others if depression or moodiness sets in so that the
            weaker Soldier is not left out.
       • Reviewing orders and drills if it becomes hard to remember things that were previously taught.
       • Reminding Soldiers that their job is to close with and destroy the enemy.
       • Keeping weapons and equipment in fighting order.
       • Reminders that operations can easily become a camping trip with serious consequences if a common
            ambush cannot be avoided in a cold environment.
       • Emphasizing following an order may take longer due to the weather but is no excuse not to do it.
       • Having knowledge, proper equipment, and rigorous training can help anyone overcome the cold and
            be successful in combat.

WEAR AND CARE OF INDIVIDUAL CLOTHING
   2-1. The Army’s cold-weather clothing system is designed for protection in various weather situations.
   The principal design features of this system are insulate, layer, and ventilate. Insulation material reduces the
   amount of body heat loss. Regulating the amount of insulation can affect the amount of heat that is lost or
   retained. (See figure 2-1.) Ventilation helps maintain a comfortable body temperature by allowing excess
   heat and moisture to escape.




                                           Figure 2-1. Insulation

    2-2. Several layers of clothing provide more insulation and movement than one heavy garment, even if the
    heavy garment is as thick as the combined layers. Air pockets trapped between the layers provide insulation,
    warmth, and ventilation.
    2-3. There are four ways to make these principles work: keep it clean, avoid overheating, wear it loose, and
    keep it dry. Remembering this mnemonic as C-O-L-D helps when dressing for the weather. (See figure 2-2.)




                                              Figure 2-2. C-O-L-D
    2-4. Dirty clothes are cold clothes. Dirt and grease clog the air spaces in clothing. reducing insulation and
    ventilation. Select comfortable clothing but do not overdress. In cold weather, it is better to be a little cool
    rather than risk overheating.
    2-5. All items of the cold-weather uniform are large enough to allow wearing the appropriate number of
    needed layers. If the uniform items do not fit loosely, the insulation that generates warmth is greatly reduced.
    2-6. It is very important to keep clothing dry. Wet clothing conducts heat away from the body faster than dry
    clothing. Moisture soaks into clothing from the inside and the outside. From the inside, guard against
    sweating and from the outside, guard against melting snow. Always brush snow and frost from clothing
    before entering any heated shelter or vehicle.
DRESSING FOR WET-COLD OR DRY-COLD WEATHER
    2-7. As mentioned in chapter one, temperatures that are above 19°F (–7°C) are known as wet-cold conditions;
    temperatures consistently below 19°F (–7°C) cause dry-cold conditions. Wet-cold conditions usually involve
    a freezing and thawing cycle, often accompanied by rain or wet snow. This type of weather is hard to dress
    for and Soldiers battle ever-changing elements to keep clothing dry.
    2-8. During dry-cold conditions, the ground is usually frozen and snow is dry. Although temperatures
    during these conditions may be lower than wet-cold conditions, it is easier to stay warm. The temperature
    zones below dry cold (intense, extreme, and hazardous) are also dry in nature, and Soldiers should dress for
    the dry-cold environment.
    2-9. A major protection in reducing the effects of heat loss is caring for the head and feet. When in the shelter,
    be sure not to hang clothing on drying lines too close to the tent heater or stove fire. Too much heat damages
    fabric or dries leather, destroying the insulation value.
BALACLAVA
    2-10. Wear the balaclava (hat) under the helmet or under the hood of the shell layer. To do so, there are three
    configurations. Fold the balaclava—
           Up, into a top-of-the-head hat.
           Down, with the face exposed.
           Down, with the face covered.

    2-11. Up, into a top-of-the-head hat: fold the bottom of the balaclava to the inside to form a hat. Wear the hat
    with the face opening to the rear to prevent breath condensation forming on the face-forward portion. If there
    is a need to change this configuration later, a wet hat is not placed directly on the face.
    2-12. Down, with the face exposed: pull the balaclava over the head with the lower portion of the face opening
    under the chin. Note that doing this repeatedly stretches the fabric and reduces its ability to stay over the nose
    when needed.

   2-13. Down, with the face covered: pull the lower portion of the face opening over the mouth and nose. Use

BOOTS
   2-14. Intermediate cold-wet boots (ICWB) with removable tan liners can be ordered using the national stock
   number (NSN) 8430-01-527-8274. They are rated from 68°F to 14°F (20°C to –10°C). Two pairs of liners
   are issued with this boot. When donning the boot with liners, put the liners on first and
   secure them with hook and loop tape. Then, insert the foot into the boot, ensuring there is no bunching
   as this causes blisters. When fitting this boot, make sure to wear the sock type used in the field and that the
   boot fits as comfortably as street shoes.
   2-15. Extreme cold-weather boots (NSN 8430-00-655-5535) are also called white vapor barrier (VB) boots,
   or bunny boots. They are rated from 19°F to –60°F (–7°C to –51C) and are used in cold-dry environments.
   Some VB boots have a pressure relief valve. The valve is used to equalize air pressure. When rapidly
   changing altitude, briefly open the valve to equalize pressure and then close it. (See figure 2-3.)

    workload. Leaders should ensure that both types of boots are available in the event of a temperature swing
    or change of mission. This gives maximum flexibility.
    2-17. When active, feet sweat. Due to the waterproof rubber lining, moisture remains on socks or in the
    bottom of the boots. The feet, even if damp, stay warm because the heat given off by the body remains inside
    the boots. If the boots are worn for long periods of time, the feet become white and wrinkled when the
    footwear is removed. This is not cause for alarm. Drying, massaging with foot powder, warming, and putting
    on dry socks corrects this.
    2-18. If there are long periods of inactivity, feet may become cold. Exercises such as knee bends, stomping,
    running in place, or wiggling toes help make them warm. Staying active and caring for these boots is very
    important. They should be washed inside and out with soapy water at least once a month, or as required by
    the unit standard operating procedure (SOP). Do not wear VB boots in a sleeping bag, as the feet need to dry.
    2-19. Inspect these boots often and repair any holes or punctures immediately. Temporary repairs
    can be made by patching the hole with any kind of tape. Even chewing gum can be used. Seal the hole
    quickly to prevent the insulation from becoming wet. If one of the boots feels heavier than the other,
    the insulation is probably wet, and should be turned in for new ones. Insulated boots are tested annually to
    ensure they are still waterproof and have less than 0.01 percent moisture in the insulation. The repair
    kit (NSN 8465-00-753-6335) is similar to a bicycle inner tube repair kit.

EXTENDED COLD-WEATHER CLOTHING SYSTEM, GEN III
    2-20. The extended cold-weather clothing system (ECWCS) is issued as a kit, allowing
    flexibility through all temperature zones. System components use the most recent materials and technology.
    The result is a reduction in weight and bulk from the GEN II system without loss of protection from
    the cold. The GEN III ECWCS uses the layering approach to draw moisture away from the body and should
    be worn as discussed below.
    2-21. The first layer of clothing is the base layer. It draws moisture away from the body, allowing it to
    evaporate. The next or middle layer is the insulation layer. It provides trapped air spaces that create warmth.
    The last layer is the shell layer. It protects all the other layers, blocking wind and precipitation.

SEVEN LEVELS OF DRESSING
   2-22. Level I: lightweight cold-weather undershirt and drawers. This is a base layer consisting of a long
   sleeve top and full-length bottom garments constructed with silk-weight moisture-wicking polyester. The

PACKING THE RUCKSACK
     2-53. Properly packing the fighting load and approach load should follow the A-B-C’S principle:
         • Access (to mission critical items).
         • Balance.
         • Compress.
         • Streamline.
ACCESS AND BALANCE
     2-54. Mission critical items (ammunition, medical kits, and other items) should be readily retrievable at any
     given time. The location of these items is standard across the unit so they can be found in a hurry by anyone,
     anywhere. Keep similar items together. For example, several Soldiers carrying crew-served weapon parts
     should stay within close proximity to each other. Material for rope installations (artificial anchors, webbing,
     rope, and other items) should be carried in close proximity to the team that sets up the installation.
     Consumables such as water, batteries, and other needed articles should come out of the pack first, instead of
     the FLC. This way if a Soldier gets separated from their rucksack, resources are still available. In extreme
     cold, the heaviest insulation layer should be on top of the rucksack to quickly put on if needed during halts.
     2-55. A rucksack that is lopsided and ill-fitting is misery. Soldiers must properly fit the rucksack to their
     bodies. (Refer to TM 10-8465-236-10 for more information.) Ensure that the body armor, FLC, and rucksack
     all work together. Remember that ounces make pounds and pounds make pain. Police Soldiers to reduce
     nonessential and redundant equipment. Strive to use multifunctional equipment as much as possible. Rations
     can be field stripped, and packaging removed from items. However, waterproofing is absolutely necessary.
     A drenching rain can more than double the weight of equipment very quickly. Do not load the sustainment
     pouches in lieu of the main compartment as they can flop around. Consider using the daypack or
     consolidating loads between two or more Soldiers.
COMPRESS AND STREAMLINE
     2-56. Utilize the compression straps to keep the internal load from shifting. This also keeps the load closer
     to the body. Carry the load on the hips and legs, not the shoulders. Tighten the waist strap to a very snug fit
     just above the bony portion of the hips. The rucksack should remain in place on the pelvic area without any
     help from the shoulder straps. The straps should be tightened enough to keep the pack snug against the body.
     2-57. Hanging equipment on the outside of the rucksack is a very poor practice. Heavier items can be felt
     swinging with each step. Items can snag on brush, make excessive noise, and possibly break or be lost in a
     tumble. When rock climbing, they get caught on nubbins and in cracks, causing a loss of balance or a fall. In
     snow-covered areas, a sled can be used to transport this equipment.
     2-58. Items such as hydration bladders and assault packs act like a lever, increasing the perceived weight.
     Place these directly on top or inside, close to the body. Mortar tubes, man-portable air defense system
     (MANPADS), and other items may be better carried by hand with a sling, especially in heavy brush.
     Equipment such as a machine gun tripod or ice axe is usually carried on the outside of the pack. Attach them
     in a secure manner where they are least likely to create problems while on the move.

SKIS
   2-67. Current standard military skis come in three lengths. The appropriate length for each Soldier is

MISCELLANEOUS EQUIPMENT
   2-70. Sunglasses and canteens are essential items every Soldier should carry with their cold-weather gear.

SUNGLASSES
   2-71. Polarized ultraviolet-blocking sunglasses are always worn when the ground is covered with snow,
   especially on bright days. Failure to wear sunglasses, even on overcast days, may result in snow blindness.

   2-72. Should this situation occur, a fellow Soldier is assigned to help the injured Soldier with the simplest of

   2-74. Hydration bladders also freeze if left outside but can be placed inside insulation layers to keep the water

CARE, CLEANING, AND MAINTENANCE
   3-1. Cold weather greatly affects the operation of weapons. Be aware of these effects to ensure the weapon
   is operational even under poor conditions such as snow, freezing rain, and extreme cold.
LUBRICANT USE
   3-2. If given proper care, weapons function in extreme cold. Lubricants that are normally used under
   temperate conditions, such as cleaner lubricant preservative (CLP) thicken in cold weather, resulting in
   stoppages or sluggish weapon action. CLP freezes at –35°F (–37.2°C). To eliminate this problem,
   the weapon is completely stripped, thoroughly cleaned, and lubricated with lubricating oil, weapons (LAW).
   3-3. Ensure that all the CLP is removed from the weapon before applying LAW. The two substances do not
   mix well and cause problems. At a minimum, the camming surfaces of the bolt should be lightly oiled with
   LAW. The rest of the weapon can be left dry.

         Note. When firing weapons dry, weapons are cleaned prior to storing.

   3-4. Dry firing is not a license to store dirty weapons. LAW is available in one-quart containers
   (NSN 9150-00-292-9689). If LAW is not available, fire the weapon dry. Graphite is known to be a good
   cold-weather lubricant but can cause galvanic corrosion over the long term.
STORING WEAPONS
   3-5. Condensation forms on weapons when they are moved from cold to warm environments. This is called
   sweating. If weapons are taken back into the cold without removing the condensation, this sweat can turn
   into ice, resulting in stoppages. For this reason, it is best to leave weapons outside when temperatures are
   below freezing. When left outside, firearms should be readily accessible but sheltered so that ice and snow
   does not enter the working parts (sight and barrel). If necessary, weapons can be taken inside for cleaning.
   Condensation continues to collect for approximately 1 hour after introducing the weapon into a warm shelter.
   Wait until the sweating process has concluded before starting the cleaning process. If weapons are to be kept
   in heated shelters, they should be kept near (but not on) the floor to minimize condensation.
   3-6. To keep snow and ice out of a weapon, some type of cover is needed. Request muzzle caps from the unit
   armorer because they are expendable and efficient (NSN 5340-00-880-7666). If none are available, improvise
   using plastic bags, tape, or any other suitable covering. Soldiers can shoot through a muzzle cap or a single
   layer of tape. Ejection port covers are kept closed.
   3-7. If weapons become frozen, various products can be used to de-ice them, such as windshield wiper fluid,
   padlock de-icer, and vehicle antifreeze. Periodic cycling of the weapon also keeps parts from freezing.
FIRING ISSUES AND STRATEGIES
   3-8. A visibility problem is possible when weapons are fired in motionless air conditions where temperatures
   are below –30°F (–34.4°C). As the round leaves the weapon, the hot propellant gases cause the water vapor
   in the air to condense. These droplets of condensed water vapor then freeze, creating ice particles that produce
   a cloud of ice fog. This fog hangs over the weapon and follows the path of the projectile, obstructing the
   gunner’s vision along the line of fire. This fog also reveals the Soldier’s location to the enemy. When faced
   with this problem, fire at a slower rate or relocate to an alternate firing position.
   3-9. Tests have shown that even in warmer temperatures, a fog develops around the gun from hot gases and
   the breath of the gunner, making it difficult to observe the strike of a round. For crew-served weapons, the
   assistant gunner may need to take up a position further left or right to help with adjustments. For individual
   weapons, frequent changes in position may be required.

BREAKAGE AND MALFUNCTIONS
     3-11. Extreme cold causes metal and plastic to become more brittle than it is at warmer temperatures.
     Breakage generally occurs early when a cold weapon is fired; the metal is warming and rapid, unequal
     expansion of the parts is occurring. In this situation, begin firing small arms at a slow rate of fire, if the
     tactical situation permits. This greatly reduces the likelihood of weapon malfunction.
     3-12. After a weapon has been fired, the heat generated by firing can cause any snow or ice it comes into
     contact with to melt. This water then refreezes, potentially causing the weapon to malfunction. Applying a
     de-icer can help thaw the weapon and keep it working properly. Cycle the weapon periodically. For this
     reason, unit armorers need to carry extra parts.
CREW-SERVED WEAPONS
     3-13. Crew-served weapons require some type of firing base or platform. Emplacement of a weapon on snow,
     ice, or frozen ground may result in breakage, inaccuracy due to sinking, or the inability to absorb shock.
     Emplacements relating to particular weapons are discussed later in this chapter.
     3-14. As the temperature drops, so does muzzle velocity and the range of projectiles. This is due to a change
     in internal and external ballistics:
          • Internal ballistics occur inside the weapon; the burning rate of propellant decreases, decreasing the
               rate of gas expansion and speed the projectile moves down the barrel.
          • External ballistics occur after the projectile leaves the muzzle:
                Decreased muzzle velocity reduces the stability of the projectile as it leaves the muzzle,
                    possibly causing the projectile to tumble.
                At longer ranges, this further reduces velocity and accuracy.
                Cold air is denser than warm air which can create increased drag on the projectile, further
                    decreasing the range.
AUTOMATIC WEAPONS
     3-15. Automatic weapons have a high rate of breakage and malfunction due to cold weather. Especially
     affected are the sear and bolt parts. Gun crews carry extra parts of this type. One common malfunction is
     short recoil where the bolt does not recoil fully to the rear. A second malfunction is caused by the freezing
     and hardening of buffers. This causes great shock and rapid recoil, increasing the cyclic rate and potentially
     causing parts to break.
     3-16. All internal components and friction surfaces of machine guns should be coated with LAW. These
     small arms should be fired cold and dry if LAW or CLP is not available. Firing should begin slowly at first
     to allow the weapon to warm; short two-round or three-round bursts at quick intervals are sufficient until the
     weapon components warm. Machine guns should be test-fired in cold weather prior to combat deployment.
     Ammunition is transported enclosed in drums or cans to prevent snow fouling. It should be kept at the same
     temperature as the weapon. All weapons should be re-zeroed in extreme cold.

SPECIFIC WEAPON PROBLEMS
    3-17. Although any number of reasons factor into a weapons failure, cold-weather situations can present a
    higher degree of malfunction. The following information provides specific considerations for the pistol; rifle
    and carbine; light, heavy, and grenade machine guns; M136 (AT-4); M220-series tube launched, optically
    tracked wire guided (TOW) tripod launcher; MANPADS (Stinger); FMG-148 Javelin; fragmentation
    grenades; and C-4 weaponry when used in cold-weather climates.

Pistol
    3-18. The pistol is affected by condensation more than other weapon because it is most often carried
    by personnel whose duties require them to frequently enter and exit heated shelters and vehicles. Freezing
    generally occurs around the slide and magazine well. Moving parts breakage is rare, but the extractor
    and firing pin are vulnerable.

Rifle and Carbine
    3-19. Little breakage occurs in the individual rifle/carbine if the weapon is fired at a slow rate until it warms.
    Breakage usually occurs around the extractor, ejector, and firing pin. Condensation in the buffer tube
    decreases its shock-absorbing ability, which may result in breakage or reduced recoil. This can result in the
    omission of the cocking step in the cycle of operation. Wipe out the buffer tube frequently to remove
    condensation and reduce the chance of weapon malfunction. When wearing mittens or bulky hand gear, open
    the trigger guard for firing. Keep the trigger guard closed but unlatched for safety when not in use.
    3-20. Re-zero the rifle/carbine when deploying from temperate to cold environments. Cold temperatures may
    cause a decrease in the burning rate of propellants, which can significantly change projectile trajectories. In
    effect, this nullifies the zero of the weapon. Altitude also has an effect on the weapon system. When a
    significant change in altitude occurs, the weapon should be re-zeroed.

Light Machine Gun
    3-21. The light machine gun has a high rate of breakage due to the large number of moving parts. Armorers
    should carry plenty of spare parts, especially those most prone to failure (firing pins, extractors, feed pawls,
    and other parts). Other things to consider when using the light machine gun is—
        • The safety selector switch is extremely difficult to operate when the weapon is cold.
        • Buffer group assemblies are affected in the same manner as the rifle/carbine:
              A common malfunction is short recoil (the bolt does not recoil fully to the rear) which occurs
                  early in firing.
              Apply immediate action procedures until the metal warms.
        • When changing barrels, do not place a hot barrel in the snow:
              The rapidly cooling barrel may warp and cause condensation to freeze in the barrel.
              Use a 30-inch by 30-inch square of doubled heavy cotton canvas to lay spare barrels on.
              The cotton canvas keeps the barrel from melting into the snow and possibly warping.
              This cotton canvas should be kept in or attached to the spare barrel bag.
              Nylon canvas melts when in contact with a hot barrel.
        • Ammunition protection is essential:
              Unprotected belts are a sure way to introduce ice into the weapon when firing.
              Another square of cotton cloth can serve as a place to lay ammunition for the medium/light
                  machine gun.
              Another solution is to link two belts together, S-feed into the original steel canister, and set
                  them next to the gun on a packed snow platform.
              Both cotton cloth and linking belts methods keep snow from being introduced into the weapon.
        • Semipermanent platforms may be constructed by attaching ski pole baskets or snowshoes
             to the bipod.
        • A rubber floor tile (similar to tiles in gym weight rooms and can be found in any home improvement
             store) is a perfectly-sized firing platform for the medium/light machine gun bipod.

Grenade Machine Gun
     3-25. The grenade machine gun uses lubricant, weapon, semifluid or grease, molybdenum disulfide lubricant
     down to –25°F (–31.6°C). Below that, use LAW.
     3-26. Use cloth covers rather than plastic to protect the weapon from the elements; plastic or rubberized
     covers can become stiff or brittle in the cold. This can result in difficulty removing them (especially when in
     a hurry) or damage to the cover. Use the same techniques for the tripod as the M2/M240.
     3-27. When working with mortars, hand protection is always worn (contact gloves). The gloves are worn
     close to the skin. When ammunition is dropped into the tube, a vacuum occurs that can suck a loose glove
     into the tube, creating a hazardous situation.
     3-28. Breathing on sights or the mortar ballistic computer (MBC) causes equipment to fog and
     freeze. When not firing the weapon, muzzle and sight covers should be used to prevent snow and ice
     from entering the tube.
     3-29. The MBC is programmed to accept temperatures down to –50°F (–45.5°C). This automatically
     compensates for cold-induced slow burning charges when computing firing data. The MBC is not
     programmed for temperature inputs colder than –50°F (–45.5°C).
     3-30. Aiming stakes become loose when placed in snow. Utilize sandbags or an anchoring device to
     keep them in place once set. Base plates become brittle when exposed to extreme cold. With the decreased
     ability of frozen ground to absorb shock, base plates are more prone to breakage than normal. Base plates
     are dug in, if possible, to prevent the base plate from skipping. Shock absorbing materials such as
     spruce branches, sandbags, even ice chips, or soil should be used for absorbing recoil during firing, but not
     to an extent that allows the base plate to bounce out of the dug-in hole. Demolitions may be used to prepare
     a firing position quickly: a 1.25-pound (0.55 kilogram [kg] block of C-4 works well). After emplacement,
     base plates can be hard to remove.
     3-31. Swab bores thoroughly after each mission to remove any excess propellants. Variable time
     fuzes are not preferred due to the severe dampening effects of snow: airbursts are preferred. Malfunctions
     occur in direct proportion to the severity of the weather. The rubber tube cover may harden and become
     extremely difficult to remove as temperatures fall. When firing in drop mode, expect a greater number of
     misfires. Using the trigger corrects this.

M136 (AT-4)

     makes sights more difficult to release from their covers.

C-4
      3-37. With demolitions, C-4 hardens, making it difficult to insert blasting caps. In extremely cold
      conditions, C-4 has shattered from the blasting cap rather than detonating. The detonation cord, time
      fuze, and shock tube become brittle, more difficult to tie, and may break. The time fuze tends to retain
      its curl and breaks when unrolled. Condensation contributes to the occurrence of misfires. Hangfire and
      misfire waiting times should be doubled.
AMMUNITION
      3-38. Small arms ammunition should be kept at the same temperature as the weapon. Ammunition clips and
      magazines are cleaned of oil and preservative and checked often. All ice, snow, and condensation are
      removed. Ammunition boxes are kept closed in order to keep out snow and ice.
      3-39. Stored ammunition should be kept in packed boxes; raised off the surface by placing them on boughs,
      cardboard, or other similar material; and covered to protect them from the snow. Ammunition is well marked
      for easy location and identification in case of later snow cover.
      3-40. Ammunition resupply may become restricted. Everyone is aware of the necessity for ammunition
      economy and fire discipline. Loaded clips, magazines, or single rounds dropped in the snow are quickly lost.
      Handling ammunition carefully is essential. Never waste ammunition. Make every round count.

EATING AND DRINKING IN A COLD ENVIRONMENT
   4-1. Most of what humans eat and drink is used to maintain body heat, while only a small portion is used to
   produce energy for physical work. Adequate caloric intake in cold-weather operations is essential. About
   4,600 calories per day are necessary for Soldier’s performing physically demanding work in this type of
   environment. Efficiency drops rapidly if this level is not maintained.
   4-2. The body loses liquid quickly in extremely cold conditions, regardless of how carefully clothing is
   adjusted and ventilated. The exertion of moving on foot, preparing bivouac sites and defenses in the snow,
   and other manual tasks, take a toll with sweat and loss of moisture due to breathing. These liquids are
   regularly replaced, preferably by hot drinks that provide extra calories when they contain sugar.
RATIONS
   4-3. The standard operational ration is the meal, ready to eat (MRE). One ration provides about 1,300
   calories. Four MREs per day are needed to meet the required calories. The downside to this ration is that
   most of its components freeze.
   4-4. The meal, cold weather (MCW) has many pocket items that should be readily accessible throughout
   the day. This ration provides about 1,450 calories per bag. The modular operational ration enhancement
   contains all pocket items to supplement additional calories throughout the day. Each packet provides
   1,100 calories. The unitized group ration series of rations has an Arctic supplement component that provides
   an additional 914 calories. The MCW is a restricted ration meant for short durations. (More information
   can be found in AR 40-25.)
LIQUIDS
   4-5. Insensible and sensible fluid loss refers to two types of ways the body loses liquid through various
   bodily functions. Insensible fluid loss cannot be perceived or measured directly. Sensible loss can be
   perceived and measured.

Insensible and Sensible Fluid Loss
   4-6. Insensible fluid loss is evaporation by breathing and perspiration. This is continuous and can account for
   approximately 1.5 to 2.25 quarts (approximately 1.4 to 2.1 liters) of moisture loss per day. The human body
   is constantly giving off moisture through the skin. To illustrate this on a small scale, tape a clear plastic bag
   to the hand. The cloudiness that forms is moisture. That loss happens over the entire skin area.
   4-7. Sensible fluid loss is what can be seen leaving the body, such as urine or sweat. This loss varies
    based on temperature, humidity, and physical exertion. Soldiers need approximately 3.75 to 5.25 quarts
    (approximately 3.5 to 5 liters) of water per day. This includes the water present in rations.

Maintaining Rations
   4-8. One MRE has close to 8 ounces (approximately .25 liters) of liquid per ration. One MCW has close to
   zero water in it because MCWs are freeze-dried and require a little more than 1 quart (approximately 1 liter)
   of water to reconstitute the entrée and beverages. Assuming each Soldier consumes four MCWs a day, one
   squad needs at least 8 gallons (approximately 30.3 liters) of water per day. At the high end, it is almost
   12 gallons (approximately 45.5 liters) per squad. To visualize this, a company needs one high mobility
   multipurpose wheeled vehicle for carrying water cans.

Maintaining Adequate Water Supplies
     4-9. While liquid water from a creek is always the best option, gather snow or ice chunks to make water when
     necessary. Ice produces more water in less time than snow. When melting snow, first put a small amount into
     the cooking pot and melt it, then more can be added. Continue this process until there is enough water. It is
     purified by bringing it to a rolling boil. When heating water, use all available stoves since this is a very
     time-consuming operation. Areas designated for ice or snow that are to be used for water are situated far
     upwind and uphill of the latrine and garbage disposal sites.
     4-10. Before going to bed, ensure that stoves are filled with fuel and enough water is prepared for melting.
     The fire watch should have the additional duty of continuously melting snow or ice to ensure the squad has
     water for the next day. After compiling enough snow, ice, or water—
         • Place the two big pots from the squad cook sets on the SHA full of snow and ice.
         • Allow the snow or ice to melt (or at least become slushy).
         • Transfer these pots to the squad stoves to boil while the two small pots are used to produce the initial
              melt on the SHA.

             Note. The two initial pots of boiling water are primarily used to sanitize the pots. The water can
             be used for hand washing but do not drink it.

         •     Keep rotating the pots between the SHA and squad stoves until enough water is made.
         •     Provide everyone with a hot drink and hot food (a major priority) by:
                Filling canteens on the FLC before filling the 5-gallon water can in the ahkio.
                This can remain in the tent until it is time to pack and move.
                If possible, the water should be hot to prevent freezing.
     4-11. Keep Soldiers full by continually consuming and replacing water. Pockets are used for storing grazing
     food that is also eaten continuously throughout the day. In addition to checking for cold-weather injury and
     overall health, leaders check the full status of their Soldiers. Additional considerations are—
         • Hot food is easier to swallow than cold food.
         • Hot beverages are morale builders and are used to increase fluid intake.
         • Utilize insulated bottles.
         • If using MREs, carry the next meal entrée against the body to thaw.
         • Calorie intake should be constant:
               Military rations are designed to be portable.
               MREs, MCWs, and modular operational ration enhancements are primarily pocket food
                    requiring little preparation.
         • Try for unitized group rations, if the mission allows.

TRENCH FOOT OR IMMERSION FOOT
   4-15. Trench foot and immersion foot are injuries caused by prolonged exposure of skin in cold or wet
   conditions. In extreme cases, the skin dies, and amputation of the foot or leg may be necessary. The following
   helps prevent trench foot or immersion foot:
       • Change into dry socks at least twice daily.
       • Dry and massage feet regularly with foot powder.
       • Wear VB boots.
       • Weari dry leather boots whenever possible, if worn.
   4-16. In the early stages of trench or immersion foot, feet and toes are cold, numb, and difficult
   to move. Walking becomes challenging, feet swell and become painful, and skin appears wrinkled
   (like a prune). In the early stages, keep feet dry, clean, and exposed to the air. In later stages, evacuate
   the victim to a medical facility.
CONSTIPATION AND DIARRHEA
   4-17. At very cold temperatures, people tend to put off the natural urge to have a bowel movement, which
   can easily result in constipation. Some common symptoms are stomach cramps, dizziness, and headaches. If
   symptoms persist, get medical help. The following helps prevent constipation:
       • Eating regularly, and including fresh or canned fruit.
       • Drinking plenty of liquids.
   4-18. Diarrhea is another problem that can become serious. If sickness does occur, seek medical
   attention and redouble efforts to keep it from spreading to others via hand-to-hand contact. Ways of
   preventing diarrhea include—
        • Washing hands frequently.
        • Ensuring food and water originate from sanitary sources.

     4-27. During cold-weather training, it is possible to experience heat cramps or other injuries typically

Heat Cramps
     4-28. Heat cramps are caused by an excessive loss of salt in the body. The loss of salt does not allow the
     muscles to relax, causing spasms. After prolonged physical activity in a wet-cold environment, the following
     signs and symptoms of heat cramps could appear in the arms, legs, or stomach:
         • Painful muscle cramps and spasms, usually in the legs and abdomen.
         • Heavy sweating.

   4-30. It is also possible to experience heat exhaustion in a wet-cold-weather environment when undergoing

THE EFFECTS OF WIND CHILL
   4-32. Wind chill is the combination of wind and low temperatures creating a condition known as wind chill.
   For example, with calm wind and a temperature of –20°F (–28.8°C) there is little danger of wind chill. If the
   temperature is –20°F (–28.8°C) and wind is blowing at 20 miles per hour (mph) (32.1 kilometers per hour
   [kph]), the equivalent chill temperature is –63°F (–52.7°C). Under these conditions there is increased danger.
   Exposed skin can freeze within 6 minutes. Wind is also created when walking, running, or skiing. Walking
   creates a wind speed of about 5 mph (8 kph) and running 10 mph (16 kph). Wind is also created by moving
   vehicles, aircraft propellers, and rotors.
   4-33. The wind-chill chart (see table 4-1 on page 52) shows how wind and temperature combine to create
   wind chill. Cold injury can disable a Soldier like an enemy bullet. To use the wind chill chart, get the ambient
   air temperature, estimate the wind speed, and then multiply by 1.5. This is necessary to make the true wind
   speed at 33 feet (10 meters), the world meteorological standard for measuring wind speed. Find these
   numbers on the chart, and where they intersect is the wind chill.

HYPOTHERMIA
     4-34. Hypothermia occurs when the core body temperature falls below 95ºF (35ºC). Hypothermia
     is characterized as mild, moderate, or severe. Mild hypothermia occurs when the core body temperature is
     between 90° (32.2ºC) and 95ºF (35ºC). Moderate hypothermia occurs at a core body temperature
     of 80° to 89ºF (26.6° to 31.6ºC). Severe hypothermia exists when the core body temperature falls
     below 80ºF (26.6°C). Rectal temperature measurement is the only way to determine an accurate core body
     temperature. As it is unlikely this method can safely be used in the field, obvious signs and symptoms
     are used to make a diagnosis. All levels of hypothermia are potentially life-threatening health emergencies
     requiring immediate care in a medical facility.

Levels of Hypothermia
     4-35. As the core body temperature begins to fall, shivering becomes the most noticeable symptom. Shivering
     alone is not a sign of hypothermia, but it does indicate that the body is having a problem with the cold. Mild
     hypothermia symptoms include—
         • Shivering.
         • Lack of sound judgment; confusion, apathy, and mild stupidity.
         • Pale, cool skin.
         • Increased heart and respiratory rates.
         • The umbles: fumbles, stumbles, tumbles, grumbles, mumbles:
                Fine motor skills decrease (fumbling with tasks that require manual dexterity such as
                   manipulating a rifle).
                Next, the gross motor skills start slipping (stumbling when just walking).
                Finally, intellectual and personality changes occur, including grumbling and incoherent
                   mumbling.

   4-36. Moderate hypothermia symptoms include—
       • Uncontrollable shivering.
       • Worsening the umbles.
       • Increased confusion.

       • Cold and pale skin.
   4-37. Severe hypothermia symptoms include—
       • Cessation of shivering.

       • Replace damp clothing with dry clothing.
       • Add a windproof or waterproof layer and/or place the Soldier in a shelter.

       • Provide food and warm liquids.
       • Exercise mildly hypothermic patients.

              Place a poncho flat on the ground.

              Carefully remove damp and wet clothing.
              Package in a hypothermia wrap.

FROSTBITE SYMPTOMS AND TREATMENT
   4-39. Frostbite results when tissues freeze from exposure to temperatures below 32°F (5.68°C). The severity
   of frostbite injury depends upon the wind-chill factor, length of exposure time, and proper protection. Soldiers
   with a history of cold injury are more prone to frostbite. There may be a tingling, stinging, aching sensation,
   or a cramping pain. The skin first turns red and then becomes pale gray or waxy white. Frostbite classification
   ranges from superficial to deep.

Preventing Frostbite
     4-40. When each Soldier takes care of themselves, it is possible to prevent frostbite. It seldom occurs to
     people maintaining proper body heat and is commonly associated with overall body heat loss. This is the
     result of poor equipment, reduced food intake, dehydration (water loss), overtiring, injury, or a combination
     of factors. Feet, hands, ears, and exposed areas of the face are the most easily hurt by frostbite and need
     constant care. The following rules help prevent frostbite:
          • Do not wear tight boots and socks.
          • Use the buddy system to check exposed areas, especially when the wind is blowing.
          • Carry extra socks and mitten liners.
          • Do not stay still for long periods of time.
          • Use caution when cold and wind are combined.
          • Check feet during halts.
          • Consume good food and hot drinks as often as possible.

Levels of Frostbite
     4-41. Superficial frostbite appears as white, waxy, and pale in lighter skin types: red and pale in darker skin
     types. There is numbness with the skin moving over the underlying tissue. When dented, the skin rebounds.
     4-42. Deep frostbite skin discoloration presents in much the same manner as superficial frostbite. There is no
     tactile sensation at all. The Soldier might describe it as feeling wooden. Skin does not move over the
     underlying tissue and when dented, does not rebound. Skin can also feel hard to the touch. After rewarming,
     a layer of dark-colored skin may form over the affected area.

Recommended Treatment for Frostbite
     4-43. If the exposure time in the cold is short, the frostbite is probably superficial. If the exposure time is
     long, the frostbite is probably deep. The following is a recommended treatment for frostbite:
         • Move the casualty to a warm and sheltered area.
         • Do not rub the frostbitten area with snow or apply cold water soaks.
         • Do not rewarm the affected area by massaging or exposing it to open fire.
         • Rewarm the face, nose, or ears by placing hands on the frozen area.
         • Rewarm frostbitten hands by placing them under clothing and against the body.
         • Close the clothing to prevent further loss of body heat.
         • Rewarm feet by removing boots and socks, then:
                Place bare feet under clothing and against the abdomen of a buddy.
                Once the feet are warmed, put on dry socks and boots, if available.
                If the victim must wear wet socks and boots, exercise the feet by wiggling the toes.
         • Loosen tight clothing and remove jewelry.
         • Improve circulation by exercising.
         • Do not allow the victim to use alcohol or tobacco:
                Alcohol increases the loss of body heat.
                Tobacco causes blood vessels to narrow in the arms and legs.
         • Reassure the victim.
         • Protect frozen tissue from further cold or trauma.
         • Evacuate the casualty to the nearest medical treatment facility.
     4-44. Deep frostbite is most common and harmful to the feet and less common to the hands and ears. When
     deep frostbite does occur, the Soldier should be moved to a sheltered area and evacuated to a medical
     treatment facility immediately. If possible, do not allow the victim to walk if the feet are frozen, and avoid
     treating or thawing the affected area. Thawing deep frostbite in the field increases pain and invites infection,
     greater damage, and possibly gangrene. Do not try to thaw deep frostbite; let the physician do it.

DEHYDRATION
   4-45. Dehydration can cause serious physical problems and make Soldiers more susceptible to other
   difficulties such as frostbite and hypothermia. The following are suggestions to prevent dehydration:
        • The minimum daily liquid requirement for Soldiers performing demanding physical work in the
             cold is approximately 3.75 quarts (3.5 liters) per day.
        • By the time a Soldier feels thirsty, they are already dehydrated and should drink water whenever
             possible, particularly during halts.
        • Ensure that canteens are full before any type of movement and fill with hot water, if possible.
        • During periods when water is scarce, do not eat snow as it can lower body temperature and the air
             it contains can cause stomach aches:
              Diuretics such as coffee should be avoided because it passes through the body very quickly.
              Water should be the first choice.
        • Check urine in the snow for dark yellow or brown spots that indicate dehydration.
   4-46. Common symptoms of dehydration are—
       • Red skin indicating a severe condition needing immediate medical evacuation to the nearest medical
           treatment facility.
       • Lack of appetite.
       • The mouth, tongue, and throat are dry.
       • Stomach cramps and/or vomiting.
       • Headaches.
   4-47. Recommended treatment for dehydration is—
       • Keeping the victim warm.
       • Giving plenty of liquids.
       • Making the Soldier rest.

CARBON MONOXIDE POISONING
   4-48. Whenever a squad stove fire, fuel-fired heater, or internal combustion engine is used indoors, there is
   danger of carbon monoxide poisoning. Fresh air in living and working quarters is vital. Carbon monoxide is
   a deadly, odorless gas that displaces oxygen in the blood stream. To prevent carbon monoxide poisoning—
       • Ensure that stoves and lanterns are functioning properly.
       • Do not let Soldiers warm themselves by engine exhaust.
       • Always crack the windows in vehicles when a heater is in use, and do not allow anyone to sleep in
            a running vehicle.
       • Use a tent guard or shut the stove off when sleeping.
   4-49. If a Soldier is found unconscious in an enclosed shelter, carbon monoxide poisoning should
   be suspected. Common symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning are—
       • Headache, dizziness, confusion, yawning, weariness, nausea, and ringing in the ears.
       • Bright red color on lips and skin.
       • Drowsiness and sudden collapse.
   4-50. Adhere to the following for proper treatment of carbon monoxide poisoning:
       • Move the Soldier to open air.
       • Keep the victim still and warm.
       • If the Soldier is not breathing, administer mouth-to-mouth resuscitation.
       • Administer cardiopulmonary resuscitation, if necessary.
       • Immediately evacuate the Soldier to the nearest medical treatment facility.
       • Immediately administer oxygen, if available.

ACCOMPLISHING THE MISSION
   5-1. The essential requirements for survival and mission accomplishment in cold regions are warmth, food,
   water, and shelter. Soldiers are provided with tents that can be erected quickly. However, in the confusion of
   combat, tents can be lost or destroyed, and Soldiers can become separated from their ahkio groups.
   5-2. When this occurs, timber and snow, which are usually available, are used to make shelter. Additionally,
   a detailed explanation of the bivouac routine should be contained in the unit SOP.
BIVOUAC SITES
   5-3. Bivouac sites, other than those that are more permanent in nature in the rear areas, are tactical or
   designated to provide living accommodations within a defended position. The tactical bivouac (patrol base)
   is usually not occupied for more than one night, is established at or after last light, and needs little preparation.
   It is organized for a rapid and disciplined response to enemy action.
   5-4. The defensive position is likely to be occupied for longer periods and is established accordingly. The
   layout and placement of tents and shelters is coordinated within the defense, and according to the unit SOP.
   5-5. The selection of bivouac areas is important and needs careful consideration. The challenge of area
   selection varies with the tactical situation, weather conditions, and terrain. The bivouac area is strategically
   located according to the principles of security and defense. There is concealment from air and ground
   observation, and shelter from the wind.

Forests
   5-6. Forested areas pose few challenges in comparison to areas that are located above the tree line. In
   selecting areas, ensure that local camouflage materials are available for use with the snow camouflage nets.
   Forests, especially those where the vegetation is spruce or pine, provide excellent patrol bases. Material for
   camouflage, firewood, and construction of shelters and fighting positions is abundant.
   5-7. Spruce and pine forests are preferable for a variety of reasons; one being that these types of trees
   generally grow on well-drained soil. Another reason is they retain their needles year-round, offering better
   concealment and protection from wind and inclement weather than deciduous trees that lose their leaves
   during the winter months. The thick canopy helps disperse smoke from heater stacks and dissipate any ice
   fog that may form in the extreme cold.

Frozen Swamps and Open Country (Tundra)
   5-8. Firmly frozen swampy areas (muskeg), especially those that are covered with trees and brush, may also
   offer a good location to establish a patrol base. Muskeg areas often have very spindly spruce trees that provide
   virtually no cover, concealment, or protection from the wind.
   5-9. Thickly iced streams and rivers located nearby can offer excellent routes for resupply, as well as routes
   for mounted and dismounted movement. It is generally difficult to construct dug-in fighting positions, forcing
   above-ground positions in these areas. Additionally, leaders should remember that a sudden thaw could leave
   everyone wallowing in a pool of mud.
   5-10. Open country (tundra) presents difficulties such as poor cover and concealment, exposure to high
   winds, and drifting snow. (See figure 5-1 on page 59.) If establishing a patrol base in open areas, tents should
   be pitched (or shelters constructed) in the lee of natural windbreaks such as depressions or the downwind
   side of eskers, ridges, and hills. Where no natural windbreaks exist, dig tents into the snow as deeply as
   possible or construct windbreaks with snow or blocks of snow. (See figure 5-2 on page 59.)

Streams and Areas Above the Tree Line
     5-11. Look for streams in order to lower the silhouette of tents and provide a fighting cover. Sometimes small
     streams are present that have mature trees on the banks. A skilled enemy understands the value of these areas
     and has them under observation.
     5-12. Above the tree line, mountainous terrain provides little shelter from observation or the elements. During
     high winds, lee slopes may offer shelter. However, they can also increase exposure to avalanche hazards if
     wind-driven snow is deposited on lee slopes. If the slope is steep enough, the snow eventually slides. In snow-
     covered mountains or when snowfall is imminent, always examine a slope’s potential for an avalanche prior
     to establishing a bivouac upon it.

Valleys and Lakes
     5-13. Areas lying in the bottom of deep valleys and large lakes should be avoided due to masses of cold air
     settling on low ground (also known as a temperature inversion). During windless conditions, temperature
     inversions can result in a low-lying patrol base producing its own cloud of ice fog due to tent heating. This
     can potentially reveal the location of the site to enemy observers, especially if the enemy is located at a higher
     vantage point than the patrol base.
     5-14. If low-lying areas are used, look for large mature spruce trees to help mask tent heating signatures.
     Temperature inversions can usually be overcome by ascending to local high ground. Also, large rivers tend
     to be funnels for wind. Anchoring tents can be accomplished by using ice screws. Additionally, lakes can be
     useful for helicopter landing zones.

ORGANIZATION OF THE TENT INTERIOR
     5-50. Tents are just large enough to provide adequate sleeping space for Soldiers, along with a small area for
     cooking, washing, and performing the duties of the fire guard. Orderly and disciplined arrangements are a
     necessity in such cramped circumstances. The following procedures are established to enhance the comfort,
     safety, and operational effectiveness of personnel required to live in tents. Many of these procedures are also
     readily adaptable to life in improvised shelters.
     5-51. Only the minimum required individual clothing, equipment, and rations are allowed inside the tent.
     Generally, Soldiers bring their canteens, daily rations, insulated sleeping mat, sleeping bag, a small sharp
     knife, and the clothing required when leaving the tent are the items needed. Damp clothing and equipment
     may be brought inside to be hung up to dry but once dry, these items should be placed in the rucksack outside
     the tent. Prior to every stand-to, all items are packed into the rucksack so that essential equipment for survival
     is available if the unit is forced to execute a rapid withdrawal under pressure.

Storage Safety Inside the Tent
     5-52. All Soldiers living in a heated tent should have a knife on hand at all times, especially while sleeping.
     If a tent fire occurs, it can take as little as 10 seconds for the fire to completely engulf the shelter, and less
     than a minute to destroy it. If Soldiers are asleep with the zipper of their sleeping bag closed, they may not
     have enough time to unzip the bag. If they cannot do this due to lack of time or a jammed zipper, they attempt
     to roll out from under the wall of the tent while still in their bag. This can be difficult, especially for someone
     disoriented and shocked into wakefulness. With a small, sharp knife immediately at hand, the Soldier can cut
     their way out of the sleeping bag and through the wall of the tent. This single item of equipment may end up
     being the difference between life and death.
     5-53. As stated above, the tent group leader is responsible for allocating personal space within the tent.
     When doing so, the leader takes into consideration the duty roster and the need for an orderly exit in the event
     of an emergency.

   5-60. When living in a close environment such as a tent or improvised shelter, the highest possible standards
   of sanitation and personal hygiene are maintained. Failure to enforce or practice good sanitation and hygiene
   can expose everyone to sickness and disease. The conditions that Soldiers live in during cold weather
   creates an exceptionally good opportunity for sickness to run rampant through the unit. Soldiers are more
   susceptible to becoming a cold-weather casualty if they do not keep everything clean. Remember the C in

HEATING AT NIGHT
   5-61. The tactical situation, weather, and Soldiers’ level of training and experience is considered
   when determining whether or not to operate heaters throughout the night. The major disadvantage of
   heating shelters all night long (other than increased fuel requirements) is the obvious thermal signature of
   a heated shelter in the middle of a cold environment and the necessity for additional Soldiers losing sleep
   to perform duty as a fire guard.
   5-62. Some advantages of keeping the stove burning are that Soldiers lose less body heat and conserve more
   energy while sleeping, improving performance the following day. Also, troops can be more ready to react to
   a threat by sleeping fully clothed on top of their sleeping bag (or just their insulating pad).
   5-63. Soldiers sleeping in heated shelters have the opportunity to dry wet clothing in the tent while they
   sleep. Wet clothes should not be dried in sleeping bags when resting. However, slightly damp clothing
   can dry in the bag.

WOOD CUTTING AREAS
     5-69. Wood cutting areas for fighting positions, improvised shelters, or wood burning heaters (if liquid fuel
     in not available) should be known by all Soldiers in the unit to prevent many areas from being cut down.
     These are places where activity is not likely and well behind the unit’s defensive area, or in a decoy bivouac
     site. Dead spruce, pine, or the small limbs and branches under a live canopy of trees make the best firewood.
     Standing deadwood burns best but hardwood burns longest.
     5-70. In an Arctic and subarctic environment, true hardwood is rare. Of the trees available, birch, tamarack,
     and spruce are the hardest woods. Burn the upper parts of dead trees during daylight because they
     give off lighter colored smoke. The lower parts of the trees contain resin and tar which burn well
     but make more and darker smoke. In daylight, this allows Soldiers positions to be easily identified by air and
     ground observation. Be aware that burning wood for heat produces a smell signature that can be detected
     at great distances. For fighting positions, use live trees 6 to 8 inches in diameter to provide the best
     resistance to enemy fire.

     to scavenge. Ravens open trash bags and can even peck their way into ration cases in a short period of time.

MOVING OVER SNOW
   6-1. Snow affects movement in many different ways. Heavy snow cover slows movement, either
   cross-country or on roads. Snow cover also blankets many terrain features and hides obstacles. The effect of
   snow on movement varies greatly with its depth and physical characteristics.
   6-2. Soldiers on foot cannot travel well over flat terrain or roads when snow is deeper than approximately
   1 foot (30 cm). However, when snow cover is deeper, using skis or snowshoes improves movement. (See
   table 6-1.) Flotation on snow is equally as important as the weapon. Do not allow Soldiers to become
   separated from their flotation. Arriving at the destination soaked in sweat is a failure. Set a pace that generates
   just enough heat to be comfortable.

     6-5. Army snowshoes consist of a hard plastic deck with steel crampons and bars to aid with traction. They
     are approximately 22 inches long and 8 inches wide (approximately 56-cm long and 20.3-cm wide) without
     flotation tails, and weigh less than 4 pounds (1.8 kg). Other characteristics of this snowshoe include the—
          • Shovels (1): the upturned front portion of the snowshoe is designed to help it ride toward the surface
               of the snow.
          • Windows (2): the opening of the snowshoe that allows the toe of the boot to pivot for added traction,
               giving a natural foot movement while walking.
          • Decks (3): this is the middle, flat portion of the snowshoe.
          • Bindings (4): secure the snowshoe to the boot.

          • Tails (6): this is the elongated rear of the snowshoe designed so the snowshoe tracks in a straight
               line while walking.
          • Flotation tail(s) (7): are detachable additional decking at the rear of the snowshoe to provide greater
               flotation in powder snow.
          • Crampons (8): are the metal structure under the deck, directly under the boot, that aids in traction
               on ice and hard-packed snow (as seen from the underside of the snowshoe.)
          • Steel bars (9): are the serrated edges along both sides of the shoe that aids with traction on ice and
               hard-packed snow (as seen from the underside of the snowshoe).

   6-6. To properly fit the military snowshoe:
        • Lay the snowshoes on the ground with the running end of the heel strap to the outside of each foot.
        • Kneel down and place one boot underneath the toe straps until the front of the boot is one-half to
            two-thirds of the way into the window.
        • Starting with the front toe strap, tighten each strap until it is snug against the boot, but take care not
            to cut off circulation or overtighten to the point of breaking the strap.
        • Secure the heel strap to the boot just above the heel lug so that the running end of the heel strap is
            on the outside of the boot to prevent tripping.
        • Repeat with the other snowshoe.

THE CHALLENGE OF LAND NAVIGATION
     6-26. In some cold-weather environments, accurate up-to-date maps may not be available, and landmarks
     can be few or nonexistent. When the terrain is snow-covered, one piece of ground looks like the other.
     Whatever the circumstances, find a way—if not by map reading, then by some other means of navigation.
     6-27. Only poor roads may exist in some areas. These are of little use in cross-country navigation. If
     large-scale maps exist, they may be unreliable. Long nights, falling or blowing snow, and fog limits visibility
     in winter. Tracks and landmarks may be erased by heavy snowfall. Accurately identify landmarks via ground
     and air observation to prevent mistakes. Conditions of daylight are different from those found in more
     temperate regions. In winter, there are long nights and short days. The low angle of the sun can temporarily
     hinder visibility.
     6-28. Magnetic disturbances may be common, making compass readings unreliable. Use several compasses
     at the same time during movements. Magnetic declinations can be extreme and vary considerably at close
     points. Many small lakes, ponds, and creeks are found that are not on the map. The size and location of
     waterways can vary from year to year and from season to season. They cannot be relied on to pinpoint a
     position. However, large lakes and rivers can be used as important aids to navigation and movement. The
     Global Positioning System (GPS) may be unreliable as batteries are quickly drained due to the extreme cold
     and high latitudes. (See TC 3-25.26 for more information.)

            Note. Do not rely on one form of navigation when moving in extremely cold weather.

DISTANCE AND DIRECTION
     6-29. When traveling from one point to another, it is necessary to know the distance between the two
     locations before starting out, then plot the journey accordingly. In temperate zones, pacing is the simplest
     method of measuring distance. In cold-weather areas, snow cover and the use of skis and snowshoes make
     pacing a challenge. Repeatedly practice this type of pacing. Assign several Soldiers within the unit the job of
     tracking their pace count, which is then averaged.
     6-30. The best method for accurate ground measurement is for two Soldiers to use a piece of salvage field
     wire or 50-meter (approximately 54.5-yard) long parachute cord. The lead Soldier marches off in the desired
     direction, trailing the wire behind. The second Soldier signals the first by jerking the wire when the first has

   reached 50 meters (approximately 54.5-yards) and then moves forward to the lead Soldier’s position. The

DETERMINING DIRECTION
   6-32. Using the compass is the most common way to determine direction. However, the magnetic field
   becomes weaker when the magnetic pole becomes closer.
   6-33. Compasses can easily be affected by other objects. It is important to make sure that the compass is not
   compromised by iron fragments in the ground, vehicles, or metal objects on the Soldier. As in the pace count,
   several Soldiers within the unit should have their compass out, monitoring the direction.

Navigation with the Sun and Stars
   6-34. During daylight hours, there are two ways to determine direction by the sun. At noon in the northern
   hemisphere, shadows fall to the north of objects. In the southern hemisphere, shadows fall to the south of
   objects. Estimate direction by using a watch and the sun.
   6-35. In northern latitudes, the hour hand is pointed toward the sun. An imaginary line halfway between the
   hour hand and 1200 points to the south. In southern latitudes, the procedure is different. Point the 1200 toward
   the sun. An imaginary line halfway between the hour hand and 1200 points to the north.
   6-36. The North Star can be used to determine true north. (See figure 6-8.) This star is easily found
   by its position relative to the Big Dipper. In latitudes under 70 degrees when traveling north, the North Star
   makes a good steering mark since its bearing is usually only 1 degree from true north and is never more
   than 2 1/2 degrees away. In higher latitudes above 70 degrees, the North Star is too high in the sky to
   indicate good direction.

Navigation Tips
   6-42. The navigator keeps the compass warm to speed up recording the position. A steering mark is an object
   or terrain feature on the line of azimuth the navigator can get a bearing on. If no steering mark exists, another
   Soldier can be used instead. The team using the pace count cord (as previously described) can be used. The
   navigator sends the other Soldier out on a bearing for a distance. This Soldier then stops and faces about. The
   lead navigator directs them left or right by hand and arm signal, or via radio. The unit then moves to that
   location. This is used in conjunction with the pace count cord in conditions of diminished visibility, wide
   open flat areas, and very dense forested areas where terrain features cannot be seen. These methods slow the
   unit to a crawl and can result in cold injuries if precautions are not taken.
   6-43. When visibility is poor, only close-in steering marks are available. Under these conditions, try to pick
   up steering marks along the correct azimuth. This can be done accurately only when the route to each steering
   mark follows a straight line. Take compass checks often to ensure that the correct azimuth is being followed.
   Set the compass for a night course. Depending on the area of the world, other signs can be used. For example,
   in the barren and semi-barren lands of eastern Canada, snowdrifts point to the northwest and windblown trees
   to the southeast due to prevailing winds. In Scandinavia, scratches on rocks and cliffs caused by the glacial
   period (ice age) run northwest to southeast.

Tundra
   6-46. In tundra areas of the far north there are very few forested areas. Walking through areas with knee-high
   brush conceals trails. Areas where large herds of moving caribou can also conceal tracks. River corridors
   may have some mature trees to help with concealment, but further north these dwindle. Assume that the
   enemy knows the locations of these tree lines and has them under observation. Relying on low ground such
   as creek beds to mask movements can help conceal movement.

Hills and Mountains
   6-47. When negotiating hills or mountains, use gentle traverses to ascend or descend. This makes it less
   fatiguing on Soldiers so they can fight after reaching the objective. As far as avalanche prone slopes are
   concerned, avoid them at all costs. Very specialized training is required to even come close to negotiating
   them safely. Some warning signs to be aware of in snow-covered mountainous terrain are taught in the
   stationery avalanche awareness class.

Water Routes
   6-48. Water routes are generally excellent for navigation. They can be superb avenues for movement after
   freeze-up. Very detailed reconnaissance is required before attempting to move on water routes. When on
   water routes, treat them as an open area and stay close to the shore. This helps to conceal Soldiers and tracks.
   Overflow is a condition common to most bodies of water where the water flows onto the ice. General rules
   for selecting routes on ice include—
        • The outside bends of rivers generally have thinner ice due to the faster current.
        • Edges of lakes generally have thicker ice as water freezes from outside to inside and top to bottom.
        • Inlets, outlets, converging channels, and tributaries are high flow areas prone to thin ice.
        • The ice in the middle of lakes and rivers tends to be thinner and the downwind side of lakes tend
             to have thicker ice.

Water Route Reconnaissance
     6-50. After a tentative route is selected, an ice check is necessary. At least two Soldiers should do this.
     Equipment consists of a 25-meter (approximately 27 yard) rope, an auger or heavy ice chipper, and a
     measuring stick with an angled end. Both Soldiers wear personal flotation devices. If the tactical situation
     permits, fighting loads should be dropped or at least lightened to just a weapon. Gas-powered augers are
     faster but are loud and require two-stroke fuel. Tactically, hand-powered augers are best. Chippers and axes
     are excruciatingly slow. To reconnaissance in cold weather—
          • The lead Soldier ties off to the end of the rope with a bowline just under the armpits:
               If the rope is around the waist there is the possibility of being folded in half by a current and
                    having the head submerged.
               The other Soldier keeps the rope fairly taut.
          • The lead carefully moves onto the ice no more than 10 meters (approximately 11 yards) from the
              shore, 1 to 2 meters (3.28 to 6.56 feet) downstream from the route (to prevent weakening the route
              for heavier loads) and drills the first hole:
               Take note of the amount of resistance against the auger.
               The water should flow up out of the hole and be approximately 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) below
                    the surface of the ice.

Night Movements
   6-51. Leaders manage movements during periods of limited visibility to minimize the likelihood of breaks
   in contact and reduce the amount of time required to regain contact. In temperate regions, this is usually an
   annoyance that slows movement. In the cold-weather environment, long halts can produce cold-weather
   injuries. For this reason, the route should follow the easiest terrain possible.
   6-52. The route should also be well marked, and guides placed where appropriate. Maximize the use of
   reconnaissance teams, well-defined travel lanes, and checkpoints. Reconnaissance teams may also be tasked
   to put up heated shelters along longer routes.
TRAIL BREAKING PROCEDURES
   6-53. Roads and trails depicted on maps or imagery may be completely obscured by heavy snowfall or
   drifting. A unit can completely miss these features if not careful. Mitigate by—
        • Knowing the exact location at all times.
        • Plotting azimuths to cross or join roads and trails at easy to identify points.
        • Maintaining an accurate log of distance travelled to know when there should be crossing, or a joining
             of roads or trails.
        • Looking at the tops of the trees in forested areas: there may be a definite open space in the canopy.
        • Looking at the sides of the trees as well.
   6-54. Dismounted consumes a considerable amount of time, effort, and energy. The first Soldier in line
   quickly becomes fatigued depending on the load carried and depth of snow. One-third of the unit is allocated
   to this task. The trail breaking party departs 1 hour earlier for each kilometer to be traveled. The leader is
   responsible for—
        • Setting a course that maintains tactical security to the larger unit while maintaining ease
             of movement:
              The general trace should be set using a map and aerial imagery.
              Every effort should be made to stay in mature forests and avoid areas that are choked
                  with brush.
              These areas make movement excruciatingly slow, and the noise generated by clearing a route
                  or just moving through it can easily alert the enemy.
        • Ensuring the trail marking SOP is adhered to and side trails or back-tracking trails are cordoned off
             to prevent units from taking a wrong turn.
        • Providing guides, if necessary.
        • Ensuring pioneer tools are available:
              The tools in an ahkio group are enough for a squad to cut a trail.
              If water routes are to be used, additional tools such as augers and depth sticks are brought along.
   6-55. The following is the procedure for the lead squad:
       • The squad leader designates the direction, and the lead Soldier begins moving, establishing
           the initial track.
       • The second in line does not step into the firsts’ tracks but steps opposite and flattens the tracks.
       • The third and fourth Soldiers offset their steps left and right by at least one snowshoe width:
            This widens the trail to allow the ahkios to be brought along.
            Should it become necessary, these Soldiers are also the cutters.
            Each one carries a machete to clear brush on the sides of the trail.
            Care is taken to lop branches close to the main trunk, so they do not become spears.
       • The squad leader’s primary focus is navigation:
            A march table should be kept for tracking the unit location.
            GPS should not be solely relied upon, as batteries are easily drained in the cold.
            The squad leader ensures the two teams are rotated, as necessary.
       • Trail team fills low spots with snow, straightens the trail if necessary, and provides security for
           the lead team.

GENERAL EMERGENCY SOLUTIONS
   7-1. Missions typically occur in Arctic or subarctic regions where all personal equipment and Soldiers
   of the unit remain together. However, it is possible to find oneself alone in a remote area with little
   or no personal gear.
   7-2. Part of mission orders is the route taken to the objective. Memorize details, if possible. Mission orders
   should contain isolated Soldier guidance. This is detailed instructions for what to do if separated from the
   unit. A Soldier can be separated from the main troop body, especially when fighting in action. This applies
   mainly to night battle or bad weather but can also happen if a Soldier is careless or march discipline is poor.
   7-3. If all is quiet and there are no battle sounds or aircraft for guidance to friendly lines, go back to the last
   known position. If that is not possible, carefully consider the journey already made and the distance and
   direction from the starting point.
   7-4. If with others, information should be gathered from the group. Send search parties out to find the next
   known steering mark. Carefully mark trails so the search party can return to guide the main group forward,
   or to rejoin the group should their search be unproductive. Meanwhile, the rest of the group should seek
   shelter. If the search party fails to find any known location, perform the steps below but remember to think
   clearly and keep calm:
        • Stay together:
              Meet the emergency as a group.
              Do not wander off as individuals, and never allow Soldiers to straggle.
        • Take shelter:
              If a tent is available, set it up and get in.
              If there is no tent, build an improvised shelter.
              The type of shelter is not important if there is protection from the weather.
        • Keep warm:
              If lost in a forest, light a fire (depending upon the enemy situation).
              If lost in an area where there are no trees, do not use the gasoline stove to heat the shelter.
              The stove is used only to prepare hot food and drinks.
              Conserve the fuel supply and get into the sleeping bag to stay warm.
        • Check the food supply:
              All food and water in the group is collected and rationed systematically.
              Supplement the food supply by shooting large animals and trapping or snaring small ones.
        • Check communications:
              If there is a radio set, use it to contact the parent unit for help.
              If in a forest, prepare fires to attract search planes.
              Smoke is better by day and flame is better at night.
              Mirrors and ground signals are helpful in attracting search parties.
              Isolated Soldier guidance has ground and air signals for search planes.
        • Rest:
              Less energy is expended.
              Food is eaten when resting.
        • Create an arctic necklace:
              This is a 30-inch (76 cm) piece of parachute cord tied in a loop with a lighter and lip balm
                  taped to it.
              A small pocketknife is secured at the knot.
              This is worn around the neck.

IMPROVISED SHELTERS
     7-5. Sometimes tents or other regular shelters are not available. In summer, if the weather is mild, it may
     mean protection only from insects. In the winter, however, it is not possible to stay in the open for long
     periods of time unless Soldiers are moving. In case of emergency, Soldiers need to know how to protect
     themselves from the effects of weather. Use natural shelters such as caves or rock ledges if available. If they
     are not, build a temporary improvised shelter.
     7-6. Building a shelter depends on the equipment and materials available. Some sort of shelter can be built
     during any season of the year. In open terrain, a shelter can be built with ponchos, canvas, snow blocks, or
     other materials. Snow caves, snow trenches, snow houses, or snow holes can be built in the winter. When in
     the woods, a lean-to is usually preferred to other types of shelter. Nature provides the means to prepare
     shelters. However, comfort depends on initiative and improvisational skills.
PONCHO SHELTER

     comfort, various types of shelters and lean-tos can be made by attaching a poncho to trees, tree branches, or
     poles. To build a one-person shelter from one poncho, spread the poncho on the ground, hood side up.
     The hood opening is tightly closed by adjusting and tying the drawstrings. The poncho is raised in the middle
     of its short dimension to form a ridge with the stakes out at the corners and sides. Snow, sod, or branches
     are used to seal the side and one end of the shelter for heat retention and additional protection from the wind.

QUINZHEE SHELTERS AND IGLOOS
     7-22. In alpine regions and other barren locations, shelter can be constructed by piling up a large mound of
     snow and then digging into it. Even light powder snow solidifies enough once it is disturbed and shoveled

              the snow down.
          • Continue to pile snow, periodically stopping to pack the snow down into a dome at least 5 feet
              (approximately 1.5 meters) tall.

FOOD
   7-25. The chances of finding a variety of food in cold-weather areas depends on the time of year and location.
   Shorelines are usually scraped clean of all animals and plants by winter ice. Food can still be found north of
   the timberline, even when mice, fish, and grubs are not available.
   7-26. If a large animal is killed or a large number of small game is found, store or preserve some of the meat
   for future use. Freezing is the best way to preserve fresh meat or fish. It may be necessary to protect supplies
   from small animals. Hang these supplies about 6.5 feet (approximately 2 meters) from the ground.
FISH AND SHELLFISH
   7-27. There are few poisonous fish in cold-weather areas, but some fish, like the sculpin, lay poisonous eggs.
   Black mussels should be avoided in any season because it is impossible to distinguish toxic from nontoxic
   varieties in the field. Some black mussels can be as deadly as strychnine.
   7-28. In cold regions, shark meat is poisonous. Salmon moving upstream to spawn in coastal streams
   and rivers may be plentiful, but their flesh deteriorates as they travel away from the ocean, making them
   unfit to eat (except in an emergency). A salmon that has been in fresh water long enough to turn a brilliant
   red is decaying.
   7-29. Coastal waters are rich in all types of seafood. Grayling, trout, white fish, burbot, and pike are common
   to the lakes, ponds, and Arctic coastal plains of North America and Asia. River snails or freshwater
   periwinkles are also plentiful in the rivers, streams, and lakes.
   7-30. Fish can be speared, shot, netted, hooked, caught by hand, or stunned with a rock or club. Fish
   hooks can be improvised from pins, pieces of wire, or any other metal object that can be bent
   into a hook. Pieces of meat, insects, or minnows can be used for bait. Some fish nibble at any small
   object that hits the water. Cod swim up to investigate strips of cloth or bits of metal or bone, making
   them easy to catch. A net can be made from stout twine or the inner strands of parachute suspension lines.
   These strands can also serve as fishing lines.

SEA MAMMALS
   7-34. During winter and spring, sea mammals—seals, walruses, and polar bears—are found on frozen pack
   ice and floes in open water.
   7-35. Seals are hard to approach but can be stalked. Keep downwind and avoid sudden moves. A white
   camouflage suit helps. Advance only when the animal is sleeping. Do not eat the liver of the bearded seal.
   Its high vitamin A content can cause sickness. Walruses are found on pack ice and are approached by boat.
   Due to its large size and boldness, the walrus is one of the most dangerous animals in the Arctic.
   7-36. Polar bears are found in almost all Arctic regions but rarely appear on land. Avoid them if possible. If
   it is necessary to kill one for food, do not eat the liver. This liver is extremely high in vitamin A, which is
   poisonous to humans. Never eat any bear meat unless it is thoroughly cooked; it is always diseased.

BIRDS AND SNAKES
      7-37. The Arctic is the breeding ground for many birds. Ducks and geese build their nests near ponds on the
      coastal plains during the summer, providing an important source of food. Grouse and ptarmigan live in
      mountainous terrain and brush-covered areas in Arctic and subarctic areas. Sea birds can be found on cliffs
      or small islands on the coast. Sea birds, ravens, and owls are useful for food.
      7-38. In winter, owls, ravens, grouse, and ptarmigans are the only birds available. Ptarmigans and grouse are
      very tame and easily approached. Although they are hard to locate because of their protective coloring, they
      are a good source of food because they can be killed with a stone or a club.
      7-39. The extreme northern reaches of the world have very few snakes. The only venomous snake is
      the European adder found in Europe and Russia. (See figure 7-10.) It is found up to the latitudinal tree line
      (approximately –32°North latitude). Alaska and Greenland are free of venomous snakes, and Canada only
      has them in the southern third of the country.

     U.S. Customary to Metric Measurement Conversions
   A-1. The standard modern form of the metric system is officially called the International System of Units.
   In 1988, Congress designated this system (commonly known as the metric system) as the preferred
   classification of weights and measurements for trade and commerce. Most of the world operates on the meter
   for length and the kilogram for weight.
   A-2. There are two options for converting U.S. customary system measurements to the metric system: apply
   mathematics to calculate the answer or consult an online conversion calculator. The math requires only a
   simple multiplication problem to turn a customary size to a metric size. Table A-1 lists approximate common
   equivalents between the two systems of measurement. These numbers have been rounded to the second
   decimal place.
   A-3. Table A-1 on page 108 is presented as a handy field reference. There are far more measurement

           Battle Drill 1: React to Direct Fire Contact While
                Dismounted—Platoon (07-PLT-D9501)
Conditions: The platoon is conducting operations in a live training environment independently or as
part of a company or larger force. The platoon is dismounted. While stationary or moving, the enemy
engages the platoon with direct fire. Some iterations of this task should be performed in mission
oriented protective posture 4 and at night.

Standards: The platoon reacts to direct fire contact while dismounted according to ATP 3-21.8. The squad in
contact returns fire immediately and seeks cover. The squad in contact locates the enemy and places well-aimed
fire on known enemy positions. Leaders point out enemy positions and identify the types of weapons, such as
small arms and light machine guns. The squads that are not in contact assume the nearest covered and concealed
position. The platoon leader reports the contact.
(Asterisks indicate a leader performance step.)

1. The squad in contact immediately returns well-aimed suppressive fire on known or suspected enemy positions
while taking up a covered position.
2. The squad not in contact assumes the nearest covered and concealed position. (See figure B-1.)

* 3. The squad leaders engage known or suspected enemy positions with well-aimed suppressive fire and report
information to the platoon leader and platoon sergeant.
* 4. The squad leaders control the fire of their Soldiers by using standard fire commands (initial and
supplemental) containing the following information:
         a. Alert.
         b. Weapons or ammunition (optional).
         c. Target description.
