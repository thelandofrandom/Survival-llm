# Cold Injuries and Physical Care — TC 21-3, Chapters 1 and 4

## Chapter 1 — Introduction: outline

- Different types of cold conditions; effects of cold weather on military operations.

## Types of cold conditions

| Zone | Temperature | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Wet cold | Above 19°F (−7°C) | Freezing-and-thawing cycle, rain or wet snow; hard to dress for; keeping clothing dry is a constant battle |
| Dry cold | Consistently below 19°F (−7°C) | Ground frozen, snow dry; colder but easier to stay warm |
| Intense cold | Below dry cold | Dry in nature; dress for dry cold |
| Extreme cold | Below intense | Dry in nature; dress for dry cold |
| Hazardous cold | Lowest zone | Dry in nature; dress for dry cold; exposure limits measured in minutes |

## Effects of cold on operations

The cold slows everything: men move slower, weapons malfunction more, batteries die, fuel thickens, metal becomes brittle, and the simplest task takes twice as long with mittens on. A mission planned for warm weather will fail in the cold unless the plan is rebuilt for it. Wind chill is the complicating factor that turns a survivable temperature into a lethal one.

## Chapter 4 — Optimizing physical care: outline

- Eating and drinking in a cold environment; cleanliness; preventive health; the effects of wind chill; carbon monoxide poisoning.

## Eating and drinking

- Most of what humans eat and drink is used to maintain body heat; only a small portion produces energy for physical work.
- **About 4,600 calories per day** are necessary for Soldiers performing physically demanding work in the cold. Efficiency drops rapidly if this level is not maintained.

**Rations:**

| Ration | Calories | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| MRE (meal, ready to eat) | ~1,300 per ration | Standard operational ration; **four MREs per day** needed to meet the requirement; most components freeze |
| MCW (meal, cold weather) | ~1,450 per bag | Pocket items accessible throughout the day; restricted ration for short durations; freeze-dried — needs a little more than 1 quart (~1 L) of water to reconstitute |
| MORE packet (modular operational ration enhancement) | 1,100 per packet | Supplements additional calories throughout the day |
| UGR Arctic supplement | +914 | Added to the unitized group ration |

## Water

- **Soldiers need approximately 3.75 to 5.25 quarts (3.5 to 5 liters) of water per day**, including the water present in rations.
- **Insensible fluid loss** (breathing and perspiration): approximately **1.5 to 2.25 quarts (1.4 to 2.1 liters) per day**. Sensible loss (urine, sweat) varies with temperature, humidity, and exertion.
- Replace liquids regularly, preferably with **hot drinks that provide extra calories when they contain sugar**.
- **Do not eat snow.** It costs more heat to melt than the water is worth, and it lowers core temperature. Melt snow and drink it hot.
- **Water logistics:** one MRE has close to 8 ounces (~0.25 L) of liquid; one MCW has close to zero. A squad on MCWs needs at least **8 gallons (~30.3 L) of water per day**, and almost **12 gallons (~45.5 L)** at the high end. A company needs a HMMWV just for carrying water cans.

## Cleanliness and preventive health

- Keep the body clean — dirt and sweat in clothing destroy insulation.
- **Potential physical problems in the cold (para 4-20):** snow blindness, sunburn, tent eye, heat cramps and heat exhaustion, hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration, carbon monoxide poisoning.
- **Snow blindness (Figure 4-1):** wear sunglasses or improvised eye protection. The snow reflects UV; unprotected eyes burn.
- **Sunburn:** the same reflection burns skin — cover up and use protection.
- **Tent eye:** irritation from stove smoke in an enclosed tent. Ventilate.
- **Heat cramps and heat exhaustion:** yes, in the cold — from overdressing and overexertion. The treatment is the same as in the heat: rest, cool down, replace fluids and salt.
- **Constipation:** at very cold temperatures people put off bowel movements, which can result in constipation (stomach cramps, dizziness, headaches). Prevent by eating regularly (including fresh or canned fruit) and drinking plenty of liquids.
- **Diarrhea:** can become serious. Prevent by washing hands frequently and ensuring food and water come from sanitary sources.

## Wind chill (paras 4-32 to 4-33, Table 4-1)

- Wind chill is the combination of wind and low temperatures.
- **Worked example from the manual:** at **−20°F (−28.8°C) with calm wind**, there is little danger of wind chill. At the same temperature with a **20 mph (32.1 kph) wind**, the equivalent chill temperature is **−63°F (−52.7°C)**. Under these conditions, **exposed skin can freeze within 6 minutes**.
- **Wind you create yourself:** walking creates a wind speed of about **5 mph (8 kph)**; running, about **10 mph (16 kph)**. Moving vehicles, aircraft propellers, and rotors create wind too.
- **How to use the chart:** get the ambient air temperature, estimate the wind speed, and **multiply the wind speed by 1.5** to get the true wind speed at 33 feet (10 meters), the world meteorological standard for measuring wind speed. Find the two numbers on the chart; where they intersect is the wind chill.
- **Cold injury can disable a Soldier like an enemy bullet.**

## Hypothermia (paras 4-34 to 4-38)

Hypothermia occurs when core body temperature falls below **95°F (35°C)**. Rectal temperature is the only accurate way to measure core temperature, and it cannot safely be used in the field — so diagnosis is by obvious signs and symptoms. **All levels of hypothermia are potentially life-threatening health emergencies requiring immediate care in a medical facility.**

| Level | Core temperature | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 90–95°F (32.2–35°C) | Shivering; lack of sound judgment, confusion, apathy, mild stupidity; pale, cool skin; increased heart and respiratory rates; **the umbles** — fumbles (fine motor skills decrease), stumbles (gross motor skills slip), tumbles, grumbles, mumbles (intellectual and personality changes) |
| Moderate | 80–89°F (26.6–31.6°C) | Uncontrollable shivering; worsening umbles; increased confusion; increased heart and respiratory rates; cold, pale skin |
| Severe | Below 80°F (26.6°C) | Cessation of shivering; muscle rigidity; stupor progressing to unconsciousness; slow or nonpalpable pulse and respiration; cold, bluish skin |

- Shivering alone is not a sign of hypothermia, but it does indicate the body is having a problem with the cold.
- **Treatment before evacuation:** use the Hypothermia Prevention & Management Kit (NSN 6515-01-532-8056) if available; if not, make a hypothermia wrap from commonly available items. Perform first aid for mild and moderate hypothermia. **All cases are evacuated to a medical facility.**

## Frostbite and trench foot

- **Frostbite:** freezing of skin and underlying tissue. Superficial frostbite (frostnip) is the early stage — the skin is pale and numb. Prevention: keep skin covered, keep clothing dry, and monitor each other for white patches on exposed skin. Teeth should be cleaned and the face protected before going out into the cold, reducing the possibility of superficial frostbite.
- **Trench foot / immersion foot (paras 4-15 to 4-16):** caused by prolonged exposure of skin in cold or wet conditions. In extreme cases the skin dies and amputation of the foot or leg may be necessary. **Prevention:** change into dry socks at least **twice daily**; dry and massage feet regularly with foot powder; wear vapor-barrier boots; wear dry leather boots whenever possible. **Early stages:** feet and toes cold, numb, difficult to move; walking becomes challenging; feet swell and become painful; skin appears wrinkled (like a prune). In the early stages, keep feet dry, clean, and exposed to the air. **Later stages:** evacuate the victim to a medical facility.

## Carbon monoxide poisoning (para 4-35+)

- Fresh air in living and working quarters is vital. Carbon monoxide is a **deadly, odorless gas that displaces oxygen in the bloodstream**.
- Sources: stoves, heaters, engines in enclosed spaces — the tent, the vehicle, the shelter.
- Prevention: ventilate living and working quarters; never run a stove or engine in an enclosed space without ventilation; keep the air fresh when cooking or heating in a shelter.
- Symptoms (headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion) are easily mistaken for fatigue or cold. If several people in the same shelter develop the same symptoms at the same time, suspect carbon monoxide and get fresh air immediately.
