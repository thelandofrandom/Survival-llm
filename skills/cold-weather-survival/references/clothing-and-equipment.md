# Clothing and Equipment — TC 21-3, Chapters 2–3

## Chapter 2 — Personal clothing and equipment: outline

- Wear and care of individual clothing; Extended Cold-Weather Clothing System, Gen III; camouflage system; clothing wear and maintenance; Cold-Weather Equipment Modular Sleep System; packing the rucksack; load-carrying equipment; moving equipment over snow; miscellaneous equipment.

## The layering principle (paras 2-2 to 2-6)

Several layers provide more insulation and movement than one heavy garment, even if the heavy garment is as thick as the combined layers. Air pockets trapped between the layers provide insulation, warmth, and ventilation.

**C-O-L-D (Figure 2-2):**
- **C — Keep it Clean.** Dirty clothes are cold clothes. Dirt and grease clog the air spaces in clothing, reducing insulation and ventilation.
- **O — Avoid Overheating.** Select comfortable clothing but do not overdress. In cold weather it is better to be a little cool rather than risk overheating.
- **L — Wear it Loose.** All items of the cold-weather uniform are large enough to allow wearing the appropriate number of layers. If the uniform items do not fit loosely, the insulation that generates warmth is greatly reduced.
- **D — Keep it Dry.** Wet clothing conducts heat away from the body faster than dry clothing. Moisture soaks in from the inside (sweat) and the outside (melting snow). Always brush snow and frost from clothing before entering any heated shelter or vehicle.

A major protection against heat loss is caring for the **head and feet** — the two places the body loses heat fastest and the two places cold injury strikes first.

## Wet cold vs. dry cold (paras 2-7 to 2-9)

- **Wet cold:** temperatures above **19°F (−7°C)**. Freezing-and-thawing cycle, often with rain or wet snow. Hard to dress for; Soldiers battle ever-changing elements to keep clothing dry.
- **Dry cold:** temperatures consistently below **19°F (−7°C)**. Ground frozen, snow dry. Colder, but easier to stay warm. The zones below dry cold (intense, extreme, hazardous) are also dry in nature — dress for the dry-cold environment in all of them.

## ECWCS Gen III — the seven levels

| Level | Item | Detail (para) |
|---|---|---|
| I (2-22) | Lightweight cold-weather undershirt and drawers | Base layer: long-sleeve top and drawers, wicks moisture off the skin |
| II (2-23) | Middleweight cold-weather shirt and drawers | Base layer; also an insulation layer when worn with Level I. Long-sleeve top and drawers |

| IV (2-26) | Wind cold-weather jacket | Lightweight, windproof, water-repellent shell |

**Gloves and mittens (Figure 2-12):** contact glove under the Arctic mitten or intermediate cold-weather gloves. Mittens are warmer than gloves (fingers together share heat); gloves are needed for fine work. Snow sticks to wool gloves and melts — keep a dry pair. **Field use for the Level VII parka (Figure 2-18):** zip it closed and slide it over the feet before getting into the sleeping bag to keep feet warm.

## Camouflage system (paras 2-33 to 2-38, Figures 2-13 to 2-16)

- **Thickly wooded area:** dark and white mix — the trees break up the white.
- **Low brush or light scrub:** mixed pattern — neither all white nor all dark.
- **Roads and trails:** mixed camouflage — the dark of the road against the white of the snow.
- **Above the tree line or in open areas:** snow camouflage — full white.
White is not always the answer. A white figure against dark trees is a target; a dark figure against open snow is a target. Match the background you are actually in.

## Modular Sleep System (paras 2-20+, Figure 2-17)

- Rated down to **−20°F (−28.8°C)** with the user wearing Level I lightweight or Level II middleweight underclothes.
- Colder ratings apply when the user is also wearing the Level III fleece jacket and Level V soft shell trousers.
- Keep the bag dry: clean snow from boots and equipment before putting them inside; keep water bottles outside or in a waterproof bag; tie the top securely so snow and water cannot get in.
- Never get into the bag wet — wet insulation is no insulation.

## Packing the rucksack (Figure 2-19)

- Heavy items high and close to the back; frequently needed items accessible; everything waterproofed (plastic bags inside the rucksack).
- The Level VII parka packs compactly for carrying in helicopters or airborne drops — it is bulky but light, and it is the difference between sleeping and shivering.

## Load-carrying and snow transport (Figures 2-20 to 2-23)

- **MOLLE with skis (Figure 2-20):** the rucksack rides with the skis attached for the march.
- **Snowshoes (Figure 2-21):** two methods of securing snowshoes to the rucksack.
- **Ice axe (Figure 2-22):** attached to the rucksack, accessible without removing the pack.
- **Snowshoe binding (Figure 2-23):** attached to the boot — the binding is the connection that decides whether the snowshoe works or comes off in deep snow.
- **Moving equipment over snow:** skis, snowshoes, and the ahkio (pulled sled). The ahkio carries the heavy load so the Soldier does not have to — in deep snow, pulling is more efficient than carrying.

## Chapter 3 — Weapons and ammunition: outline

- Care, cleaning, and maintenance; breakage and malfunctions; shooting positions.

## Care, cleaning, and maintenance

- **Condensation:** a cold weapon brought into a warm shelter condenses moisture, which freezes when the weapon goes back out. Keep the weapon outside, or keep it at a constant temperature — do not cycle it.
- **Lubricants thicken** in the cold. Use the cold-weather lubricant, and use less of it.
- **Snow in the barrel** is a catastrophic failure waiting to happen. Keep the muzzle covered at all times.
- **Fuel, oil, or solvent splashed on skin** in the cold can cause frostbite — the liquid evaporates and supercools the skin.
- **Barrel covers:** cotton canvas is preferred — it keeps the barrel from melting into the snow and possibly warping. Nylon canvas melts when in contact with a hot barrel.
- **Hot barrel hazards:** the barrel can get hot enough to melt the lanyards on ski poles and the nylon on gloves and mittens. Handle the weapon by the furniture, not the barrel.

## Breakage and malfunctions

- Metal becomes brittle in extreme cold; plastic cracks. Handle weapons and equipment gently — a dropped rifle in −40°F can break.
- Malfunction drills are the standard ones, adapted: do them with mittens on, because you will be wearing mittens.

## Shooting positions in the snow

- **Standing on skis and snowshoes (Figure 3-1):** the skis or snowshoes are the platform.
- **Kneeling on skis (Figure 3-2) and on snowshoes (Figure 3-3).**
- **Prone on skis and snowshoes (Figure 3-4).**
- **M240 machine gun in tripod mode (Figure 3-5).**
- Rule: never try to shoot from a position the snow will not hold. If the platform sinks or shifts, the shot goes with it.
