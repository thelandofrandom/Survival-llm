# Safety Equipment and Abandon Ship — FM 21-22

## Safety Equipment Aboard Ship

Safety equipment aboard a troop transport includes life preservers, lifeboats, life rafts, life floats, life nets, ladders, ropes, and lifebuoys. Every man boarding a vessel is given a life preserver, a life-preserver light, a whistle, and a pamphlet of safety rules; a single-blade jackknife may also be issued. Each man should check his own equipment, provide himself with a line of convenient length, and improvise a canvas or sacking container for personal essentials. A line is indispensable aboard ship or in the water; in spare time, practice tying knots and lashings with it. Unit commanders must have the use of the equipment explained to all soldiers and conduct drills for the particular vessel.

## Life Jackets

Each jacket will support at least **20 pounds downward pull of gravity for 24 hours**. Buoyant material may be cork or balsa blocks, granulated cork, or kapok; kapok jackets are most widely used because they are more comfortable, warmer, and safer. The hard cork or balsa jacket, when jumping, is forced up against the jaw by impact with the water.

### Wear rules

- **Kapok jackets are worn:** at all times when the vessel is outside inland waters; when jumping from transports of moderate freeboard and from tugs and harbor craft; when floating or swimming; when aboard lifeboat, float, or raft.
- **Balsa and cork jackets are worn:** at all times outside inland waters; when floating or swimming; aboard lifeboat, float, or raft; when jumping from vessels of **not more than 10-foot freeboard** — hug the jacket tightly until under the surface.
- **Life jackets are NOT worn:** when jumping from great heights (tie the jacket to your person instead — it will follow you into the water); when jumping into flame or oil-covered surface; when swimming through flame or oil, or under water.

### Putting it on

Bend over, pass one arm through, straighten up, throw the jacket over the shoulders, insert the other arm. Tie the straps with a **double bowknot** — any other knot, especially when wet, is difficult to untie in an emergency. Tie the lower inside straps first, then the lower outside and upper outside straps. The jacket must be tied properly, securely, and snugly, or you will need your hands to hold it on while floating.

## Light and Whistle

The waterproof, battery-operated life-preserver light is turned on by screwing the red cap as far as it will turn; it is pinned or clipped high on the jacket for maximum visibility and to keep it out of the water. The light is **red** to distinguish floating personnel from floating equipment, which shows a white light. The whistle is worn at all times on a cord around the neck — immersion in salt water may affect the voice, but a whistle can still be blown. Carbon-dioxide life belts are ordinarily issued to amphibious troops (paragraph 95 of the manual).

## Abandon-Ship Preparation

- Men are assigned abandon-ship stations and shown the routes to them, often marked by arrows painted on passageway floors; learn the number and location of your lifeboat or raft so well you can find it in the dark.
- Platoon commanders inspect every soldier's equipment at least once every 3 days.
- Abandon-ship drills are held at different hours without warning, first by day (to learn ladders, cables, equipment, and projecting parts) and later in the dark; alternate routes are prescribed and routes are sometimes blocked off so men must use others.
- Blackout at night, use of the ship's head at prescribed times, and the prohibition against throwing garbage overboard are rigidly enforced.

## Individual Safety Procedure Aboard Ship

- Always have your life preserver or safety belt, whistle, rope, life-preserver light, and a **full canteen of water** with you. Wear the preserver during the day and use it as a pillow at night.
- Do not wear leggings aboard a troop ship. Smoke only at designated times and places. Keep out of the ship's crew's way.
- Deck traffic moves counterclockwise: starboard side toward the bow, port side toward the stern.
- Know the emergency signals for all drills. Train yourself to get hold of essential personal equipment in the dark. Sleep as nearly fully clothed as conditions permit.
- Always dress warmly — woolen underwear at all times, in tropic or arctic regions; it protects against both cold air and sunburn.
- Keep passage doors hooked open unless otherwise directed — an explosion may slam unhooked doors and jam closed doors.
- If on deck when bombs hit, lie flat to avoid blast and flying fragments.

## When the Signal Sounds

Go to your abandon-ship station quietly and quickly and wait for orders. Keep silent so orders may be heard. Do not get into the lifeboat or attempt to lower it — wait for orders; normally crew members are assigned to each boat, and you assist only if they are casualties. If your boat or raft is damaged, do not get into another without permission of the man in charge of your boat. Before abandoning ship, listen for the announcement of the ship's approximate position — it lets you set a course for the nearest land. Use nets, ladders, and ropes hanging over the side to board lowered boats or rafts; do not throw anything down into a lifeboat.

## "Every Man for Himself"

Until that order is given, everyone acts according to orders. The order means: get into the water immediately and at least **50 yards away from the ship**. Jump only as a last resort — use ladders, nets, or ropes when you can.
