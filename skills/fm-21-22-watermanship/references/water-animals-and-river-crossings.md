# Water Animals and River Crossings — FM 21-22

## Protection Against Water Animals

Some water animals attack only in self-defense; others may attack if attracted by blood, shiny objects, and light colors such as a man's skin. Do not drag hands or feet overboard. The best defense is to look for them and detour around them — observe underwater, on shoal bottoms, among rocks, and at the surface. Swim slowly and quietly. Keep clothing on if dangerous fish are known to infest the water. Move away from any blood in the water. Stay calm, especially when stung — stings wear away.

| Animal | Where / what it is | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| **Shark** | Widely distributed, most common in warm seas; body normally not more than 11 feet; most dangerous have unsymmetrical tails; attacks usually on the surface, fins breaking water like a periscope | Splash, move arms and legs rapidly, make commotion underwater; metallic noises such as striking a canteen underwater are best; avoid display of skin, underclothing, or shiny objects; **strike the snout** — the most sensitive part |
| **Barracuda** | Warm seas only; long, grayish, pikelike, about 6 feet, long pointed jaws with sharp teeth; attracted by anything that moves, especially light-colored or shiny objects; attacks quickly from below | Creating noise underwater may frighten it away |
| **Sting ray** | All warm seas and some fresh-water rivers; disk-shaped, 1–10 feet across, one or two long barbed spines; conceals itself in muddy or sandy flats and is stepped on | Walk in turbid water poking ahead with a stick and sliding the feet; a touched ray swims away; treat wounds like snake bites |
| **Saw-fish** | Body like a shark's, 10–20 feet, swordlike snout with spines; not vicious, but can swing its saw with power enough to break a man's leg | Strike the areas around the eyes and at the base of the saw; avoid the saw |
| **Moray eel** | All warm seas, especially crevices about coral reefs; up to 6 feet | Knife or spear in defense; keep hands and bare feet out of rock crevices |
| **Sea porcupine** | Warm seas; covered in erectile spines; swells by swallowing water or air; jaws like a parrot's beak, powerful enough to bite off a finger | Keep out of its way |

The manual also covers the alligator and crocodile (paragraph 93), the giant clam (paragraph 86), and the bluefish (paragraph 89).

## Landing Craft

Safety measures and emergency procedure in landing craft (Section IX, paragraphs 94–99) cover the carbon-dioxide life belt (paragraph 95), the equipment worn with it (paragraph 96), and conduct in the craft. Amphibious troops are ordinarily issued the CO2 belt rather than the kapok jacket.

## River Crossings

River crossings are like landing operations except that troops take an active part in operating the boats, distances are shorter, and smaller boats are used. Equipment, tactics, and technique are in FM 5-6, FM 5-10, and TM 5-271; FM 21-22 covers only taking care of yourself on or in the water during the crossing.

### Individual equipment

Worn loosely so it may be slipped off easily if necessary — but not so loosely that it causes noise. All noise and talking is prohibited except whispering instructions.

### Rowing assault boats

- Passengers without paddles crouch low, holding their own and the paddlers' rifles upright against the bottom.
- Men with paddles kneel on their outside knees along the sides, inner hand on top of the paddle, outer hand grasping the shaft near the top of the blade.
- Paddle as rapidly as possible without striking the sides, placing the entire blade in the water.
- **Never stand up in the boat. Do not fire from the boat.**
- The engineer in charge kneels in the stern and steers; normally the boat is pointed directly at the far bank, by compass if necessary, with **no effort made to counteract drift**.
- Nonstandard boats such as civilian rowboats or canoes are handled much the same as engineer assault boats, with additional precautions — a canoe is relatively frail and unstable, so avoid capsizing it or damaging its skin.

### Abandoning the boat

Only on the order of the engineer in charge. Discard all unessential equipment and jump overboard, stepping on the gunwale and steadying yourself with the inner hand on the gunwale. Once in the water, swim to the far bank — men must reach it with their arms and ammunition.

### Swimming a current

Do not swim against the current — it will exhaust you quickly. Mark some point downstream on the far bank, diagonally from where you enter, and swim downstream toward that mark.

### Storm boats

All personnel except the motor operator lie on the bottom facing the bow; no one lies immediately in front of the motor, which may pivot into the boat when beaching under power. The motor operator warns the load before beaching so men can brace. Disembarking and abandoning storm boats is the same as for assault boats.
