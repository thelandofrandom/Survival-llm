# Life Aboard and First Aid at Sea — FM 21-22

> **Historic warning.** The first aid in this reference is 1944 practice. The Schaefer prone-pressure method of artificial respiration has been superseded by modern rescue breathing and CPR, and morphine syrettes were a wartime abandon-ship-kit item. Follow current TCCC / FM 4-25.11 practice. This reference preserves what the 1944 manual actually taught.

## Operation of Life Craft

A lifeboat commander and crew are assigned to every lifeboat and take charge of getting it into the sea and of the passengers. In their absence, soldiers must be prepared to assist in lowering the boat. Rafts and floats are launched per paragraph 31 of the manual; boarding from nets, ladders, and ropes is covered in paragraph 33.

## Command Aboard the Boat

Once safely aboard a life craft, chances of rescue increase considerably — rescue parties start out immediately the sinking is made known. Command aboard a lifeboat is assigned and **takes precedence over rank**; if the boat commander becomes a casualty, the next senior officer or senior NCO assumes command. The commander appoints at least two assistants and is responsible for:

1. Placing a man in charge of all arms and ammunition (as a precaution against insanity or mutiny).
2. Placing a man in charge of all water and provisions.
3. Assigning tasks to all men except the severely exhausted or seriously wounded.
4. Arranging living and sleeping accommodations.
5. Dividing equipment for an equal share of comfort.
6. Rationing water and food.
7. Arranging a suitable diet for each person per day, depending on the provisions aboard.
8. Scheduling the number of meals and time for each.
9. Examining all equipment aboard for serviceability; supervising repairs.
10. Setting a definite course and maintaining it.
11. Taking charge of first-aid equipment and supervising administration of first aid.
12. Maintaining morale and faith; scheduling regular periods of worship if circumstances permit.

Survival depends on everyone carrying out routine tasks cheerfully and promptly, sharing hardships equally and without complaint.

## Water Discipline

- "Water is the most important item for survival. Man can exist only about 7 days without water." Loss of body moisture is hastened by heat and exercise.
- **Do not drink salt water** — it causes diarrhea, weakness, and unbearable thirst; delirious men may have to be forcibly restrained from drinking it.
- **Never drink urine.**
- If water is not available, **do not eat**: eliminating food wastes absorbs water from the kidneys and decreases water in the body.
- Sources, in order: the boat's supply (primary); canteens, which are commandeered by the boat commander and pooled; rain water, collected with cups, tin cans, sea anchor, boat cover, sails, strips of clean clothing, and all canvas gear.
- To keep water in a breaker from freezing, remove the plug and insert a weighted stick protruding a foot or more — the boat's motion keeps the stick moving so ice cannot form.

## Care of Equipment and Clothing

Lash down everything aboard; discard nothing unless sure it will be of no further use; keep the boat as dry as possible. As soon as possible, squeeze out wet clothing, but do not take off all your clothes unless the weather is warm and dry with a moderate wind — undress and dry clothes layer by layer.

## First Aid at Sea — 1944 Practice

### Immediate action

Aid the survivor from the water and place him reclining with head low and feet raised. Examine for injuries, swelling, immersion foot, burns, frostbite, numbness, paralysis, shock, and internal pain. Handle gently; keep warm but do not apply heat directly to the body. If his condition permits, ask about the period of exposure, underwater explosions, conditions during exposure, seawater drunk, and food and fresh water taken.

### Artificial respiration — the Schaefer method (superseded)

Lay the victim on his stomach, one arm extended overhead, the other folded under the head, face toward the extended arm; clear the mouth and pull the tongue forward. Kneel astride the thigh on the side the head faces, palms on the patient's lower ribs, little fingers just touching the lowest rib. With arms straight, swing forward slowly, bringing body weight to bear for the count of "one thousand one, one thousand two, one thousand three"; then swing back, removing all pressure suddenly, for the count of "one thousand one, one thousand two." Repeat. **Modern training uses rescue breathing/CPR — follow current doctrine, not this paragraph.**

### Shock

- Symptoms: pallor, rapid and weak pulse, nausea, irregular sighing breathing, cold and clammy body, chills, glassy eyes with a fixed stare.
- Treatment: keep the patient horizontal with feet elevated and head low — except with a head injury, when the head must be elevated. Keep warm with sailcloth or blankets. Ammonia inhalant from the first-aid kit as a stimulant. Morphine from the abandon-ship kit: **one syrette immediately, repeatable after 3 hours if needed**.

### Blast concussion injury

Caused by depth charges, torpedoes, or aerial bombs exploding in the water; the blast transmitted through water injures lungs, stomach, or intestines. Prevention is the back-float position (legs crossed, body tensed, anus tightened, near the surface). Symptoms: difficult breathing, frothy blood spat or coughed up, abdominal pain, swollen or rigid stomach, shock. Treatment: lay the victim down with head low, keep warm, give morphine for pain, give fresh water in small doses.

### Eye inflammation

Shipwreck victims are often coated in dirty oil; wind and sun glare also inflame the eyes. Prevention: keep eyes covered with a cool, damp cloth during the day or improvise an eyeshade; keep eyes closed or above water when swimming in oil-covered water. Symptoms: red, bloodshot, tearful eyes, sometimes with a sticky crust on the lids; pain on looking at light. Treatment: cleanse with eye dressing from the first-aid kit or drop mineral oil into the eyes; cold compresses over the eyes 10 minutes out of every hour if there are no ulcers.
