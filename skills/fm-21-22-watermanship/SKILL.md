# FM 21-22 Watermanship

## Purpose

FM 21-22, *Watermanship* (War Department Basic Field Manual, 25 April 1944, 156 pages) — the WWII Army manual for taking care of yourself on or in the water in any military situation. It combines ordinary swimming ability and knowledge of the sea with common sense and self-discipline. Its purpose is three-fold: to give the basic principles of watermanship that let a soldier meet any water emergency with confidence; to teach the fundamental practices of small landing-craft operations; and to show how to swim small lakes or rivers under combat conditions. It does not cover waterproofing of individual equipment and weapons — that was left to the technical services' manuals — and its methods are offered as ways and means to be modified to the actual situation, not as rigid rules.

> **HISTORIC WWII-ERA DOCTRINE — READ THIS FIRST.** This is a 1944 manual. Its procedures, equipment (kapok life jackets, carbon-dioxide life belts, morphine syrettes), and medical practice are dated. Its durable value is in principles and fieldcraft: energy conservation in the water, panic control, abandon-ship discipline, life-raft routine, river-crossing technique, and defense against dangerous marine animals. Where a current manual governs, it governs: for modern water survival use **MCRP 3-02F, *Survival*** (Marine Corps, already a skill in this vault) and **ATP 3-50.21, *Survival*** (2018), and for current first aid use modern TCCC / FM 4-25.11 practice — notably, the 1944 Schaefer prone-pressure method of artificial respiration in §7 has been superseded by modern rescue breathing and CPR. Do not follow the 1944 medical procedures in preference to current training.

The doctrine distilled below is drawn only from legible passages; where a figure caption or a measurement was unreadable, it is omitted rather than guessed. ## 1. The Core Idea — Panic Kills First

**1.1. Watermanship defined.** "Military watermanship means taking care of yourself on or in the water in any military situation." The manual's central claim is psychological: "Panic due to ignorance is often the main cause of loss of life at sea. Those who know what to do and keep a cool head have little trouble in keeping afloat until picked up." Training is therefore mostly actual practice under varied conditions, aimed at knowledge and confidence, not at swimming speed.

**1.2. The shipwreck rule.** If shipwrecked far from shore, the soldier does **not** try to swim to shore. He tries to keep afloat until he is rescued. Everything in the swimming section is built around that fact: the skills that matter are the ones that conserve energy and insure buoyancy for long periods. → `references/swimming-and-floating.md`

## 2. Swimming — Energy Conservation, Not Speed

**2.1. What military swimming includes.** Swimming in full clothing and equipment to reach an objective in fit condition for action, plus: submerging and floating; dog paddle and breast stroke; treading water; side stroke; elementary back stroke; jumping; underwater swimming. Detailed stroke instruction is referred to FM 21-20, chapter 5. A practical training note: if a man does the scissors kick more naturally than the frog kick, or vice versa, do not try to change his style.

**2.2. Submerging and bobbing.** A man must first learn to submerge in shallow water with his eyes open, and learn by experience that it is hard to stay under because air trapped in the chest forces him up — expelling part of the air through the nose lets him submerge without effort. **Bobbing** is the controlled version: raise the arms abruptly, sideward and upward, to go down; lower them abruptly, sideward and downward, to rise. Bobbing also conceals the swimmer and protects him from enemy fire.

**2.3. The three floats.** Floating is the best way to conserve energy, and anyone can float, motionless or with slight arm or leg movement. Expand the chest as much as possible to increase buoyancy; after a deep breath, further expansion can be had without sucking in air by pulling up the stomach with the stomach muscles.

- **Back float** — the best relaxing float. Legs together or apart, arms extended to the side or overhead. With legs together and arms by the side, the legs tend to sink and drag the body under.
- **Jelly-fish float** — body doubled up, knees pulled to the chest, head dropped on the chest, arms clasping the knees. The body rolls forward until only the back shows above water. Used mainly when removing clothing.
- **Prone float** — face down, arms and legs extended. The basic position for all prone swimming strokes; face submerged with eyes open to see under water, or head high to observe above it.

**2.4. The strokes.** The **dog paddle** is done from the prone float, hands slightly cupped reaching forward and pulling back, legs doubled up and extended to the rear; left leg and left arm, right leg and right arm, work together. Since arms and legs do not break the surface, it is the silent-swimming stroke. The **breast stroke** is extremely easy and relaxed, usable in all types of water, calm or choppy, with the head held high for observation; it is also used for underwater swimming and when carrying equipment on the back. By splashing with hands and arms on the forward movement, the breast stroke can push away debris and thin surface oil and flame. **Treading water** is standing upright with the feet going through the motions of climbing a ladder, hands pushing down on the water — the position for observing while swimming. The side stroke and elementary back stroke are covered in FM 21-20.

**2.5. Blast protection in the water.** If depth charges, torpedoes, or aerial bombs are exploding nearby, float on the back, **cross the legs, tense the body, tighten the anus, and keep as near the surface of the water as possible** — the same position is prescribed for the back float when blast injury is a risk. Get out of the danger area and out of the water as soon as possible. → `references/swimming-and-floating.md`

## 3. Safety Equipment — Know It Before You Need It

**3.1. What is issued.** Safety equipment aboard a troop transport includes life preservers, lifeboats, life rafts, life floats, life nets, ladders, ropes, and lifebuoys. Every man boarding a vessel is given a life preserver, a life-preserver light, a whistle, and a pamphlet of safety rules; a single-blade jackknife may also be issued. Each man should check his own equipment, provide himself with a line of convenient length, and improvise a canvas or sacking container for personal essentials. A line is indispensable aboard ship or in the water; in spare time, practice tying knots and lashings with it. Unit commanders must have the use of the equipment explained to all soldiers and conduct drills for the particular vessel.

**3.2. Life jackets.** Each jacket will support at least **20 pounds downward pull of gravity for 24 hours**. Buoyant material may be cork or balsa blocks, granulated cork, or kapok; kapok jackets are most widely used because they are more comfortable, warmer, and safer. The hard cork or balsa jacket, when jumping, is forced up against the jaw by impact with the water.

- **Kapok jackets are worn:** at all times when the vessel is outside inland waters; when jumping from transports of moderate freeboard and from tugs and harbor craft; when floating or swimming; when aboard lifeboat, float, or raft.
- **Balsa and cork jackets are worn:** at all times outside inland waters; when floating or swimming; aboard lifeboat, float, or raft; when jumping from vessels of **not more than 10-foot freeboard** — hug the jacket tightly until under the surface.
- **Life jackets are NOT worn:** when jumping from great heights (tie the jacket to your person instead — it will follow you into the water); when jumping into flame or oil-covered surface; when swimming through flame or oil, or under water.

**Putting it on:** bend over, pass one arm through, straighten up, throw the jacket over the shoulders, insert the other arm. Tie the straps with a **double bowknot** — any other knot, especially when wet, is difficult to untie in an emergency. Tie the lower inside straps first, then the lower outside and upper outside straps. The jacket must be tied properly, securely, and snugly, or you will need your hands to hold it on while floating.

**3.3. Light and whistle.** The waterproof, battery-operated life-preserver light is turned on by screwing the red cap as far as it will turn; it is pinned or clipped high on the jacket for maximum visibility and to keep it out of the water. The light is **red** to distinguish floating personnel from floating equipment, which shows a white light. The whistle is worn at all times on a cord around the neck — immersion in salt water may affect the voice, but a whistle can still be blown. Carbon-dioxide life belts are ordinarily issued to amphibious troops (described in paragraph 95 of the manual). → `references/safety-equipment-and-abandon-ship.md`

## 4. Abandoning Ship — Discipline Before the Water

**4.1. Preparation.** Men are assigned abandon-ship stations and shown the routes to them, often marked by arrows painted on passageway floors; learn the number and location of your lifeboat or raft so well you can find it in the dark. Platoon commanders inspect every soldier's equipment at least once every 3 days. Abandon-ship drills are held at different hours without warning, first by day (to learn ladders, cables, equipment, and projecting parts) and later in the dark; alternate routes are prescribed and routes are sometimes blocked off so men must use others.

**4.2. Individual safety procedure aboard ship.** Always have your life preserver or safety belt, whistle, rope, life-preserver light, and a **full canteen of water** with you. Wear the preserver during the day and use it as a pillow at night. Do not wear leggings aboard a troop ship. Smoke only at designated times and places. Keep out of the ship's crew's way. Deck traffic moves counterclockwise: starboard side toward the bow, port side toward the stern. Know the emergency signals for all drills. Train yourself to get hold of essential personal equipment in the dark. Sleep as nearly fully clothed as conditions permit. Always dress warmly — woolen underwear at all times, in tropic or arctic regions; it protects against both cold air and sunburn. Keep passage doors hooked open unless otherwise directed — an explosion may slam unhooked doors and jam closed doors. If on deck when bombs hit, lie flat to avoid blast and flying fragments.

**4.3. When the signal sounds.** Go to your abandon-ship station quietly and quickly and wait for orders. Keep silent so orders may be heard. Do not get into the lifeboat or attempt to lower it — wait for orders; normally crew members are assigned to each boat, and you assist only if they are casualties. If your boat or raft is damaged, do not get into another without permission of the man in charge of your boat. Before abandoning ship, listen for the announcement of the ship's approximate position — it lets you set a course for the nearest land. Use nets, ladders, and ropes hanging over the side to board lowered boats or rafts; do not throw anything down into a lifeboat.

**4.4. "Every man for himself."** Until that order is given, everyone acts according to orders. The order means: get into the water immediately and at least **50 yards away from the ship**. Jump only as a last resort — use ladders, nets, or ropes when you can. → `references/safety-equipment-and-abandon-ship.md`

## 5. Lifeboats, Rafts, and Living Aboard

**5.1. Operation of life craft.** A lifeboat commander and crew are assigned to every lifeboat and take charge of getting it into the sea and of the passengers. In their absence, soldiers must be prepared to assist in lowering the boat. Rafts and floats are launched per paragraph 31 of the manual; boarding from nets, ladders, and ropes is covered in paragraph 33.

**5.2. Command aboard the boat.** Once safely aboard a life craft, chances of rescue increase considerably — rescue parties start out immediately the sinking is made known. Command aboard a lifeboat is assigned and **takes precedence over rank**; if the boat commander becomes a casualty, the next senior officer or senior NCO assumes command. The commander appoints at least two assistants and is responsible for: placing a man in charge of all arms and ammunition (as a precaution against insanity or mutiny); placing a man in charge of all water and provisions; assigning tasks to all men except the severely exhausted or seriously wounded; arranging living and sleeping accommodations; dividing equipment for an equal share of comfort; rationing water and food; setting a definite course and maintaining it; taking charge of first-aid equipment; and maintaining morale and faith.

**5.3. Water — the decisive item.** "Water is the most important item for survival. Man can exist only about 7 days without water." **Do not drink salt water** — it causes diarrhea, weakness, and unbearable thirst; delirious men may have to be forcibly restrained from drinking it. **Never drink urine.** If water is not available, do not eat: eliminating food wastes absorbs water from the kidneys and decreases water in the body. Sources, in order: the boat's supply (primary); canteens, which are commandeered by the boat commander and pooled; rain water, collected with cups, tin cans, sea anchor, boat cover, sails, strips of clean clothing, and all canvas gear. To keep water in a breaker from freezing, remove the plug and insert a weighted stick protruding a foot or more — the boat's motion keeps the stick moving so ice cannot form.

**5.4. Care of equipment and clothing.** Lash down everything aboard; discard nothing unless sure it will be of no further use; keep the boat as dry as possible. As soon as possible, squeeze out wet clothing, but do not take off all your clothes unless the weather is warm and dry with a moderate wind — undress and dry clothes layer by layer. → `references/life-aboard-and-first-aid.md`

## 6. First Aid at Sea (1944 Practice — See the Historic Warning)

**6.1. Immediate action.** Aid the survivor from the water and place him reclining with head low and feet raised. Examine for injuries, swelling, immersion foot, burns, frostbite, numbness, paralysis, shock, and internal pain. Handle gently; keep warm but do not apply heat directly to the body. If his condition permits, ask about the period of exposure, underwater explosions, conditions during exposure, seawater drunk, and food and fresh water taken.

**6.2. Artificial respiration — the Schaefer method (superseded).** Lay the victim on his stomach, one arm extended overhead, the other folded under the head, face toward the extended arm; clear the mouth and pull the tongue forward. Kneel astride the thigh on the side the head faces, palms on the patient's lower ribs, little fingers just touching the lowest rib. With arms straight, swing forward slowly, bringing body weight to bear for the count of "one thousand one, one thousand two, one thousand three"; then swing back, removing all pressure suddenly, for the count of "one thousand one, one thousand two." Repeat. **This is the 1944 method. Modern training uses rescue breathing/CPR — follow current doctrine, not this paragraph.**

**6.3. Shock.** Symptoms: pallor, rapid and weak pulse, nausea, irregular sighing breathing, cold and clammy body, chills, glassy eyes with a fixed stare. Treatment: keep the patient horizontal with feet elevated and head low — except with a head injury, when the head must be elevated. Keep warm with sailcloth or blankets. Ammonia inhalant from the first-aid kit as a stimulant. Relieve pain with morphine from the abandon-ship kit: **one syrette immediately, repeatable after 3 hours if needed**.

**6.4. Blast concussion injury.** Caused by depth charges, torpedoes, or aerial bombs exploding in the water; the blast transmitted through water injures lungs, stomach, or intestines. Prevention is the back-float position of §2.5. Symptoms: difficult breathing, frothy blood spat or coughed up, abdominal pain, swollen or rigid stomach, shock. Treatment: lay the victim down with head low, keep warm, give morphine for pain, give fresh water in small doses.

**6.5. Eye inflammation.** Shipwreck victims are often coated in dirty oil; wind and sun glare also inflame the eyes. Prevention: keep eyes covered with a cool, damp cloth during the day or improvise an eyeshade; keep eyes closed or above water when swimming in oil-covered water. Symptoms: red, bloodshot, tearful eyes, sometimes with a sticky crust on the lids; pain on looking at light. Treatment: cleanse with eye dressing from the first-aid kit or drop mineral oil into the eyes; cold compresses over the eyes 10 minutes out of every hour if there are no ulcers. → `references/life-aboard-and-first-aid.md`

## 7. Protection Against Water Animals

**7.1. General rule.** Some water animals attack only in self-defense; others may attack if attracted by blood, shiny objects, and light colors such as a man's skin. Do not drag hands or feet overboard. The best defense is to look for them and detour around them — observe underwater, on shoal bottoms, among rocks, and at the surface. Swim slowly and quietly. Keep clothing on if dangerous fish are known to infest the water. Move away from any blood in the water. Stay calm, especially when stung — stings wear away.

**7.2. The animals, as the manual describes them.**

| Animal | Where / what it is | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| **Shark** | Widely distributed, most common in warm seas; body normally not more than 11 feet; most dangerous have unsymmetrical tails; attacks usually on the surface, fins breaking water like a periscope | Splash, move arms and legs rapidly, make commotion underwater; metallic noises such as striking a canteen underwater are best; avoid display of skin, underclothing, or shiny objects; **strike the snout** — the most sensitive part |
| **Barracuda** | Warm seas only; long, grayish, pikelike, about 6 feet, long pointed jaws with sharp teeth; attracted by anything that moves, especially light-colored or shiny objects; attacks quickly from below | Creating noise underwater may frighten it away |
| **Sting ray** | All warm seas and some fresh-water rivers; disk-shaped, 1–10 feet across, one or two long barbed spines; conceals itself in muddy or sandy flats and is stepped on | Walk in turbid water poking ahead with a stick and sliding the feet; a touched ray swims away; treat wounds like snake bites |
| **Saw-fish** | Body like a shark's, 10–20 feet, swordlike snout with spines; not vicious, but can swing its saw with power enough to break a man's leg | Strike the areas around the eyes and at the base of the saw; avoid the saw |
| **Moray eel** | All warm seas, especially crevices about coral reefs; up to 6 feet | Knife or spear in defense; keep hands and bare feet out of rock crevices |
| **Sea porcupine** | Warm seas; covered in erectile spines; swells by swallowing water or air; jaws like a parrot's beak, powerful enough to bite off a finger | Keep out of its way |

The manual also covers the alligator and crocodile (paragraph 93), the giant clam (paragraph 86), and the bluefish (paragraph 89). → `references/water-animals-and-river-crossings.md`

## 8. Landing Craft and River Crossings

**8.1. Landing craft.** Safety measures and emergency procedure in landing craft (Section IX, paragraphs 94–99) cover the carbon-dioxide life belt (paragraph 95), the equipment worn with it (paragraph 96), and conduct in the craft. Amphibious troops are ordinarily issued the CO2 belt rather than the kapok jacket.

**8.2. River crossings.** River crossings are like landing operations except that troops take an active part in operating the boats, distances are shorter, and smaller boats are used. Equipment, tactics, and technique are in FM 5-6, FM 5-10, and TM 5-271; FM 21-22 covers only taking care of yourself on or in the water during the crossing.

- **Individual equipment** is worn loosely so it may be slipped off easily if necessary — but not so loosely that it causes noise. All noise and talking is prohibited except whispering instructions.
- **Rowing assault boats:** passengers without paddles crouch low, holding their own and the paddlers' rifles upright against the bottom. Men with paddles kneel on their outside knees along the sides, inner hand on top of the paddle, outer hand grasping the shaft near the top of the blade. Paddle as rapidly as possible without striking the sides, placing the entire blade in the water. **Never stand up in the boat. Do not fire from the boat.** The engineer in charge kneels in the stern and steers; normally the boat is pointed directly at the far bank, by compass if necessary, with **no effort made to counteract drift**.
- **Abandoning the boat:** only on the order of the engineer in charge. Discard all unessential equipment and jump overboard, stepping on the gunwale and steadying yourself with the inner hand on the gunwale. Once in the water, swim to the far bank — men must reach it with their arms and ammunition.
- **Swimming a current:** do not swim against the current — it will exhaust you quickly. Mark some point downstream on the far bank, diagonally from where you enter, and swim downstream toward that mark.
- **Storm boats:** all personnel except the motor operator lie on the bottom facing the bow; no one lies immediately in front of the motor, which may pivot into the boat when beaching under power. The motor operator warns the load before beaching so men can brace. Disembarking and abandoning storm boats is the same as for assault boats. → `references/water-animals-and-river-crossings.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Panic kills first.** Knowledge and a cool head keep a man afloat until rescue; ignorance and panic do not.
2. **Conserve energy in the water.** Float when you can; swim only when you must; never try to swim to a distant shore from a shipwreck.
3. **Know your equipment before the emergency.** Jacket, light, whistle, line, and full canteen — checked, worn, and practiced with.
4. **Discipline in the boat.** Command takes precedence over rank; water is pooled and rationed; nothing is discarded that may still be of use.
5. **Never drink salt water or urine.** If there is no water, do not eat.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** FM 21-22 is a historic field reference, not the authority — MCRP 3-02F and ATP 3-50.21 govern modern water survival, and modern CPR governs resuscitation.
