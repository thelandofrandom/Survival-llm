# Warrior Ethos and Soldier Combat Skills

## Purpose

TC 3-21.75, The Warrior Ethos and Soldier Combat Skills, U.S. Army, August 2013, 308 pages — the Army's individual-Soldier manual, in two parts. Part One, Warrior Ethos, is the mindset and the preparation: the Army values, the law of land warfare, individual readiness, combat casualty care and preventive medicine, and the three environments that kill the unprepared (desert, jungle, arctic). Part Two, Soldier Combat Skills, is the craft: cover, concealment, and camouflage; fighting positions; movement; urban areas; "Every Soldier is a Sensor"; combat marksmanship; communications; survival, evasion, resistance, and escape; and CBRN weapons. It supersedes FM 3-21.75 (28 January 2008).

This skill is written for the individual — the Soldier, the citizen, or the survivor who has no squad to lean on and must do the job himself. The manual's governing idea is in its title: ethos first, skills second. The skills are useless without the ethos that makes a person keep fighting, keep reporting, and keep living when everything goes wrong.

## 1. The Warrior Ethos (Chapter 1)

**The operational environment** is the starting point: the modern battlefield has no front line, no safe rear, and no noncombatant bystanders who can be assumed friendly. Every Soldier is a combatant and a sensor, whether his job is infantry or logistics.

**Army values** — the seven, in the order the Army teaches them: Loyalty, Duty, Respect, Selfless Service, Honor, Integrity, Personal Courage (LDRSHIP). They are not slogans; they are the decision rules for the moments when no order covers the situation.

**Law of land warfare.** Soldiers fight within the law of war: distinguish combatants from noncombatants, use proportional force, treat prisoners humanely, and respect protected persons and places. The law is not a constraint on winning — it is what separates a Soldier from a murderer, and it is what keeps the force legitimate in the eyes of the population it operates among.

**Warrior culture** is the shared identity: the Soldier's Creed, the Warrior Ethos ("I will always place the mission first. I will never accept defeat. I will never quit. I will never leave a fallen comrade."), and the discipline to live them when nobody is watching.

**Battle drill and Warrior Drills.** A battle drill is a collective action trained to automaticity — the same concept as the Ranger Handbook's Chapter 8. Warrior Drills are the individual versions: the actions a single Soldier performs without thinking when contact is made, when a casualty falls, or when a weapon stops. The manual's point is that drills are what survive fear. You will not rise to the occasion; you will fall to the level of your training. → `references/warrior-ethos-and-readiness.md`

## 2. Individual readiness (Chapter 2)

**Predeployment** is a checklist, not a mood: personal affairs in order (will, powers of attorney, family care plan), financial affairs settled, medical and dental readiness complete, equipment issued and inspected, and training current. **Legal assistance** is available before deployment — use it, because a Soldier who deploys with his affairs unsettled fights with half his mind at home.

**Personal weapon.** The Soldier's weapon is his responsibility from issue to turn-in: know it, clean it, zero it, and carry it in a condition that lets it fire the moment it is needed. Chapter 10 covers the marksmanship; Chapter 2 covers the ownership. → `references/warrior-ethos-and-readiness.md`

## 3. Combat care and preventive medicine (Chapter 3)

**Combat lifesaver.** Every Soldier should have combat lifesaver training on basic life-saving procedures. The first aid table (Table 3-1) is the priority order:

1. **Check for BREATHING** — lack of oxygen, from a compromised airway or inadequate breathing, can cause brain damage or death in just a few minutes.
2. **Check for BLEEDING** — life continues only with sufficient blood to carry oxygen to tissues.
3. **Check for SHOCK** — unless shock is prevented, first aid performed, and medical treatment provided, death may result even from an otherwise nonfatal injury.

**The airway.** The tongue is the single most common airway obstruction. In most cases the **head-tilt/chin-lift** technique clears it — it pulls the tongue away from the air passage. The **jaw-thrust** is the technique for a casualty with a suspected neck injury, because it can usually be done without moving the neck. After opening the airway, keep it open and check breathing by observing the chest. If the casualty fails to resume adequate spontaneous breathing, rescue breathing is performed — and the manual's standard is uncompromising: if the heart has stopped, seek medical help immediately, because seconds count; a casualty with no pulse is unconscious, limp, and has wide-open pupils.

**Bleeding control** includes the tourniquet — applied above the knee for a leg wound (Figures 3-36, 3-38), knotted over a rigid object and twisted to tighten. The casualty evacuation section covers the carries: the two-hand seat carry and the other manual carries for moving a casualty when no litter is available, including transport over long distances (over 300 meters) by teams working in rotation.

**Preventive medicine** is the other half of the chapter: clothing and sleeping gear (the right layers, kept dry, and the sleeping system used correctly), and **mental health and morale** — the manual treats them as medical subjects, because a Soldier who has lost his will to fight is a casualty just as surely as one who has lost blood. → `references/warrior-ethos-and-readiness.md`

## 4. The environments that kill (Chapter 4)

**Desert.** Types of desert (rocky, sandy, mountainous, coastal); preparation: water discipline is the whole game — the manual's desert section is organized around finding, carrying, and conserving water, and around the heat injuries (heat cramps, heat exhaustion, heat stroke) that kill faster than the enemy. Some desert areas contain sand dunes over 1,000 feet (300 meters) high and 10 to 15 miles (16 to 25 km) long — the terrain itself is an obstacle.

**Jungle.** Types of jungle; preparation: the jungle's threats are water, disease, and visibility — the manual covers water purification, foot care (the jungle rots feet faster than any other environment), and the navigation problem of a canopy that hides the sun and the landmarks.

**Arctic.** Types of arctic terrain; preparation: cold injuries, the wind chill problem, and the clothing and shelter discipline that keeps a Soldier alive below freezing. The companion skill `cold-weather-survival` (TC 21-3) goes deep; this chapter is the individual primer. → `references/warrior-ethos-and-readiness.md`

## 5. Cover, concealment, and camouflage (Chapter 5)

**Cover** is protection from fire: natural cover (rocks, folds in the ground, trees thick enough to stop a bullet) and man-made cover (walls, berms, vehicles). **Concealment** is protection from observation: natural concealment (vegetation, shadows, ground folds) and actions as concealment (staying still, staying low, staying in shadow). **Camouflage** is the deliberate defeat of observation: movement, positions, outlines and shadows, shine, shape, colors, dispersion, preparation, and individual techniques.

The rules that matter: **break up the outline** (the human silhouette is the most recognizable shape on a battlefield); **kill the shine** (skin, metal, glass, and optics all reflect — cover them); **mind the shadow** (your shadow gives you away from the air); **disperse** (a group in one place is a target; spread out); **move like the terrain** (slow and low in the open, fast between cover). The manual's individual techniques — face paint, vegetation, helmet cover — are the application of those rules to one Soldier. → `references/combat-skills-fieldcraft.md`

## 6. Fighting positions (Chapter 6)

Cover, concealment, and camouflage again — now applied to a hole in the ground. **Sectors and fields of fire** are assigned before the position is dug, and the position is built to serve them. **Hasty fighting positions** are dug fast (a prone scrape with a parapet) when contact is imminent; **deliberate fighting positions** are dug to standard when time allows. The **two-man fighting position** is the standard: two Soldiers, interlocking sectors, one digging while the other provides security, then switching. The **one-man fighting position** is the fallback. **Close combat missile fighting positions** are built for anti-armor weapons, with the backblast area cleared. **Range cards** (DA Form 5517) are the record of the position: the sector sketch, the ranges to reference points, and the data that lets a replacement gunner fight from the position without re-learning it. → `references/combat-skills-fieldcraft.md`

## 7. Movement (Chapter 7)

**Individual movement techniques:** the low crawl, the high crawl, the rush (3–5 seconds, then down — never longer than the enemy needs to aim), and the movement between covered positions. **Immediate actions while moving:** react to flares, react to indirect fire, react to direct fire — each a drill, each automatic. **Fire and movement:** one element fires while the other moves, alternating, so the enemy is always suppressed and the moving element is always covered. **Movement on vehicles:** mounting, riding, and dismounting under fire — the vehicle is transport, not cover, and the dismount is the most dangerous moment. → `references/combat-skills-fieldcraft.md`

## 8. Urban areas (Chapter 8)

**Movement techniques:** avoid open areas; move parallel to buildings; move past windows (below the sill, fast, without silhouetting); cross a wall (over the top, not around the end, where the enemy expects you); move around corners (pie the corner, don't step into it); move within a building (along the walls, clearing as you go). **Other procedures:** entering a building (the fatal funnel — the doorway is the most dangerous place in urban combat, so enter fast and dominate the room); clearing a room (the manual's room-clearing drill, with the four-man entry as the standard and the adaptations for fewer men). **Fighting positions:** hasty and prepared, in and around buildings — the urban fighting position uses the building as its parapet and its walls as its cover. → `references/combat-skills-fieldcraft.md`

## 9. Every Soldier is a Sensor (Chapter 9)

**The idea:** every Soldier observes, collects, and reports. The unit's intelligence picture is built from the reports of individuals, not from sensors alone. **Report levels:** Level I (the SALUTE report — the basic spot report), Level II (detailed), Level III (full), Level IV (analytical). The levels help the S-2 record and disseminate both important and subtle details for all-source analysis and future planning.

**SALUTE format (Table 9-1):**
- **S — Size/Who:** quantity and echelon or size. Report "10 enemy infantrymen," not "a rifle squad." Multiple units get multiple entries.
- **A — Activity/What:** a concise bullet statement, related to the priority intelligence requirement. "Emplacing mines in the road."
- **L — Location/Where:** generally a grid coordinate, including the 100,000-meter grid zone designator; an address if appropriate, but still with an eight-digit grid. For movement, give "from" and "to"; the route goes under Equipment.
- **U — Unit/Who:** the complete designation of a military unit, or the name and identifying features of civilians or insurgent groups.
- **T — Time/When:** the date-time group for future events; ongoing events reported as such. Report the time you **saw** the activity, not the time you report it. Always local or Zulu (Z) time.
- **E — Equipment/How:** what equipment the enemy has and how he is using it.

Reports should answer who, what, and where, after when. Make notes and draw sketches to help you remember details. Report all information about the enemy to your leader **quickly, accurately, and completely**.

**Handling and reporting of the enemy:** prisoners, defectors, and captured documents are reported and handled per the unit SOP and the law of war — they are intelligence, not trophies. **Operations security:** what you report, and how, is itself information the enemy wants; the manual covers the OPSEC discipline of reporting.

**Observation techniques and range estimation** — the skill's most portable section. **Methods (para 9-33):** the 100-meter unit-of-measure method; the appearance-of-objects method; the flash-and-sound method; the mil-relation method; a combination. **100-meter method (para 9-34):** picture 100 meters on the ground; for ranges up to 500 meters, count the 100-meter lengths; beyond 500 meters, pick a halfway point, count to it, and double. **Appearance of objects (Table 9-2):**

| Range | What you see on a human body |
|---|---|
| 200 m | Clear in all detail — equipment, skin color |
| 300 m | Clear body outline, face color good, remaining detail blurred |
| 400 m | Body outline clear, other details blurred |
| 500 m | Body tapered, head indistinct from body |
| 600 m | Body a wedge shape, no head apparent |
| 700 m | Solid wedge shape (body outline) |

**Flash-and-sound (para 9-36):** best at night. Sound travels at 1,100 feet (300 meters) per second. When you see the flash or smoke of a weapon, start counting; stop when you hear the sound. **Multiply the count by three** — the result is the approximate distance in hundreds of meters. Count 1 = ~300 m; count 3 = ~900 m. If you must count higher than nine, start over at one each time you hit nine — counting higher throws the timing off. **Mil-relation (para 9-37):** the easiest and best way. At 1,000 meters, a 1-mil angle equals 1 meter. **Range = (estimated height of target in meters ÷ size of target in mils) × 1,000.** **Combination (para 9-38):** battlefield conditions are rarely ideal — use two methods and compare; if they agree, the figure is close to true. → `references/sensor-marksmanship-comms-sere.md`

## 10. Combat marksmanship (Chapter 10)

Safety and administrative procedures first; then the weapons (M16/M4 family); fire control (when to shoot, what to shoot at, and how to distribute fire); and the zeros. **Combat zero** is the zero for the fight you are in. **Mechanical zero** is the sight's centered starting point — M16A2/A3 (Figure 10-13) and M16A4/M4 (Figure 10-14) have their own procedures. **Battlesight zero** is the zero that lets you hit a man-sized target from point-blank to 300 meters without adjusting the sights — M16A2/A3 (Figure 10-15), M16A4 (Figure 10-16), M4 (Figure 10-17). **Shot groups** are the diagnostic: a tight group in the wrong place is a zero problem; a scattered group is a fundamentals problem. **Borelight zero** is the zero done with a laser boresight when live fire is not possible. **Misfire procedures and immediate action:** SPORTS (Slap, Pull, Observe, Release, Tap, Shoot) — the manual's immediate action for a stoppage, drilled until it is a reflex. **Reflexive fire** is the close-range engagement technique: weapon up, eyes on the target, fire on the move. → `references/sensor-marksmanship-comms-sere.md`

## 11. Communications (Chapter 11)

**Means:** messengers, wire, visual signals, sound, radio — in that order of reliability when the enemy is jamming you. **Radiotelephone procedures:** the rules of the net, types of nets, precedence of reports, message format, common messages, prowords, operation on a net. **Communications security:** classifications, signal operating instructions (SOI), automated net control device (ANCD). **Equipment:** radios (the SINCGARS family and the patrol radios), wire, and telephone equipment. The companion skills `visual-signals` (TC 3-21.60) and `radio-operator` (MCRP 3-40.3B) go deep; this chapter is the individual Soldier's working knowledge. → `references/sensor-marksmanship-comms-sere.md`

## 12. Survival, evasion, resistance, and escape (Chapter 12)

**SURVIVAL** — the acrostic that guides action in any survival situation:
- **S — Size up the situation:** surroundings, physical condition, equipment. Conceal yourself from the enemy; security is key. Determine if the enemy is attacking, defending, or withdrawing. Plan around water, food, and shelter.
- **U — Use all your senses:** undue haste makes waste. Evaluate before acting; an unplanned action can result in capture or death. Plan your moves; stay ready to move quickly without endangering yourself.
- **R — Remember where you are:** find who has a map or compass; keep track of your location relative to enemy units, friendly units, water sources, and cover and concealment.
- **V — Vanquish fear and panic:** your greatest enemies. Uncontrolled, they destroy decision-making and drain energy. Control them with self-confidence and training.
- **I — Improvise:** use a tool designed for one purpose for another; use natural objects (a rock for a hammer). Save survival-kit items for when you have no other options.
- **V — Value living:** maintain a high value on living. Perseverance — refusal to give in — gives the mental and physical strength to endure.
- **A — Act like the natives:** locals and animals have adapted to the environment. Observe their routines — where they get food and water, when they eat, when they sleep. The answers help you avoid capture.
- **L — Live by your wits, but for now, learn basic skills:** the manual's closing charge — learn the skills before you need them.

**The three-phase individual survival kit:** what you carry on your person (no equipment), what you carry in your load-bearing equipment, and what you carry in your rucksack. **Evasion:** the movement discipline for staying free — routes, shelters, and the tool for remembering shelter locations (Figure 12-2). **Resistance:** the Code of Conduct (Figure 12-3) — the six articles that govern a captured Soldier's behavior: I am an American, fighting in the forces which guard my country and our way of life; I will never surrender of my own free will; if I am captured I will continue to resist by all means available; if I am captured I will keep faith with my fellow prisoners; if I am captured I will accept neither parole nor special favors; I will never forget that I am an American, fighting for freedom, responsible for my actions, and dedicated to the principles which made my country free. **Escape:** the planning and execution of getting out — the manual's treatment is deliberately general, because every escape is specific to its circumstances. → `references/sensor-marksmanship-comms-sere.md`

## 13. CBRN weapons (Chapter 13)

**Chemical weapons:** nerve agents (act quickly — effects in seconds, minutes, or hours; the antidote is atropine and 2-PAM, carried as the Mark I kit or the ATNAA autoinjector; self-aid is one NAAK injected in the thigh if symptoms appear, with a second if symptoms persist — and the manual warns that atropine and 2-PAM alone often do not prevent severe and long-lasting convulsions, which is why the convulsant antidote for nerve agents, CANA, is also carried); blister agents (burning sensations in the skin, eyes, and nose — decontaminate eyes and skin at once); blood agents; incapacitating agents; choking agents. **Detection:** the M256 kit, M8/M9 paper, and the other detectors in the Soldier's load. **Protective actions:** mask, MOPP levels, and the discipline of putting the mask on before the agent arrives — by the time you smell it, you are already exposed. **Protective equipment:** the protective mask, the JSLIST suit, gloves, and boots, and the decontamination procedures that follow exposure. The chapter also covers biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons and the same pattern for each: detect, protect, decontaminate, report. → `references/sensor-marksmanship-comms-sere.md`

## Output Contract

When answering from this skill, lead with the ethos when the question is about what to do, and with the procedure when the question is about how to do it. Give SALUTE reports in the six-line format, range estimates with the method used, and first-aid answers in the Table 3-1 order (breathing, bleeding, shock). For SERE questions, use the SURVIVAL acrostic as the framework. If the question crosses into a companion manual, say so: TCCC depth lives in `tccc-first-aid` (ATP 4-02.11), signaling depth in `visual-signals` (TC 3-21.60), cold-weather depth in `cold-weather-survival` (TC 21-3), and survival depth in `survival` (ATP 3-50.21).

## Operating Rules

1. **Ethos first, skills second.** The Warrior Ethos is what makes a person keep fighting, keep reporting, and keep living when everything goes wrong.
2. **Report quickly, accurately, and completely.** SALUTE, every time, with the time you saw it — not the time you report it.
3. **Breathing, bleeding, shock — in that order.** The first aid priority never changes.
4. **You will fall to the level of your training.** Drills are what survive fear; drill until the action is automatic.
5. **Every Soldier is a sensor.** Observe, record, report — the intelligence picture is built from individuals.
6. **SURVIVAL is a sequence, not a slogan.** Size up first; act second; panic never.
