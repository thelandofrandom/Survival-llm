# Warrior Ethos and Readiness — TC 3-21.75, Chapters 1–4

## Chapter 1 — Introduction: outline

- Operational environment; Army values; law of land warfare; warrior culture; battle drill; warrior drills.

## The operational environment

The modern battlefield has no front line and no safe rear. Every Soldier is a combatant and a sensor, regardless of military occupational specialty. The manual opens here because everything that follows assumes a Soldier who may be attacked at any time, in any place, and must be ready to fight, report, and survive as an individual.

## Army values (LDRSHIP)

- **Loyalty** — bear true faith and allegiance to the U.S. Constitution, the Army, your unit, and other Soldiers.
- **Duty** — fulfill your obligations.
- **Respect** — treat people as they should be treated.
- **Selfless Service** — put the welfare of the nation, the Army, and your subordinates before your own.
- **Honor** — live up to Army values.
- **Integrity** — do what is right, legally and morally.
- **Personal Courage** — face fear, danger, or adversity (physical or moral).

## Law of land warfare

- Distinguish combatants from noncombatants; use proportional force; treat prisoners humanely; respect protected persons and places (medical, religious, cultural).
- The law is not a constraint on winning — it is what separates a Soldier from a murderer and keeps the force legitimate in the eyes of the population.

## Warrior culture

- The Soldier's Creed and the Warrior Ethos: "I will always place the mission first. I will never accept defeat. I will never quit. I will never leave a fallen comrade."
- **Battle drill:** a collective action trained to automaticity. **Warrior Drills:** the individual versions — the actions a single Soldier performs without thinking when contact is made, a casualty falls, or a weapon stops. Drills are what survive fear.

## Chapter 2 — Individual readiness: outline

- Predeployment; legal assistance; personal weapon.

**Predeployment checklist:** personal affairs in order (will, powers of attorney, family care plan); financial affairs settled; medical and dental readiness complete; equipment issued and inspected; training current. **Legal assistance** is available before deployment — use it, because a Soldier who deploys with unsettled affairs fights with half his mind at home. **Personal weapon:** know it, clean it, zero it, carry it in a condition that lets it fire the moment it is needed.

## Chapter 3 — Combat care and preventive medicine: outline

Section I. Combat casualty care: combat lifesaver; lifesaving measures (first aid); casualty evacuation.
Section II. Preventive medicine: clothing and sleeping gear; mental health and morale.

## First aid — the priority order (Table 3-1)

1. **Check for BREATHING** — lack of oxygen, from a compromised airway or inadequate breathing, can cause brain damage or death in just a few minutes.
2. **Check for BLEEDING** — life continues only with sufficient blood to carry oxygen to tissues.
3. **Check for SHOCK** — unless shock is prevented, first aid performed, and medical treatment provided, death may result even from an otherwise nonfatal injury.

## Airway and breathing (paras 3-4 to 3-14)

- **Assess:** check responsiveness (gently shake, ask "Are you OK?"); call for help; position the unconscious casualty on his back on a firm surface.
- **The tongue is the single most common airway obstruction.** The **head-tilt/chin-lift** technique clears it in most cases — it pulls the tongue away from the air passage (Figures 3-2, 3-3).
- **Jaw-thrust** is the technique for a casualty with a suspected neck injury, because it can usually be done without moving the neck.
- After opening the airway, keep it open and check breathing by observing the chest. If the casualty resumes breathing, monitor and maintain the open airway. If the casualty fails to promptly resume adequate spontaneous breathing after the airway is open, rescue breathing is performed.
- **If the heart stops:** seek medical help immediately — seconds count. A casualty with no pulse is unconscious, limp, and has wide-open pupils. Stoppage of the heart is soon followed by cessation of respiration.

## Bleeding and shock

- **Tourniquet** (Figures 3-36, 3-38): applied above the knee for a leg wound; knotted over a rigid object and twisted to tighten. A tourniquet is the bleeding control of last resort in this manual's first-aid sequence — but it is the one that saves the life when direct pressure cannot.
- **Shock:** prevent it by controlling bleeding, keeping the casualty warm, and getting him to medical care. Shock kills after the wound stops bleeding.

## Casualty evacuation

- **Manual carries:** the two-hand seat carry and the other carries for moving a casualty without a litter, including transport over long distances (over 300 meters) by teams working in rotation.
- Evacuation is a team task — one Soldier cannot carry a casualty far, and a casualty left behind is a casualty lost.

## Preventive medicine

- **Clothing and sleeping gear:** the right layers, kept dry; the sleeping system used correctly. Wet clothing in the cold is a casualty in the making.
- **Mental health and morale:** treated as medical subjects. A Soldier who has lost his will to fight is a casualty just as surely as one who has lost blood. Leadership, rest, food, and contact with home are preventive medicine.

## Chapter 4 — Environmental conditions: outline

Section I. Desert: types; preparation. Section II. Jungle: types; preparation. Section III. Arctic: types; preparation.

## Desert

- **Types:** rocky, sandy, mountainous, coastal. Some areas contain sand dunes over 1,000 feet (300 meters) high and 10 to 15 miles (16 to 25 km) long.
- **Preparation:** water discipline is the whole game — find, carry, and conserve water. Heat injuries (heat cramps, heat exhaustion, heat stroke) kill faster than the enemy. Acclimatization, work-rest cycles, and salt replacement are the countermeasures.

## Jungle

- **Types:** rain forest, secondary jungle, swamp, savanna edge.
- **Preparation:** water purification, foot care (the jungle rots feet faster than any other environment — keep feet dry, change socks, treat blisters early), and the navigation problem of a canopy that hides the sun and the landmarks. Disease (malaria, dysentery, skin infection) is the jungle's main killer.

## Arctic

- **Types:** tundra, taiga, ice cap, mountain arctic.
- **Preparation:** cold injuries, wind chill, and the clothing and shelter discipline that keeps a Soldier alive below freezing. The companion skill `cold-weather-survival` (TC 21-3) is the deep treatment; this chapter is the individual primer — and the rule it shares with the cold-weather manual is the same: stay dry, stay fed, stay moving, and never let the cold get a foothold.
