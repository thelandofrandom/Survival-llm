# Sensor, Marksmanship, Comms, and SERE — TC 3-21.75, Chapters 9–13

## Chapter 9 — 'Every Soldier is a Sensor': outline

- Definition; resources; forms of questioning; report levels; SALUTE format; handling and reporting of the enemy; operations security; observation techniques; limited-visibility observation; range estimation.

## The idea

Every Soldier observes, collects, and reports. The unit's intelligence picture is built from the reports of individuals, not from sensors alone. A Soldier who sees something and does not report it has wasted the observation.

## Report levels

- **Level I:** the SALUTE spot report — the basic report, sent immediately.
- **Level II:** detailed report, with more information and context.
- **Level III:** full report, with sketches, overlays, and supporting detail.
- **Level IV:** analytical report, with assessment and implications.
The levels help the S-2 record and disseminate both important and subtle details for all-source analysis, future planning, and passing to higher headquarters.

## SALUTE format — full table (Table 9-1)

| Line | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | (S)ize / Who | Expressed as a quantity and echelon or size. Report "10 enemy infantrymen" (not "a rifle squad"). If multiple units are involved, make multiple entries |
| 2 | (A)ctivity / What | Related to the priority intelligence requirement. A concise bullet statement — e.g., "emplacing mines in the road" |
| 3 | (L)ocation / Where | Generally a grid coordinate, including the 100,000-meter grid zone designator. An address if appropriate, but still with an eight-digit grid. For movement, include "from" and "to" entries; the route used goes under Equipment/How |
| 4 | (U)nit / Who | The complete designation of a military unit; the name and other identifying information or features of civilians or insurgent groups |
| 5 | (T)ime / When | For future events, the date-time group for when the activity will initiate. Ongoing events reported as such. Report the time you **saw** the activity, not the time you report it. Always local or Zulu (Z) time |
| 6 | (E)quipment / How | What equipment the enemy has and how he is using it; the route for a moving enemy |

Reports should answer who, what, and where, after when. Make notes and draw sketches to help you remember details. Report all information about the enemy to your leader **quickly, accurately, and completely**.

## Handling and reporting of the enemy

Prisoners, defectors, and captured documents are reported and handled per the unit SOP and the law of war. They are intelligence, not trophies. Search, segregate, silence, speed, safeguard — the five S's of handling prisoners.

## Operations security

What you report, and how, is itself information the enemy wants. Do not report over unsecured means what the enemy can use; do not discuss operations where they can be overheard; and remember that the enemy is listening to your radio, watching your signals, and reading your trash.

## Observation techniques

- **Scanning:** systematic observation of a sector, in overlapping strips, near to far.
- **Limited-visibility observation:** at night and in bad weather, use sound, movement, and the edges of your vision (the center of the eye is blind at night — look slightly away from the object). Night-vision devices extend the range but narrow the field of view.
- **Observation positions:** pick a position with a good field of view, good cover, and a covered route in and out.

## Range estimation — full treatment (paras 9-33 to 9-38)

**Methods:** the 100-meter unit-of-measure method; the appearance-of-objects method; the flash-and-sound method; the mil-relation method; a combination of these.

**100-meter unit-of-measure (para 9-34):** picture a distance of 100 meters on the ground. For ranges up to 500 meters, count the number of 100-meter lengths between the two points. Beyond 500 meters, pick a point halfway to the target, count the 100-meter lengths to the halfway point, and double. Accuracy depends on how much ground is visible — if you can only see part of the ground to a target 500 meters or more away, the method is hard to use accurately.

**Appearance of objects (para 9-35, Table 9-2):** know the characteristic details of objects at known ranges, then compare. Anything that limits visibility (weather, smoke, darkness) limits the method.

| Range (m) | What you see on a human body |
|---|---|
| 200 | Clear in all detail — equipment, skin color |
| 300 | Clear body outline, face color good, remaining detail blurred |
| 400 | Body outline clear, other details blurred |
| 500 | Body tapered, head indistinct from body |
| 600 | Body a wedge shape, with no head apparent |
| 700 | Solid wedge shape (body outline) |

**Flash-and-sound (para 9-36):** best at night. Sound travels at 1,100 feet (300 meters) per second. When you see the flash or smoke of a weapon, or the dust it raises, start counting immediately; stop when you hear the sound. **Multiply the count by three** — the result is the approximate distance in hundreds of meters. Count 1 = ~300 m; count 3 = ~900 m. If you must count higher than nine, start over at one each time you hit nine — counting higher throws the timing off.

**Mil-relation (para 9-37):** the easiest and best way. At 1,000 meters, a 1-mil angle equals 1 meter (wide or high). **Range (m) = [estimated height of target (m) ÷ size of target (mils)] × 1,000.** Example: a 2-meter-tall target measuring 4 mils in the binocular reticle is (2 ÷ 4) × 1,000 = 500 meters away.

**Combination (para 9-38):** battlefield conditions are rarely ideal. If terrain limits the 100-meter method and haze limits the appearance method, use both and compare. If they agree, the figure is close to true.

## Chapter 10 — Combat marksmanship: outline

- Safety; administrative procedures; weapons; fire control; combat zero; mechanical zero; battlesight zero; shot groups; borelight zero; misfire procedures and immediate action; reflexive fire.

**Safety and administrative procedures:** treat every weapon as loaded; never point at anything you do not intend to shoot; keep the finger off the trigger until ready to fire; be sure of the target and what is beyond it. Clearing, loading, and unloading procedures are done the same way every time.

**Fire control:** when to shoot (on command, or on the appearance of a target, per the rules of engagement); what to shoot at (the most dangerous target first); how to distribute fire (across the enemy formation, not all on one man).

**Zeros:**
- **Combat zero:** the zero for the fight you are in — set for the expected engagement range and conditions.
- **Mechanical zero:** the sight's centered starting point. M16A2/A3 (Figure 10-13) and M16A4/M4 (Figure 10-14) have their own procedures — the windage and elevation are centered before the live zero begins.
- **Battlesight zero:** the zero that lets you hit a man-sized target from point-blank to 300 meters without adjusting the sights. M16A2/A3 (Figure 10-15), M16A4 (Figure 10-16), M4 (Figure 10-17). The battlesight zero is the default for combat — it trades precision at one range for hits at every range.
- **Shot groups:** the diagnostic. A tight group in the wrong place is a zero problem — adjust the sights. A scattered group is a fundamentals problem — fix the shooter, not the sights.
- **Borelight zero:** the zero done with a laser boresight when live fire is not possible. It gets the weapon close; live fire confirms it.

**Misfire procedures and immediate action:** SPORTS — **S**lap the magazine, **P**ull the charging handle, **O**bserve the chamber, **R**elease the charging handle, **T**ap the forward assist, **S**hoot. Drilled until it is a reflex, because a stoppage in a fight is a death sentence if the response is thinking instead of doing.

**Reflexive fire:** the close-range engagement technique — weapon up, eyes on the target, fire on the move. Aimed fire is for distance; reflexive fire is for the room and the trench.

## Chapter 11 — Communications: outline

Section I. Means of communications: messengers; wire; visual signals; sound; radio.
Section II. Radiotelephone procedures: rules; types of nets; precedence of reports; message format; common messages; prowords; operation on a net.
Section III. Communications security: classifications; signal operating instructions; automated net control device.
Section IV. Equipment: radios; wire; telephone equipment.

**Means, in order of reliability when the enemy is jamming you:** messengers (slow, secure), wire (secure, fragile), visual signals (fast, short-range, interceptable), sound (short-range, interceptable), radio (fast, long-range, jammable and interceptable). The companion skills `visual-signals` (TC 3-21.60) and `radio-operator` (MCRP 3-40.3B) go deep.

**Radiotelephone procedures:** the rules of the net (who talks, when, and how); types of nets (command, admin/logistics, fire support); precedence of reports (routine, priority, immediate, flash — the flash report interrupts everything); message format; common messages (contact report, situation report, spot report); prowords (the standard words that mean the same thing on every net — "over," "out," "roger," "wilco," "say again," "wait"); operation on a net (entering, leaving, and the discipline of keeping transmissions short).

**Communications security:** classifications (what can be said on which net); signal operating instructions (the SOI — frequencies, call signs, challenges and passwords); automated net control device (the ANCD — the electronic fill device that loads the SOI into the radio).

## Chapter 12 — Survival, evasion, resistance, and escape: outline

- Survival; evasion; resistance; escape.

## SURVIVAL — the full acrostic (para 12-1, Figures 12-1, 12-2)

| Letter | Meaning | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| S | Size up the situation | Surroundings, physical condition, equipment. Conceal yourself; security is key. Determine if the enemy is attacking, defending, or withdrawing. Plan around water, food, and shelter. Check wounds and give first aid; drink water; put on clothing against the cold |
| U | Use all your senses | Undue haste makes waste. Note sounds, smells, temperature changes. Stay observant; act carefully. An unplanned action can result in capture or death. Plan your moves; stay ready to move quickly without endangering yourself |
| R | Remember where you are | Find who has a map or compass; keep track of your location relative to enemy units, friendly units, water sources, and cover and concealment. Rely on yourself to keep track of your route |
| V | Vanquish fear and panic | Your greatest enemies. Uncontrolled, they destroy decision-making and drain energy. Control them with self-confidence and training |
| I | Improvise | Use a tool designed for one purpose for another; use natural objects (a rock for a hammer). Save survival-kit items for when you have no other options |
| V | Value living | Maintain a high value on living. Perseverance — refusal to give in — gives the mental and physical strength to endure |
| A | Act like the natives | Locals and animals have adapted to the environment. Observe their routines — where they get food and water, when they eat, when they sleep. The answers help you avoid capture |
| L | Live by your wits, but for now, learn basic skills | Learn the skills before you need them |

**The three-phase individual survival kit:**
- **Phase 1 — on your person (no equipment):** the items you carry in your pockets and on your body, that you have even if you lose everything else.
- **Phase 2 — load-bearing equipment:** a small survival kit in your web gear.
- **Phase 3 — rucksack:** the larger kit for sustained survival.

**Evasion:** the movement discipline for staying free — routes, shelters, and the tool for remembering shelter locations (Figure 12-2). Move at night, rest in the day, avoid roads and populated areas, and never use the same route twice. **Resistance:** the Code of Conduct (Figure 12-3), six articles: (I) I am an American, fighting in the forces which guard my country and our way of life. I am prepared to give my life in their defense. (II) I will never surrender of my own free will. If in command, I will never surrender the members of my command while they still have the means to resist. (III) If I am captured, I will continue to resist by all means available. I will make every effort to escape and aid others to escape. I will accept neither parole nor special favors from the enemy. (IV) If I become a prisoner of war, I will keep faith with my fellow prisoners. I will give no information or take part in any action which might be harmful to my comrades. (V) When questioned, should I become a prisoner of war, I am required to give name, rank, service number, and date of birth. I will evade answering further questions to the utmost of my ability. (VI) I will never forget that I am an American, fighting for freedom, responsible for my actions, and dedicated to the principles which made my country free. I will trust in my God and in the United States of America. **Escape:** the planning and execution of getting out — deliberately general in the manual, because every escape is specific to its circumstances.

## Chapter 13 — CBRN weapons: outline

Section I. Chemical weapons: types; detection; protective actions; protective equipment.
(Sections II–IV cover biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons on the same pattern: detect, protect, decontaminate, report.)

## Chemical agents

| Agent type | Effects | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Nerve agents | Act quickly — effects in seconds, minutes, or hours after exposure | Mask immediately; self-aid with the antidote (see below) |
| Blister agents | Burning sensations in the skin, eyes, and nose | Decontaminate eyes and skin at once |
| Blood agents | Interfere with the blood's ability to carry oxygen | Mask; evacuate to medical care |
| Choking agents | Damage the lungs; delayed effects | Mask; rest; evacuate |
| Incapacitating agents | Disable without killing | Mask; report; evacuate |

**Nerve agent antidote:** atropine and 2-PAM, carried as the Mark I kit (two autoinjectors) or the ATNAA (antidote treatment, nerve agent, autoinjector — a single injector delivering both drugs). **Self-aid:** if you have symptoms of nerve agent poisoning, inject one NAAK (nerve agent antidote kit) in your thigh. If symptoms persist, inject a second. The manual warns that atropine and 2-PAM alone often do not prevent severe and long-lasting convulsions after nerve agent exposure — which is why the convulsant antidote for nerve agents (CANA, diazepam) is also carried, to control nerve-agent-induced seizures. Two NAAKs are carried as the standard load.

**Detection:** the M256 chemical-agent detector kit, M8/M9 detector paper, and the other detectors in the Soldier's load. **Protective actions:** mask first — by the time you smell a chemical agent, you are already exposed. MOPP levels (mission-oriented protective posture) trade protection against heat and fatigue. **Protective equipment:** the protective mask, the JSLIST suit, gloves, and boots. **Decontamination:** skin decontamination kit, personal decontamination, and unit decontamination after exposure. **Biological, radiological, and nuclear weapons** follow the same pattern — detect, protect, decontaminate, report — with the added rule for nuclear weapons that time, distance, and shielding are the three protections, and for radiological weapons that the hazard is invisible and the detector is the only warning.
