# Environment, Personnel, Equipment — MCWP 3-35.6 (Chapter 1)

## The desert as a battlefield

Adaptation to the environment is required; equipment and tactics must be modified for a dusty, rugged landscape with temperatures from extreme highs to freezing. If the desert floor will not support wheeled traffic, roads and tracks become key terrain and crossroads are vital — desert warfare is often a battle for control of the lines of communication (LOC): protect your own LOC while interdicting the enemy's. Water sources are vital, especially without long-distance resupply. Defiles matter: in the Western Desert of Libya, a coastal escarpment was a barrier except through a few passes, and control of those passes was vital; similar escarpments exist in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait.

## Three types of desert terrain

- **Mountain deserts:** scattered barren ranges/hills separated by dry flat basins; high ground several thousand feet up; infrequent rain runs off as flash floods, eroding gullies; shallow salt lakes (Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea) where water accumulates.
- **Rocky plateau deserts:** extensive flat areas with solid or broken rock at or near the surface, cut by steep-walled wadis, gulches, canyons; narrow valleys extremely dangerous from flash flooding though their flat bottoms look attractive as assembly areas. Examples: National Training Center, Golan Heights.
- **Sandy or dune deserts:** sand or gravel flats; dunes over **1,000 feet high** and **10–15 miles long**; trafficability depends on dune gradients and sand texture; other areas totally flat for 3,000 meters and beyond; plant life from none to scrub over 6 feet high. Examples: Sahara ergs, Empty Quarter, California/New Mexico, Kalahari.

## Effects on personnel

Heat exhaustion and heatstroke are the central threats. **Acclimatization:** significant in **4–5 days**, full in **7–14 days**, with **2–3 hours/day of exercise in the heat** and gradually increasing activity. Acclimatization does **not** reduce water requirements — it may increase them. Dehydration nullifies acclimatization and fitness. A man can be ~2 quarts below ideal hydration without any sense of thirst.

**Water rules:** one quart in the morning, at each meal, and before strenuous work; in hot climates **at least one quart per hour**, over **two quarts per hour** at higher temperatures. Loss of 2 quarts (2.5% of body weight) decreases efficiency. Heat exhaustion is the collapse of the body's cooling mechanism progressing toward **heatstroke — the most dangerous condition, potentially fatal**. Work/rest cycles and enforced water consumption are command responsibilities. Constant wind noise increases fatigue. (For scale: a camel regains weight by drinking up to **27 gallons / 120 liters** at a time; a 3½-foot barrel cactus yields about a quart of milky juice; a survival still uses a 2- to 4-quart container.)

## Effects on equipment

Sand temperatures reach **165°F** — increase the PLL of tires and tracks. Extra fan belts and tires; service tires and fuel in the heat of day and you get low tire pressure, overheated tires, under-filled tanks — check tire pressure when cool. Change battery specific gravity for the desert (wrong charge can cause a battery to explode); increase dry-battery supplies for heat attrition. Lubricants must be the correct viscosity and kept clean — diluted lubricants cool less effectively and evaporate faster. Blown sand degrades optics gradually; dust buildup may not show until low-light performance has severely deteriorated — keep optics covered; reflections reveal positions. Sand is extremely effective for cleaning weapons and optics. Condensation affects items stored cooler than air temperature; bare metal rusts, mold grows in enclosed spaces.
