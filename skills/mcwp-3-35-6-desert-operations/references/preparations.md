# Preparations — MCWP 3-35.6 (Chapter 2)

A force sent to a desert theater fights with the equipment it has, per current doctrine; equipment and doctrine can be modified, but success depends on mastered individual training. Well-trained troops adapt quickly; "if their individual and collective skills have been neglected, no amount of desert lore will remedy the situation."

## The commander's questions

On alert, answer: to what country is the unit going? Climatic and terrain conditions? Will the unit take its own equipment overseas? Timeline — time for individual training while vehicles are in transit? What equipment is being sent, what needs modification (including camouflage painting), what special equipment and maintenance are required? Personnel with desert experience (observers/controllers, any desert experience)? All personnel physically fit? How many nondeployable? What types of operations are expected? Then build a program with training priorities to reach full capability in harsh desert conditions.

## Individual training

Field training should accustom troops to hardship: minimum contact with garrison/urban areas except for medical or welfare reasons; supplies brought to field locations; commercial soft drinks and foods not permitted once field training starts. Enemy organizations and tactics: sand tables and map maneuvers in garrison, tactical exercises without troops, unit field exercises; enemy equipment brought in for firsthand study when available.

## Desert navigation

Brief troops on: water sources, if any; landmarks and permanent terrain features; friendly and enemy areas of operation; prevailing winds; whether the local populace is pro or con the United States. Maps are the most obvious aid — obtain operational-area maps early for study. Some use foreign terrain terms (sebhka, summan, hidiba, dikaka) and non-UTM grids: the **Palestine grid**, used in much of the Middle East, surveyed during World War II and the following decade, in 1:100,000 or 1:50,000 scale, does not mesh with standard U.S. maps. In some instances accurate maps may not be available at all.
