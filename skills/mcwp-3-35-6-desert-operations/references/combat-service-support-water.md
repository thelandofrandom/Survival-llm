# Combat Service Support and Water — MCWP 3-35.6 (Chapter 4, Appendices)

## Combat service support (Chapter 4)

Every kind of resource is lacking in the desert, especially ports and railways for high-capacity movement. A unit's tactical effectiveness depends largely on available combat service support; its vulnerability lies in exposed lines of communication and immobile supply bases. Greater maneuver distances complicate supply; local water is short; sand and dust damage raises maintenance requirements; extended supply lines for expanded frontages need special procedures for adequate, timely resupply. Covers: base development plan, theater support, corps and division support commands, combat service support element, support operations, security of supply routes, supply, maintenance, personnel support, health services, naval and Air Force assistance.

## Appendix G — water usage

**Priority order for water use:** (1) personnel, drinking only; (2) medical treatment; (3) vehicle and equipment cooling systems; (4) personnel, uses other than drinking; (5) decontamination; (6) food preparation; (7) laundry; (8) construction.

**How much:** an acclimatized soldier/Marine needs as much (if not more) water as a non-acclimatized one; more than **3 gallons of drinking water per man per day** may be needed; **9 quarts per day to survive, but 5 gallons are recommended**. The biggest leadership problem is hoarding — troops who know how valuable water is may not drink enough. Before activity, have them drink as much as they can; during activity, make them replace sweat losses. Thirst is a poor indicator.

**Work in the heat:** maximum heavy work — **5 hours/day in heat condition BLACK** (on a 20-minute work/40-minute rest cycle, 5 hours of work takes 15 hours); **6 hours in RED, 7 hours in YELLOW, 8 hours in GREEN**. Troops in enclosed vehicles (tanks, APCs) face air 20–30°F hotter — drink as if two or three heat conditions higher. Light duty in the shade needs less water and rest. Exploit cooler times of day — early morning, late evening, night — for productivity, water savings, and morale.

**Effects of water loss:** after ~**2 quarts (2.5–3.0% of body weight)**, effectiveness is impaired — stumbling, fatigue, inability to concentrate, headaches; thirst present but not overpowering, and troops may not replace the loss unless trained or reminded. Further dehydration brings heat cramps and heat exhaustion (treatable, casualty returned to duty in a few days) and **heatstroke — fatal without prompt medical attention**, and even a survivor will probably not return to duty. Preventing casualties is much easier than treating them.

**Water on an NBC battlefield (Appendix G/D):** needs rise dramatically — decontamination consumes large quantities, and being "buttoned up" in vehicles or NBC gear locks in heat and sweat, raising drinking-water needs.
