# MCWP 3-35.6 Desert Operations (2004)

## Purpose

MCWP 3-35.6 (formerly FMFM 7-27), *Desert Operations* (U.S. Marine Corps, 15 September 2004, 223 pages; PCN 143 000126 00) — the Marine Corps' desert operations manual, built on the Army's FM 90-3 text (the running headers in the document still read "FM 90-3," and a 15 September 2004 erratum reidentifies the publication's short title from FMFM 7-27 to MCWP 3-35.6). It covers the desert environment and its effects on personnel and equipment, preparations for desert operations, operations in desert conditions, and combat service support — with appendices on desert countries, aircraft employment, operating equipment in the desert, NBC weapons effects, concealment and camouflage, mountain operations, water usage, and fratricide reduction. Its examples run from Rommel and Montgomery in North Africa to Operation Desert Storm.

**Currency warning — read this first.** This is a 2004 manual. The current Marine desert reference is **MCTP 12-10D, *Desert Operations* (2021)**, already a skill in this vault as `mctp-12-10d-desert-operations` — use that skill for current doctrine, and this one for the 2004 treatment, which is fuller on the environment, on water discipline, and on the Desert Storm experience. Where the two disagree on a procedure or a number, follow MCTP 12-10D.

## 1. The environment and its effects on personnel and equipment (Chapter 1)

**The desert as a battlefield.** Successful desert operations require adaptation to the environment and to the limits its terrain and climate impose; equipment and tactics must be modified for a dusty, rugged landscape where temperatures vary from extreme highs down to freezing and visibility can change rapidly. If the desert floor will not support wheeled traffic, the few roads and tracks become key terrain, and crossroads are vital — desert warfare is often a battle for control of the lines of communication (LOC): the side that protects its own LOC while interdicting the enemy's prevails. Water sources are vital, especially if a force cannot resupply water over long distances. Defiles matter where they exist — in the Western Desert of Libya, an escarpment paralleling the coast was a barrier to movement except through a few passes, and control of those passes was vital; similar escarpments are found in Saudi Arabia and Kuwait.

**Three types of desert terrain.** **Mountain deserts:** scattered ranges or barren hills separated by dry, flat basins; high ground rising gradually or abruptly several thousand feet; infrequent rain falling on high ground and running off as flash floods, eroding deep gullies and depositing sand and gravel; shallow salt lakes (Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea) where water accumulates. **Rocky plateau deserts:** extensive flat areas with solid or broken rock at or near the surface, cut by steep-walled wadis, gulches, or canyons; narrow valleys are extremely dangerous from flash flooding, though their flat bottoms look attractive as assembly areas — the National Training Center and the Golan Heights are examples. **Sandy or dune deserts:** extensive flat areas covered with sand or gravel; dunes can be over 1,000 feet high and 10–15 miles long, and trafficability depends on the windward/leeward gradients and the texture of the sand; other areas are totally flat for 3,000 meters and beyond; plant life ranges from none to scrub over 6 feet high — the ergs of the Sahara, the Empty Quarter of Arabia, areas of California and New Mexico, and the Kalahari.

**Effects on personnel.** The extreme heat can cause heat exhaustion and heatstroke and puts a premium on hydration, activity management, and acclimatization. **Acclimatization:** significant acclimatization can be attained in **4–5 days**, but full acclimatization takes **7–14 days**, with **2–3 hours per day of exercise in the heat** and gradually increasing physical activity. Acclimatization does **not** reduce water requirements — it may increase them, since the acclimatized man sweats more readily. Dehydration nullifies the benefits of acclimatization and physical fitness. A man can be about 2 quarts below ideal hydration status without any sense of thirst. **The water rules:** drink one quart of water in the morning, at each meal, and before strenuous work; in hot climates drink **at least one quart of water each hour**, and at higher temperatures hourly requirements rise to **over two quarts**. A loss of 2 quarts of body fluid (2.5 percent of body weight) decreases efficiency. Heat exhaustion is the general collapse of the body's cooling mechanism progressing toward **heatstroke, the most dangerous condition, potentially fatal**. Work/rest cycles and enforced water consumption are command responsibilities. Constant wind noise is tiresome and increases fatigue. For scale, the manual notes a camel regains lost weight by drinking up to **27 gallons (120 liters)** of water at a time — and, for the survivor, that a barrel cactus 3½ feet high yields about a quart of milky juice, and a cheap survival still built around a 2- to 4-quart container can produce drinking water.

**Effects on equipment.** Sand temperatures reach **165 degrees**; increase the unit's prescribed load list (PLL) of tires and tracks accordingly. Vehicles need extra fan belts and tires; air and fluids expand and contract with temperature — service tires and fuel in the heat of the day and you get low tire pressure, overheated tires, and under-filled tanks; check tire pressure when cool. Change battery specific gravity for the desert (an incorrect charge can cause a battery to explode) and increase dry-battery supplies to offset high heat attrition. Lubricants must be the correct viscosity for the temperature and kept clean — diluted lubricants cool less effectively and evaporate at higher temperatures. Blown sand degrades optics gradually, and dust buildup on optics may not be apparent until low-light performance has severely deteriorated; keep optics covered when not in use, and remember that reflections from optics reveal positions. Sand is extremely effective at cleaning weapons and optics. Condensation affects optics and other items stored cooler than the air temperature; bare metal rusts and mold grows in enclosed spaces. → `references/environment-personnel-equipment.md`

## 2. Preparations for desert operations (Chapter 2)

**The commander's questions.** A force sent to a desert theater will fight with the equipment it has, in accordance with current doctrine; equipment and doctrine can be modified, but much depends on how well soldiers/Marines and leaders have mastered their individual training. Units that trained in Germany and the United States have the basic technical and tactical skills that can be adapted; well-trained troops adapt quickly, and "if their individual and collective skills have been neglected, no amount of desert lore will remedy the situation." When alerted, the commander must answer: to what country is the unit going? What are the climatic and terrain conditions? Will the unit take its own equipment overseas? What is the timeline — is there time for individual training while vehicles are in transit? What equipment is being sent, what needs modification (including camouflage painting), what special equipment and special maintenance are required? Are there personnel with desert experience as observers or controllers, or any desert experience at all? Are all personnel physically fit, and how many are nondeployable? What types of operations are expected? Then build a program, with training priorities as a guide, to bring the unit to full capability in harsh desert conditions.

**Individual training.** Field training should accustom troops to hardship: contact with garrison or urban areas kept to a minimum except for medical or welfare reasons, supplies brought to field locations, and commercial soft drinks and foods not permitted once field training starts. Enemy organizations and tactics are taught in garrison on sand tables and map maneuvers, followed by tactical exercises without troops and unit field exercises — with enemy equipment brought in for firsthand study when available. **Desert navigation** is a training priority: troops must be briefed on water sources, landmarks and permanent terrain features, friendly and enemy areas of operation, prevailing winds, and whether the local populace is pro or con the United States. Maps are the most obvious aid, but operational-area maps must be obtained early for study — some use foreign terrain terms (sebhka, summan, hidiba, dikaka) and non-UTM grids (the Palestine grid, used in much of the Middle East, surveyed during World War II and the following decade, in 1:100,000 or 1:50,000 scale, which does not mesh with standard U.S. maps), and in some instances accurate maps may not be available at all. → `references/preparations.md`

## 3. Operations in desert conditions (Chapter 3)

**Mobility is the key.** The tactics that won in Desert Storm were wide and rapid flanking movements, similar to Rommel's and Montgomery's in North Africa. Trafficability is generally good and cross-country movement a lesser problem — but not always: salt marshes create NO-GO conditions in the rainy season; sand bogs down traffic and makes foot movement slow and exhausting; steep dune slopes and rocky mountains can make vehicular movement NO-GO; wadis cross-compartment the terrain, their banks steep and unconsolidated, and when it rains they become torrents of rushing water leading to flat lake beds that turn to NO-GO mud; rock quarries and mining areas, often unmarked on maps, can also affect mobility (satellite imagery helped identify them in Kuwait). In rocky terrain, sharp angular debris easily punctures tires; given ample fuel, water, and resources, units can go around natural and man-made obstacles.

**Detection and night movement.** Movement is easily detected because of sand and dust signatures in the loose surface — in an engagement this may obscure the unit from direct fire while advancing, but the element of surprise may be lost. Moving at night becomes the logical choice. Reconnaissance must be tempered with deception: extensive reconnaissance in one area can alert the enemy to intended operations there, and reconnaissance may itself serve as a deceptive measure to draw attention from the real objective.

**Offensive operations.** Concentrate overwhelming combat power — mass achieved in time and space: units must rapidly concentrate at a given time and place and then disperse just as rapidly to avoid offering a lucrative target. Concentration means the ability to place an overwhelming concentration of fires on the enemy, not necessarily massing men and vehicles in a small area. Mutual support matters as much in the desert as in temperate climates, but over large maneuver distances it means the capability to maneuver in support without disrupting the scheme of maneuver. Movement for concentration should take place at night or in limited visibility whenever possible, and deception "cannot be overemphasized" in an environment of negligible concealment. The attacker must suppress enemy weapon and surveillance systems; attack helicopters and high-performance aircraft are extremely useful for maneuvering firepower over a large battlefield quickly, so suppression of enemy air defense has high priority, as does destruction of enemy antitank capability — no target with a long-range antitank capability should be disregarded. In featureless terrain, shock, overwhelm, and destroy demand accurate reconnaissance to identify actual positions from false ones, and excellent navigation so the commander can be certain of his location. **Defensive operations** (Section III) apply the same logic in reverse: the defender's objective is to stop and destroy the attacking force by direct and indirect fires, obstacles, and counterattacks. → `references/operations.md`

## 4. Combat service support (Chapter 4)

There is a lack of every kind of resource in the desert, especially the sophisticated infrastructure of ports and railways for high-capacity movement of combat supplies. Logistical support is always a challenge, and an arid environment burdens all types — supply, aviation, communications, and maintenance. A unit's tactical effectiveness depends to a large degree on the combat service support available, and its vulnerability lies in its exposed lines of communication and the immobility of its bases of supply and support. Greater maneuver distances complicate supply procedures; locally available water is short; sand and dust damage increases maintenance requirements; and extended supply lines for expanded frontages demand special procedures so that adequate, timely supplies arrive. The chapter covers the base development plan, theater support, corps and division support commands, the combat service support element, support operations, security of supply routes, supply, maintenance, personnel support, health services, and naval and Air Force assistance. → `references/combat-service-support-water.md`

## Appendices — water, aircraft, equipment, NBC, camouflage, fratricide

**Appendix G — water usage** is the manual's most important appendix and is treated in full in the references. Its priority order for water use: (1) personnel, drinking only; (2) medical treatment; (3) vehicle and equipment cooling systems; (4) personnel, uses other than drinking; (5) decontamination; (6) food preparation; (7) laundry; (8) construction. Water needs: an acclimatized soldier/Marine needs as much (if not more) water as a non-acclimatized one; more than 3 gallons of drinking water per man per day may be needed; **9 quarts of water per day to survive, but 5 gallons are recommended**. The biggest water-related leadership problem is hoarding: troops who recognize how valuable water is may not drink enough — before activity, have them drink as much as they can, and during activity, take positive steps to make them replace sweat losses. Thirst is a poor indicator.

**Work in the heat.** Men cannot routinely perform more than about **5 hours of heavy work per day in heat condition BLACK** — and on a 20-minute work/40-minute rest cycle, 5 hours of work takes 15 hours. Maximum heavy work: **6 hours in RED, 7 hours in YELLOW, 8 hours in GREEN**. Troops in enclosed vehicles (tanks, APCs) face air temperatures 20–30°F higher than outside — increase water intake to that for two or three heat conditions higher. Troops on light duty in the shade need less water and less rest. Work schedules should exploit the cooler times of day — early morning, late evening, and night — which increase productivity, reduce water use, and are easier on morale.

**Effects of water loss.** After a loss of about **2 quarts (2.5–3.0 percent of body weight)**, effectiveness is impaired — stumbling, fatigue, inability to concentrate, headaches; thirst is present but not overpowering, and unless well trained or reminded, troops may not replace the loss. As dehydration continues, heat cramps and heat exhaustion develop — treatable with good success, the casualty returned to duty in a few days — but **without prompt medical attention, heatstroke can be fatal**, and even a survivor will probably not return to duty. Preventing casualties is much easier than treating them.

**Other appendices.** Appendix A lists the desert countries of the world; Appendix B covers employment of aircraft in desert operations; Appendix C gives techniques for operating equipment in the desert; Appendix D covers effects of the environment on NBC weapons (water needs rise dramatically on a nuclear or chemical battlefield — decontamination consumes large quantities, and being "buttoned up" in vehicles or NBC gear locks in heat and sweat); Appendix E covers desert concealment and camouflage; Appendix F covers operations in mountains (described in detail in FM 90-6); Appendix H covers fratricide reduction — "the employment of friendly weapons against friendly forces," a problem as old as warfare itself. → `references/combat-service-support-water.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Water is the mission.** Potable drinking water is the single most important need; never mistake nonpotable water for drinking water, and use only government-issued containers.
2. **Drink on schedule, not on thirst.** A man can be 2 quarts down with no sense of thirst; enforce drinking and work/rest cycles.
3. **Acclimatize or pay for it.** 4–5 days for significant, 7–14 for full acclimatization — and it does not reduce water needs.
4. **Move at night.** Dust signatures betray daylight movement; night is the logical choice.
5. **Protect the LOC.** Desert warfare is a battle for lines of communication; exposed supply lines are the vulnerability.
6. **Current doctrine governs.** MCTP 12-10D (2021) is the current Marine desert reference; MCWP 3-35.6 (2004) is the deep historical treatment.
