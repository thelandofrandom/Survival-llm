# FM 9-207 Cold Weather Ordnance

## Purpose

FM 9-207/TO 36-1-40, *Operations and Maintenance of Ordnance Materiel in Cold Weather* (Headquarters, Department of the Army and Department of the Air Force, 20 March 1998, 113 pages) is the Army and Air Force guide to keeping vehicles, weapons, and communications equipment working in extreme cold — sustained operations in temperatures as low as **−65 °F**. It familiarizes operators and crews with the special procedures and techniques for working in sub-zero temperatures, and helps unit commanders, supervisors (motor sergeants, battalion motor officers), and maintenance personnel anticipate and plan for the onset of cold weather or an arctic deployment. Six chapters and five appendices: cold weather considerations; preparation for operation; operating and maintenance procedures; weapons, munitions, and fire control equipment; small arms; communication and information systems equipment — plus appendices on cold-weather POL products and materials, special materials, environmental protection, a sample cold-weather supplement to the unit maintenance SOP, and NBC defense in cold conditions.

**Status note — read this first.** FM 9-207 is a 1998 manual and supersedes the 10 August 1989 edition. It does **not** replace the applicable technical manuals (TMs) for specific equipment operations, servicing, or repair — it is an overview of the special difficulties of cold weather, and the operator's TM is the authority for any specific item. Lubricant, antifreeze, and battery specifications and NSNs have changed since 1998: verify every product, specification, and part number against the current TM and lubrication order (LO) for the specific equipment before using it. Much of the manual's information came from the USA Cold Regions Test Activity and the USA Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory. The proponent is Headquarters, U.S. Army Training and Doctrine Command (TRADOC). The vault lists this manual's title as "Ordonance" — a vault typo; the manual is "Ordnance".

The manual's framing warning: in a cold region, a force faces two enemies — the tactical enemy and the environment, which "aggressively attacks and can destroy equipment and men." Failure to follow the guidance can cause injury to personnel and damage to equipment, besides limiting mission accomplishment.

## 1. The Cold Weather Challenge
**Levels of operation.** Operating equipment at temperatures no lower than **10 °F** presents few problems — like winter in the northern continental United States. From **10 °F to −25 °F**, operations become more difficult; nonacclimated troops have difficulty even above −10 °F. Below **−25 °F**, operations become increasingly difficult. Nearing **−50 °F** and lower, even the simplest tasks with completely winterized materiel require the maximum efforts of well-trained personnel.

**Personnel.** Most U.S. units are stationed in temperate or tropical climates, so soldiers generally lack cold-weather training and experience. Self-preservation in cold weather eats time and energy and reduces efficiency. Unit leaders must ensure personnel and equipment can withstand the cold. Historical examples (Napoleon, Germany in Russia, UN forces in Korea) → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`
## 2. Effects on Materiel
**Metals.** Metals become brittle in severe cold and cannot withstand the shock loads they sustain at higher temperatures. At **−20 °F**, certain steels can withstand only **50 percent** of the shock load they sustain at room temperature.

**Rubber.** Natural rubber gradually stiffens as it cools but retains much elasticity until below **−20 °F**; cooled gradually, it remains flexible until about **−60 °F**, then quickly loses elasticity and becomes brittle — and kept at −20 °F for a long time, crystallization makes it brittle. Fueling hoses may crack, increasing the potential for spills. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`

**Tires.** Tires become rigid in cold, causing flat spots during shutdown. A tire inflated to **40 PSI indoors will change to 25 PSI when moved outside at −50 °F**. Generally, inflate tires **10 PSI over the normal pressure** for winter operations. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`

**Plastics, glass, and fabrics.** Plastics expand and contract much more than metal or glass, making them brittle in cold weather; vehicular canvas covers with plastic windows may break from cold and vibration.

**Effects on mission functions.** **Movement/maneuver:** units are less maneuverable in icy conditions or deep snow; deep snow reduces vehicle traction on hills and raises breakdown/damage risk from hidden obstacles; motorized units are restricted almost exclusively to roads; personnel tend to operate close to vehicles, which is tactically unwise; trailers and towed artillery further reduce mobility; emplacement of artillery for fire missions is very difficult. **Rubber-covered cables:** handle with extreme care at low temperatures; if jackets harden, protect from shock loads and bending to prevent short circuits; warm cables before bending. **Ammunition:** increased need in cold weather — cold degrades firing, responsiveness, effectiveness, and accuracy; fire several light- to medium-zone rounds before maximum propellant charges to avoid stressing howitzer recoil mechanisms. **Fuel/lubrication:** expect a dramatic increase in POL and fuel requirements (movement difficulty, extended idling, heating); use special fuel and lubricants to prevent freezing and jelling. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`
## 3. Logistics, Maintenance, and Recovery
**Logistics.** Vast distances, climatic or terrain obstacles, or both often separate combat service support (CSS) elements from supported forces, so logistical planning must be continuous and aggressive, using all modes of transportation. Unit distribution is the rule, not the exception. Economic order quantities (EOQ) and ammunition required supply rates (RSR) should be computed on a seasonal basis rather than on the standard 180-day demand criterion. Below 32 °F, keep water and liquids (especially medical items) liquid and provide heated storage for power sources, command centers, dining facilities, aid stations, maintenance shelters, and critical supplies (medicines, batteries, POL).

Remote cold regions present an unrelenting hostile climate, formidable terrain, great distances, water obstacles, movement problems, poor communications, low population densities, and a lack of shelter and resources. **Planning.** Plans must be both detailed and flexible; personnel must be properly trained and prepared. Anticipate more medical problems (frostbite and hypothermia), increased psychological stress, more time for all tasks, higher fuel consumption and battery demand, less reliable equipment, security problems, harder command and control, and flank units working harder to keep up and requiring frequent rotation. **Host nation support:** host nation personnel are critical to identifying sources of food, shelter, warmth, supplies, and services, and should identify specific environmental restrictions pertinent to cold weather operations (chemical substitutions, spill response, and corrective actions). → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`

**Maintenance.** The importance of maintenance, especially preventive maintenance checks and services (PMCS), must be impressed on all cold region soldiers. Below −20 °F, maintenance may take **five times as long**. Without heaters, maintenance man-hours increase **25 percent to 200 percent** above normal.

**Maintenance facilities and carbon monoxide.** Heated buildings or shelters are necessary for cold weather maintenance; when buildings are not available, maintenance tents are a temporary expedient — if possible, with wood flooring and heated by portable duct heaters or tent stoves. Constantly watch for vehicle deficiencies exposing personnel to carbon monoxide poisoning: inspect and test passenger and crew compartments per **Technical Bulletin (TB) MED 269** at regular intervals. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md` **Provide proper ventilation in heated shelters to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning** from running engines and defective maintenance facilities. Avoid leaning on cold-soaked equipment or kneeling/lying on the ground — rapid body cooling may cause cold injury. Place insulating material (fiber packing, cardboard, rags, tarpaulins) between yourself and cold-soaked equipment. Use a box or pan for small parts, and a tarpaulin under each vehicle to catch dropped parts.

**Equipment recovery.** Vehicle recovery in cold, snowy conditions can be extremely dangerous. **Winches:** operate the engine at a reasonable speed per the operator's manual — high speeds damage the winch; use a snatch block whenever possible to reduce load. Read all caution plates and estimate a **25-percent reduction in capacity** when determining load — for example, a 5,000-pound winch capacity becomes 3,750 pounds in cold weather. In preparing a winch for operation: clean, inspect, and lubricate per the operator's manual. A cold-soaked or inoperable vehicle can present a maintenance challenge; recovery is often extremely difficult due to reduced mechanical capacity, ice, snow, and limited approach routes. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`
## 4. Antifreeze, Fuels, Hydraulic Fluids, and Lubricants
**Antifreeze.** Proper antifreeze materials are critical for cold operations; those specified for liquid-cooling systems are listed in Appendix A. **Arctic-type antifreeze protects to −90 °F.

**Fuels.** Arctic fuels for gasoline or diesel engines are selected for proper atomization for a combustible fuel-air mixture; these fuels are listed in Appendix A.

When vehicles, generators, and POL containers are brought into warm storage from the cold, fill fuel tanks and containers only **three quarters full** — cold POL expands and can spill, creating a serious fire hazard.

**Hydraulic fluids.** Hydraulic fluids must work in cold conditions; those for hydraulic systems, recoil mechanisms, and equilibrators are listed in Appendix A.

**Lubricants and lubrication orders.** Lubrication for Army equipment is specified in the lubrication order (LO) or TM. LOs are based on three anticipated ranges: above 32 °F, from +40 to −10 °F, and from 0 to −65 °F. **Oil, engine, arctic (OEA)** is a synthetic SAE 0W-20 lubricant for 40 °F to −65 °F for engines and 120 °F to −65 °F for transmissions and hydraulics; approved for engines, power steering, and automatic and standard transmissions — although OEA can be used in most automatic transmissions, consult the LO first, since OEA may void the warranty in some applications. Check frequently. Petroleum-product symbols on LOs follow MIL-STD 290. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`
## 5. Batteries and Winterization Equipment
**Vehicle batteries.** Cold on batteries is one of the greatest hindrances to winter operations. Current delivered at **15 °F is only 50 percent** of normal; at **−30 °F** only a little over **10 percent**; at **−40 °F** and below, practically zero. A fully charged battery does not freeze in extreme cold, but a discharged battery freezes readily — the manual's text states that a battery with specific gravity 1.100 freezes at 10 °F, while its Table 1-1 gives 19 °F for the same specific gravity (an internal inconsistency in the manual; the table values are given below). Unless a storage battery is warmed to about **35 °F**, it does not receive an adequate charge from the generator. In constant cold weather, test storage batteries for state of charge every **three days**; recharge if the specific gravity is less than **1.1250**.

**Freezing points of batteries at various specific gravities (Table 1-1, electrolyte corrected to 80 °F):** 1.280 → −90 °F; 1.250 → −62 °F; 1.200 → −16 °F; 1.150 → 5 °F; 1.100 → 19 °F; 1.000 → 32 °F. Fill batteries with **1.280 specific gravity electrolyte**, not the tropical electrolyte of 1.250.

**Small equipment batteries.** Nickel-cadmium and lithium sulfur dioxide batteries have their own cold-weather characteristics and handling requirements (Chapter 1, Section V) — consult the equipment TM for the specific battery type, and keep batteries warm until needed. **Water should never be added to a cold battery; add it when the battery is warm and charging, or it will not mix — freezing could result due to reduced specific gravity.** Add water to a battery only when the vehicle is to be immediately operated for **30 minutes or more**.

**Winterization equipment.** **Personnel heaters:** shut off personnel and crew cab heaters at least **20 minutes** prior to entering any shelter — to prevent fire hazards from fuel pumping and gasoline vapors if the heater operates faultily. **Power plant heaters, portable fuel-burning heaters, hardtop closure kits, quick heat systems, standby heat systems,** and the **auxiliary power (slave) receptacle** are the winterization items in Chapter 1, Section VI — each has its own installation and operating procedures in the equipment TM. → `references/cold-effects-on-materiel.md`
## 6. Preparing, Operating, and Maintaining Vehicles
**Preparation for operation.** Before operating vehicles, crews should review the operator manuals for cold weather operations — these all include a section subtitled "Operations Under Unusual Conditions." Operators must know other basic skills, such as working with tire chains and slave-starting. Chapter 2 covers chassis and body, power train, electrical, and auxiliary equipment. → `references/vehicle-preparation-and-operation.md`

**Operating and idling.** Operating the main or auxiliary generator engine when a vehicle is stationary exposes the crew to possible carbon monoxide poisoning, greatly increased when hatch doors are closed. **When there is no outside air movement, personnel must dismount for at least 10 minutes each hour** (tactical situation permitting).

**Ether.** **Inject ether into the engine air intake system only while the engine is being turned over.

**Slave starting.** Using the auxiliary power (slave) receptacle: connect the slave cable **positive to positive and negative to negative**; check batteries prior to slave starting; if the dead vehicle has a master switch, it **must be off while connecting the slave cable**.

**Tow starting.** **Towing a vehicle to start its engine will only be done in an extreme emergency.** Use a tow bar; use tow chains only as a last resort. **Vehicles should never be started by pushing.**

**During operation and maintenance.** Chapter 3, Sections II–VIII cover operating and maintenance procedures for chassis and body, power train, engine lubrication, electrical, fuel, and cooling systems, and auxiliary equipment. Throughout: check oil frequently (see §4), drain water from air filters and reservoirs after every eight hours of continuous operation, and add water to a battery only when the vehicle will run for 30 minutes or more. → `references/vehicle-preparation-and-operation.md`
## 7. Weapons, Munitions, and Fire Control Equipment
**Effects of cold on weapons.** Cold weather affects weapons functioning through cold-dry air, condensation, snow and ice, and reduced visibility. **Below 10 °F, LAW (lubricant, arctic, weapons) helps moving parts slide better than CLP or LSA.**

**Operation and maintenance of weapons.** **Use of covers:** use covers and muzzle caps when available, but remove them prior to firing. **Dealing with condensation:** condensation ("sweating") forms on weapons taken from the cold into heated shelters; taken back into the cold, the film freezes, especially in internal parts, causing stoppages and malfunctions. **Procedures for lubricating weapons:** strip completely, clean with dry-cleaning solvent to remove all lubricants and rust-prevention compounds, then apply the cold-weather lubricant; keep moisture, snow, ice, and dirt out of the mechanism.

**Breech, bores, recoil, and equilibrators.** **Breech and firing mechanisms:** keep them clean and dry; condensation and ice in the breech cause misfires. **Bores:** keep bores clean and free of snow and ice. **Recoil mechanisms:** adjusting recuperator gas pressure is a **direct support (DS) or general support (GS)** maintenance function — injury to personnel or damage to materiel is possible. **Gascheck pad:** do not use dry-cleaning solvent, mineral spirits, paint thinner, or rifle-bore cleaner — wipe clean, permit to dry, do not lubricate. **Do not pack grease in the jack housing.** **Equilibrators:** newer howitzers use nitrogen; pressures require frequent checks (see the operator's TM).

**Respirators:** extreme caution must be exercised to keep the parts of a respirator free of snow and ice.

**Direct and indirect fire weapons.** **Armor vehicle main armament** is covered in Chapter 4, Section IV. **Field artillery:** with cold rounds in a cold tube, achieved ranges may be as much as **20 percent less at −4 °F** than tabular firing tables — four rounds fired over one to three minutes will warm the tube. **Mortars:** non-anticontact gloves can keep a shell from dropping properly down the tube, damaging equipment and causing injury or death; bulky/loose gloves may get pinched between round and bore. **Antitank wire-guided missiles and rockets, field artillery missiles and rockets, and air defense weapons** are covered in Chapter 4, Sections VI–VIII — each system has cold-weather preparation and handling procedures in its TM.

**Ammunition and munitions.** **Do not use ammunition at ambient temperatures below that specified on the ammunition.** Chapter 4, Section IX covers ammunition, grenades, mines, and demolitions. **Height of burst in feet above ground surface** (manual's table): near (contact) fuze — earth 1–3, snow 1–3, lake ice 1–5; proximity fuze — earth 9–15, snow 1–5, lake ice 10–25; delay fuze — earth 0–3, snow 0–3, lake ice 0–3. Select multioption fuzes whenever available (see §3). → `references/weapons-munitions-fire-control.md`
## 8. Fire Control Equipment
**Precautions and fogging.** Fire control equipment — sights, telescopes, range finders, and their mounts — is vulnerable to cold, moisture, and rough handling. **Fogging of eyepieces** is a constant problem moving between cold air and warm shelters, or when breath condenses on cold glass. Purging (dry nitrogen or dry air, per the TM) keeps sealed optical instruments dry inside. **Compasses, binoculars, and other optical instruments** need the same care as sights — keep dry, warm gradually, protect from shock. **Digitized equipment** (computers, digital fire control) has its own cold-weather limits; consult the TM.

**Powered systems, level vials, and lasers.** Electrically powered fire control systems have difficulty obtaining an effective electrical ground when firing from frozen surfaces — overcome through training or expedient grounding techniques. Level vials can freeze or give false readings in the cold. → `references/weapons-munitions-fire-control.md`
## 9. Small Arms
**Cold weather problems.** Severe cold can adversely affect small arms; special care and procedures are needed to keep them functioning properly. **Condensation:** see §7 — sweating may continue for as long as one hour in heated shelters; wait one hour, remove all condensation, then clean. **Cold-dry conditions:** exposed metal surfaces require more frequent applications of smaller amounts of lubrication to remain fully functional in the cold-dry air. When enemy contact is imminent, keep the interior climate of troop compartments (especially aircraft) close to freezing (32 °F), to prevent overheating troops in cold regions uniform and moisture condensing and refreezing on weapons as troops debark.

**Functioning difficulties.** **Sluggishness** is a common weapons problem in cold weather — normal lubricants thicken at low temperatures and stopped or sluggish action results. Below 10 °F, LAW helps moving parts slide better than CLP or LSA. **Snow and ice:** blowing snow gets into working parts, sights, barrels, magazines, and ammunition in deep snow under combat conditions; use covers and muzzle caps when available, remove before firing. **Visibility:** blowing snow and ice fog inhibit target acquisition; weapons fire creates a pall of ice fog over the position, blocking the gunner's vision and revealing the position. **Breakages and malfunctions:** higher rate in severe cold, primarily from the cold, though snow in weapons also causes stoppages.

**Effects on pistols, rifles, machine guns, and ammunition.** **Precautions:** when using weapons or handling ammunition in severe cold, wear gloves or trigger-finger mittens with liners. Sudden temperature changes can crack plastic handgrips, like those on the M203 grenade launcher — wrapping the weapon in a blanket or poncho before bringing it from cold to warm helps warm it gradually. **Emplacement:** crew-served weapons need a natural base or gun platform; on solid frozen ground there is no "give" — all firing shock is absorbed by the weapon, causing damage. **Pistols:** damage to moving parts, firing while wearing arctic mittens, snow- or ice-plugged magazines. **Rifles:** malfunctions and breakages from snow- or ice-plugged magazines; apply LAW to keep bipods from freezing in position. **Machine guns:** break and malfunction at a high rate in cold weather — carry extra sears and bolt parts. **Ammunition:** cold does not materially affect small arms ammunition performance; keep it at weapon temperature, in bandoleers, extra in parka or rucksack pockets. Storage, cleaning, and resupply rules → `references/weapons-munitions-fire-control.md`
## 10. Communication and Information Systems Equipment


**Cold weather phenomena.** Cold temperatures with dry air, condensation from rapid temperature changes, frozen surfaces, snow and ice, visibility, and electromagnetic conditions affect communications equipment.

**Radio systems.** Handle all communications and electronic equipment very carefully when cold — a radio dropped on cold ground or thrown into a vehicle is easily damaged. Warmup is considerable — **15 to 20 minutes warmup before voice transmission** may be required, and a cold-soaked AN/GRC-42 radioteletype system might require warming for **three to four hours**. **Lithium batteries, like the BA-5598 for the SINCGARS radio set, are especially effective in cold weather and do not need warming at temperatures above −20 °F.** Dry cells should be kept warm until needed. Carry the manpacked-radio handset inside at least one layer of clothing to prevent the push-to-talk button from freezing. **Friction tape is advised rather than plastic tape, which loses its adhesiveness in the cold.** Vehicular radios: warm for **three to four minutes on low power** before changing to high power or transmitting; cycle the vehicle periodically per its −10 series manual below **−30 °F**, idling at approximately **1,200 RPM** — or the high discharge rate of running both radio and heater soon wears out vehicle batteries.

**Antenna systems.** Proper antenna assembly is commonly overlooked in the cold and dark. Erect the RC-292 and OE-254 masts with enough sections for necessary height; use GP-101 (RC-292) or GP-112 (OE-254) cold-weather stakes in frozen ground. The **RG-213 coaxial cable is recommended over RG-8 cable; RG-8 becomes brittle and cracks at temperatures below −20 °F.** HF and long wire antennas can approach 75 meters. Lubricate mast section ends only with **silicone lubricant**. → `references/comms-and-nbc.md`

**Wire and cable systems.** Laying wire over long distances in deep snow is greatly facilitated by oversnow vehicles — but their skis and tracks damage surface-laid wire and cable, dragging away large sections and cutting critical circuits. A **slack factor of 30 percent is recommended** for wire-laying teams instead of the 20 percent suggested in the TM — this allows for cold weather shrinkage. The TA-1/PT voice-powered telephone performs poorly in extreme cold beyond squad or platoon boundaries. The TA-312/PT, powered by alkaline batteries (such as the BA-3030/U), provides reliable communications and is the preferred instrument for wire troubleshooting teams. The newer WD-1/TT field communications wire performs nearly as well in the cold as the old spiral wire, but is less durable and more susceptible to vehicle traffic damage — use it in less traveled areas. The WD-36/TT assault wire is lighter and easier to handle, man-pack, dispense, and splice than WD-1/TT in the cold, but much less durable.

**Power sources and computers.** Thoroughly inspect and winterize generators — they fail more at sub-zero temperatures; consult the generator TM. → `references/comms-and-nbc.md`
## 11. NBC Defense in Cold Conditions
Many effects of nuclear, biological, and chemical (NBC) agents change in the cold weather environment. FM 3-4, *NBC Protection*, covers NBC defense. Materials of protective equipment become brittle, crack, and tear easily in extreme cold.

**Effects on nuclear weapons.** Below **−25 °F**, the effective radius of a nuclear blast may increase **up to 20 percent**; icepack or hardened snowpack extends static overpressure distance, while dynamic pressure distances may decrease. Blast waves and ground shock may create fissures, crevasses, avalanches, and rock slides as far as **30 kilometers** from ground zero. **Minimum safe distances may need to be increased by as much as 50 percent** for thermal blast; at extremely low temperatures, initial fallout distances may be reduced by as much as **25 percent**. → `references/comms-and-nbc.md`

**Effects on biological and chemical agents.** Biological attacks must be anticipated; up-to-date immunizations, acclimatized personnel, and strict hygiene are the defenses, covered in Appendix E, which gives agent persistency data by degree of contamination and temperature — consult it and FM 3-4 for the specific agent. → `references/comms-and-nbc.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a cold-weather ordnance question from FM 9-207, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (temperatures, pressures, specific gravities, times), then the safety check that proves the answer (the carbon monoxide check, the battery charge check, the recuperator pressure check). Name the chapter the procedure comes from. Where the current TM or LO differs, say so and follow the newer document. Quote the manual sparingly.
## Operating Rules

1. **The environment is the second enemy.** Plan for it the way you plan for the tactical enemy — winterization is not optional.
2. **Winterize early.** Proper mechanical condition before the onset of cold requires time for disassembly, repair, cleaning, and reassembly — anticipate low temperatures far enough in advance to complete winterization.
3. **The TM is the authority.** FM 9-207 is an overview; the operator's TM and the lubrication order govern the specific equipment — and the 1998 product specifications must be verified against current documents.
4. **Carbon monoxide kills silently.** Ventilate heated shelters, test crew compartments per TB MED 269, and dismount 10 minutes each hour when idling with no outside air movement.
5. **Batteries are the weak link.** Keep them charged, warm, and tested every three days; a discharged battery freezes and ruptures.
6. **Lubricant changeover is a procedure, not a top-off.** Strip, clean with dry-cleaning solvent, and apply LAW below 10 °F — and keep moisture, snow, ice, and dirt out of the mechanism while you do it.
7. **Cold rounds, cold tube, short range.** Indirect-fire ranges can be 20 percent less at −4 °F — warm the tube with four rounds over one to three minutes.
8. **Never tow-start by pushing, and tow-start only in an extreme emergency.** Some vehicles cannot be tow-started at all — check the operator's manual first.
