# Operations — FM 31-71

## The Northern Area

The area of northern operations is defined as those northern areas of the Northern Hemisphere lying north of the temperate zone, where environmental conditions require special techniques and equipment not normally required in a more temperate climate. It includes both the Arctic and the subarctic: about **45 percent of the North American continent and 65 percent of the Eurasian land mass** lie in these regions. The doctrine and techniques may be applicable in any area of the world that has snow and cold weather. The terms cold weather operations, operations in the subarctic, arctic operations, operations in the far north, northern latitudes, polar operations, and operations on polar ice are synonymous, combined under "northern operations," embracing both summer and winter and applicable to North America and Eurasia.

## Basic Considerations

The northern environment is a dynamic force: he who recognizes and understands this force can use it; he who disregards or underestimates it is threatened with failure or destruction. Because of the demanding requirements on the individual soldier, leadership must be of the highest caliber — leaders at all levels down to the squad must make decisions far surpassing the scope of their usual responsibilities. In the north the human element is all-important; the effectiveness of equipment is greatly reduced; specialized training and experience are essential. **The climate does not allow a margin of error for the individual or the organization.** Ground mobility is restricted; movements must be carefully planned with the knowledge that distance can be as difficult to overcome as the enemy. Momentum is difficult to achieve and quickly lost. Maintenance of equipment is difficult and of great importance.

## Command Leadership

Leadership in the north is highly demanding, with emphasis on small unit operations. Command is decentralized to insure maximum flexibility for leaders at all levels. Resourcefulness and initiative are requisites for unit commanders. Forceful and personal leadership is the key to success: commanders at all echelons must plan and prepare in great detail, actively supervise, keep themselves and their subordinates informed, and maintain close coordination with adjacent and supporting units.

## Tactics

**Mobility is a cardinal principle** — obtained only through proper integration and use of all appropriate transportation, including aircraft, wheeled and tracked vehicles, water craft, and individual means. Operational planning emphasizes envelopments to exploit surprise: the attack may be conducted as an envelopment to seize objectives from which the enemy position may be dominated by fire or attacked from the rear. With proper use of airmobility most operations will be reconnaissance and security missions, finding the enemy and destroying him in place with all available firepower. Where predominantly ground forces are employed, movement to contact is conducted under cover of a highly mobile force containing aviation, combat support and combat service support elements, and sufficient firepower to eliminate minor opposition.

**Night is the rule.** In winter operations during the long periods of darkness, night movement and night operations should become the rule rather than the exception. An attack has a better chance of succeeding during the night because during daylight, columns of troops and equipment are easily located and extremely vulnerable. The long night of winter must not be considered a bar to operations. The assault should be conducted at night or during periods of low visibility; surprise is an important factor. Excellent concealment for troop movements is afforded by darkness, fog, or falling snow.

**Lines of communication are life blood.** Enemy forces may be defeated by severing his air and ground lines of communication, since the terrain and climate may deny alternate routes. Lack of adequate ground lines requires more emphasis on aircraft and low ground pressure cross-country vehicles. Deception is vital because of vulnerability brought on by problems of concealment, slow movement, and limited land lines of communication.

**Shelter denial as a weapon.** In winter, denial of adequate shelter to a force can cause casualties as surely as the delivery of firepower. This fact can work for or against the field unit.

## Low Visibility

**Whiteout** — a milky atmospheric phenomenon in which the observer appears engulfed in a uniformly white glow; only very dark nearby objects can be seen. Whiteouts occur over unbroken snow cover beneath a uniformly overcast sky, and blowing snow can cause the same effect. **Greyout** is similar except the horizon is distinguishable. Ice fog limits or prevents movement during darkness and may disclose the location of weapons, vehicles, and troops. High winds and whiteout interfere with aviation operations. The long night of winter must not be considered a bar to operations — but operations in whiteout, without landmarks and without visibility, require navigation by compass and dead reckoning, and movement in pairs.

## Operations (Chapter 2)

Planning, organization, combat intelligence, patrolling, counterintelligence, and security — all with northern modifications. Patrolling is harder and more important: patrols must be self-sufficient, paired as buddies, and prepared for whiteout and extreme cold. Offensive operations emphasize envelopment and night attack; defensive operations emphasize key terrain, depth along successive ridges, and the shelter-denial principle; retrograde operations in the north are slow and dangerous, and a retrograde that loses its shelter and supply line becomes a rout by cold. Airmobile operations follow FM 57-35 with northern modifications; combat under conditions of low visibility is a standing requirement, not a special case.

## Combat and Combat Support (Chapter 3)

Infantry, armor, artillery, Army aviation, chemical operations and biological defense, flame and smoke operations, nuclear weapons, and engineers — each with northern modifications. Armor is limited by ground that will not bear it; artillery is limited by the difficulty of moving guns and ammunition and observing fire; aviation is limited by weather, whiteout, and ice fog but gains value because ground movement is so slow; engineers are essential for routes, shelter, and water points.

## Other Tactical Operations (Chapter 6)

**Airborne operations.** Mobility and flexibility suit the north, but limitations are amplified: weather (blizzards, ice fog, whiteout); navigational difficulties from inadequate maps and lack of landmarks; lack of marshalling areas with warm shelters; few hours of daylight for airlanded operations in winter; bulkiness of cold-weather clothing and equipment requires more aircraft space per individual, necessitating more aircraft; maintenance difficulties from insufficient shelters and decreased personnel efficiency; heavy snow or deep drifts on airfields and landing zones.

**Operations on permanent ice and snow.** Movement, shelter, and supply on ice caps and permanent snowfields, where there is no ground at all — only ice, and the crevasse hazard. See FM 31-72 for the mountaineering detail.

**Amphibious and riverine operations.** Northern coasts and rivers are usable in summer and frozen in winter; the seasonal change reverses the tactical problem. Boat procedures are in Appendix D.

**Raids, rear area operations, special forces, psychological operations.** Small, self-sufficient forces operating in the enemy's rear, exploiting the same mobility and concealment the north gives the defender. **Use of non-air defense weapons against aircraft** — in the north, where air defense assets are scarce, every weapon is an air defense weapon when aircraft appear.

## Training (Chapter 7)

Training is divided into general, winter training, training in other seasons, and hints for instructors. Appendix B gives a suggested training program for winter indoctrination. The manual's position is that northern competence is a training product, not an equipment product: a unit that has trained in the cold can operate in it; a unit that has not cannot be equipped into competence. Appendix C is a commander's guide for northern operations — a checklist of the decisions and preparations a commander must make before committing a unit to the north, from clothing and shelter through communications, medical support, and evacuation.
