# Communications — FM 31-71

## The Environment's Effect on Communications

Low temperatures, low visibilities, and the lack of ground lines of communication impose greater reliance on radio for command and control of ground combat forces. Radio navigation aids, necessary because of the scarcity of landmarks, prevailing low visibility, and long hours of darkness, are particularly sensitive to electronic warfare — especially where alternate base stations are few and far between. The enemy must also rely on electronic aids, so a small electronic warfare unit properly employed can play a decisive role in northern operations. Support of signal communications activities by Army aviation is essential, since aviation provides the relay and retransmission that ground stations cannot.

## The Auroral Effect

The reflecting properties of the ionosphere are directly related to the position and radiation activity of the sun. The sun's bombardment of the earth's atmosphere coupled with strong magnetic activity concentrated near the poles causes the aurora borealis in northern latitudes. Ionization of atmospheric particles is increased, and radio waves reaching distant receiving points by sky wave propagation are reduced in intensity by increased absorption. This absorption becomes more pronounced during violent eruptions of the sun's surface, visible as sunspots. During such a period, a radio **"blackout"** may occur on nets crossing or passing through the auroral zone.

- The greatest auroral activity occurs between **60° and 70° North latitude** and at intervals of **27 to 28 days**.
- Signals from distant stations are usually much stronger prior to such periods.
- Monthly predictions of expected conditions are published by the National Bureau of Standards and are available to communications-electronics staff officers at all levels.
- Blackouts occur throughout the high frequency spectrum and down to 26 KHz and may last several days.
- High frequency sky wave transmissions are also subject to sporadic blackouts, unpredictable and erratic in duration and coverage.

## Atmospheric Static

In the medium frequency band (300 to 3,000 KHz) very little continuous high-level static is experienced in northern latitudes, but steady rushes of high-level noise are common and signify an auroral blackout on the frequency being monitored.

**Precipitation static** — flakes or pellets of highly charged snow are occasionally experienced in the North, just as rain and sand static are encountered in tropical and desert regions during high winds. Charged particles of snow driven against metal vehicles, antennas, and other objects usually discharge with a high-pitched static roar that can blanket all frequencies for several hours at a time. While uncommon except in aircraft, it can take place just when communications are vital. Antiprecipitation, static-proof antennas can be constructed by covering exposed portions of antennas with layers of polystyrene tape and shellac to withstand breakdown voltages of 30,000 to 40,000 volts.

## Propagation Methods

Sky wave, ground wave, and line-of-sight propagation behave differently in the north. Sky wave is vulnerable to auroral absorption; ground wave is affected by frozen ground and snow cover; line-of-sight is limited by terrain and by ice fog. Antennas and grounds must be adapted: frozen ground makes poor electrical ground, and antennas must be kept clear of snow and ice loading. Operators must understand which propagation method their net is using and what the auroral forecast means for it.

## Cold Effects on Battery Power Supplies

Extreme cold impairs electrical components, but its most serious effect is on the dry type primary batteries that power small man-carried portable radios and many test instruments. Only batteries designed for cold weather operations should be used for northern operations.

- Batteries of all types show decreased power capacity at low temperatures; specially designed cold weather batteries are more efficient.
- Store batteries at supply points between **10°F and 36°F**. On removal from storage, heat them slowly to **70°F** before use. Warm batteries give good results if used promptly on exposure to cold.
- The conventional dry cell loses efficiency rapidly at low temperatures and decreases in capacity as temperature drops below 70°F. The terminal voltage is not affected by cold, but capacity, life, and effectiveness are. At **0°F it is 40 percent effective; at −10°F, 20 percent; at −30°F, only 8 percent effective**.
- Dry batteries, if kept warm, deliver satisfactory service life. Carrying batteries inside clothing, or using insulated containers or heating devices, keeps them serviceable as long as battery temperature does not exceed 100°F.
- Battery vests designed for wear under outer clothing, with flat-cell temperate batteries, should be used with man-packed radio sets during extreme cold.
- Batteries inactive because of cold may be reactivated by thorough warming at temperatures below 100°F. A battery no longer serviceable in cold weather may be used indoors where temperatures are warmer.

## Techniques for Range and Reliability

Operators require special training initially and refresher training prior to commitment to northern operations. Training of tactical FM radio operators in special techniques is particularly important because these personnel normally have no formal training. Consideration should also be given to additional training for Radio Telegraph, SSB-voice, and other HF radio sets recommended for northern operations. Techniques include proper antenna siting and orientation, use of relay and retransmission stations, selection of frequencies suited to the time of day and the auroral forecast, and disciplined radio procedure to minimize transmission time.

## Visual Communication, Messenger, and Photography

Visual communication (flags, lights, panels, pyrotechnics) and messenger are covered as alternates when radio fails — in the north, the messenger is a communications system, not a fallback. Photography is used for reconnaissance and for recording terrain and enemy positions where radio reporting is impossible or insecure. Maintenance and care of signal equipment in extreme cold is a standing requirement: keep equipment dry, keep batteries warm, and protect connectors and cables from ice and brittleness.
