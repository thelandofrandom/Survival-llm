# FM 31-71 Northern Operations

## Purpose

FM 31-71, *Northern Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, June 1971, 199 pages) — the Army's northern operations manual, written for commanders and staffs operating combat, combat support, and combat service support units in the northern regions of the world. It is directed primarily toward operations below division level, where the forward elements of divisions or task forces must overcome the many summer and winter problems inherent in northern operations. It implements the provisions of Standardization of Operations and Logistics (SOLOG) Agreement 23R, Arctic Doctrine. The manual covers general considerations and the environment, operations, combat and combat support, combat service support, communications, other tactical operations, and training, with appendices on references, a suggested winter indoctrination training program, a commander's guide for northern operations, boat procedures, and measurement conversion factors. It directs the reader to FM 31-70 and FM 31-72 for further information.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 31-71 is a 1971 manual and its equipment, weapons, communications, and organizational details are obsolete. For current Army doctrine, northern and arctic operations are governed by **ATP 3-90.96, *Arctic and Extreme Cold Weather Operations* (2025)** — follow the current manual where it disagrees with FM 31-71. FM 31-71 remains valuable because it is the most complete single-volume treatment of the north as an operational theater: its chapters on the northern environment, the primacy of mobility and lines of communication, low-visibility operations, and northern communications (auroral blackout, atmospheric static, cold effects on batteries) have no equivalent in a single current manual. The durable core is the operational judgment — mobility, decentralized command, night operations, deception, and the treatment of shelter denial as a weapon — and the communications chapter. The weapons, radios, and organizational details are period material.

## 1. The Northern Area and Its Basic Considerations

**1.1. Area of northern operations.** The area is defined as those northern areas of the Northern Hemisphere lying north of the temperate zone, where environmental conditions require special techniques and equipment not normally required in a more temperate climate. It includes both the Arctic and the subarctic: about **45 percent of the North American continent and 65 percent of the Eurasian land mass** lie in these regions. The doctrine and techniques may be applicable in any area of the world that has snow and cold weather. For this manual, the terms cold weather operations, operations in the subarctic, arctic operations, operations in the far north, northern latitudes, polar operations, and operations on polar ice are synonymous, combined under "northern operations," embracing both summer and winter and applicable to North America and Eurasia.

**1.2. Basic considerations.** The northern environment is a dynamic force: he who recognizes and understands this force can use it; he who disregards or underestimates it is threatened with failure or destruction. Because of the demanding requirements on the individual soldier, leadership must be of the highest caliber — leaders at all levels down to the squad must make decisions far surpassing the scope of their usual responsibilities. In the north the human element is all-important; the effectiveness of equipment is greatly reduced; specialized training and experience are essential. **The climate does not allow a margin of error for the individual or the organization.** The ground mobility of all units is restricted; movements must be carefully planned and executed with the knowledge that distance can be as difficult to overcome as the enemy. Momentum is difficult to achieve and quickly lost. Maintenance of equipment is difficult and of great importance.

**1.3. Command leadership.** Because of severe environmental conditions, leadership in the north is highly demanding, with emphasis on small unit operations. Command is decentralized to insure maximum flexibility for leaders at all levels. Resourcefulness and initiative are requisites for unit commanders. Forceful and personal leadership is the key to success: commanders at all echelons must plan and prepare in great detail, actively supervise, keep themselves and their subordinates informed, and maintain close coordination with adjacent and supporting units.

## 2. Tactics in the North

**2.1. Tactical principles.** Tactical principles are the same as elsewhere, but emphasis falls on principles less important in milder climates. **Mobility is a cardinal principle** — obtained only through proper integration and use of all appropriate transportation, including aircraft, wheeled and tracked vehicles, water craft, and individual means. Operational planning emphasizes envelopments to exploit surprise: the attack may be conducted as an envelopment to seize objectives from which the enemy position may be dominated by fire or attacked from the rear. With proper use of airmobility most operations will be reconnaissance and security missions, finding the enemy and destroying him in place with all available firepower. Where predominantly ground forces are employed, movement to contact is conducted under cover of a highly mobile force containing aviation, combat support and combat service support elements, and sufficient firepower to eliminate minor opposition.

**2.2. Night is the rule.** In winter operations during the long periods of darkness, **night movement and night operations should become the rule rather than the exception**. An attack has a better chance of succeeding during the night because during daylight, columns of troops and equipment are easily located and extremely vulnerable. The long night of winter must not be considered a bar to operations. The assault should be conducted at night or during periods of low visibility; surprise is an important factor. Excellent concealment for troop movements is afforded by darkness, fog, or falling snow.

**2.3. Lines of communication are life blood.** Enemy forces may be defeated by severing his air and ground lines of communication, since the terrain and climate may deny alternate routes. Lack of adequate ground lines requires more emphasis on aircraft and low ground pressure cross-country vehicles for supply, resupply, and troop movement. Deception is vital in all operations because of vulnerability brought on by problems of concealment, slow movement, and limited land lines of communication.

**2.4. Shelter denial as a weapon.** In winter, denial of adequate shelter to a force can cause casualties as surely as the delivery of firepower. This fact can work for or against the field unit — a force that destroys or denies the enemy's shelter inflicts cold casualties without firing a shot, and a force that loses its own shelter is defeated by the weather.

**2.5. Low visibility.** Whiteout is a milky atmospheric phenomenon in which the observer appears engulfed in a uniformly white glow; only very dark nearby objects can be seen. Whiteouts occur over unbroken snow cover beneath a uniformly overcast sky, and blowing snow can cause the same effect. Greyout is similar except the horizon is distinguishable. Ice fog limits or prevents movement during darkness and may disclose the location of weapons, vehicles, and troops. High winds and whiteout interfere with aviation operations. → `references/operations.md`

## 3. Operations

**3.1. Planning and organization.** Planning, organization, combat intelligence, patrolling, counterintelligence, and security — all with northern modifications. Patrolling is harder and more important: patrols must be self-sufficient, paired as buddies, and prepared for whiteout and extreme cold. Security must account for the ease with which a mobile enemy can move through supposedly impassable ground on skis or by air.

**3.2. Offensive, defensive, retrograde.** Offensive operations emphasize envelopment and night attack; defensive operations emphasize key terrain, depth along successive ridges, and the shelter-denial principle; retrograde operations in the north are slow and dangerous, and a retrograde that loses its shelter and supply line becomes a rout by cold. Airmobile operations follow FM 57-35 with northern modifications, and combat under conditions of low visibility is a standing requirement, not a special case.

**3.3. Combat and combat support.** Infantry, armor, artillery, Army aviation, chemical operations and biological defense, flame and smoke operations, nuclear weapons, and engineers — each with northern modifications. Armor is limited by ground that will not bear it; artillery is limited by the difficulty of moving guns and ammunition and observing fire; aviation is limited by weather, whiteout, and ice fog but gains value because ground movement is so slow; engineers are essential for routes, shelter, and water points.

## 4. Combat Service Support

**4.1. Logistics.** Unusual weather and terrain make supply, evacuation, transportation, and services more difficult and more time-consuming. Time and space factors vary with terrain, climate, and season; more time must be allowed for movement of supplies and troops because of the environment. **Distance is measured in time rather than space.** Commanders must issue orders early to allow adequate time for subordinates to move supplies and equipment. Logistical support planning is required in great detail, even for small unit operations. Adequate support must provide survival and comfort as well as combat resupply: food, fuel, clothing, sleeping gear, tentage, mountain gear, winter equipment, repair parts, and ammunition. The capacity of the combat service support units to provide adequate logistical support may be the determining factor in evaluating the feasibility of a planned operation — the commander must be ready to alter the plan when environmental stresses make support impossible.

**4.2. Medical support.** Division-level health services should be available in the brigade combat trains, equipped with appropriate radios to communicate with air ambulances and vehicles with mobility equal to the units supported; an X-ray unit eliminates the need to evacuate all sprains as possible fractures. Small units operating as independent task force elements should establish a relatively short holding period — **1 to 3 days** at unit level, subject to change by the unit surgeon — because adverse conditions make extensive definitive care over an extended period exceedingly difficult. Surface evacuation of patients is difficult in winter and virtually impossible in summer; the lack of adequate roadnets and the necessity of moving supplies over the same route greatly restrict patient evacuation. **The most practical means of patient movement is by helicopter**; air medical evacuation is the primary means outside the task force area, and aircraft resupplying the task force can carry patients on the return trip. Walking wounded are a problem where there are no prominent terrain features to follow to the aid station; snowstorms, fog, and clouds limit visibility to a few feet, so walking wounded evacuated by foot must move in groups, never alone.

**4.3. Transportation, personnel, chaplain.** Transportation and traffic control, ports, rail, highway, and air transportation — all with northern modifications. Personnel: selection, replacements, morale, discipline, prisoners of war, refugees. Morale in the long northern night is a command function, sustained by routine, purpose, and the visible competence of leaders. Religious services are provided for in the same planning as any other support. → `references/logistics-and-medical.md`

## 5. Communications in the North

**5.1. The environment's effect.** Low temperatures, low visibilities, and the lack of ground lines of communication impose greater reliance on radio for command and control. Radio navigation aids, necessary because of the scarcity of landmarks, prevailing low visibility, and long hours of darkness, are particularly sensitive to electronic warfare — especially where alternate base stations are few and far between. The enemy must also rely on electronic aids, so a small electronic warfare unit properly employed can play a decisive role.

**5.2. The auroral effect.** The reflecting properties of the ionosphere are related to the position and radiation activity of the sun; bombardment of the atmosphere coupled with strong magnetic activity near the poles causes the aurora borealis, and ionization increases absorption of radio waves reaching distant points by sky wave propagation. During violent solar eruptions, a radio **"blackout"** may occur on nets crossing or passing through the auroral zone. The greatest auroral activity occurs between **60° and 70° North latitude** at intervals of **27 to 28 days**; monthly predictions are published by the National Bureau of Standards. Blackouts occur throughout the high frequency spectrum and down to 26 KHz and may last several days; high frequency sky wave transmissions are also subject to sporadic blackouts, unpredictable and erratic in duration and coverage.

**5.3. Atmospheric static.** In the medium frequency band (300 to 3,000 KHz) very little continuous high-level static is experienced, but steady rushes of high-level noise are common and signify an auroral blackout on the frequency being monitored. Precipitation static — flakes or pellets of highly charged snow driven against metal vehicles, antennas, and other objects — discharges with a high-pitched static roar that can blanket all frequencies for several hours. Antiprecipitation, static-proof antennas can be constructed by covering exposed portions with layers of polystyrene tape and shellac to withstand breakdown voltages of 30,000 to 40,000 volts.

**5.4. Cold effects on batteries.** Extreme cold has its most serious effect on the dry type primary batteries that power small man-carried portable radios. Only batteries designed for cold weather operations should be used. Batteries should be stored at supply points between **10°F and 36°F**, and on removal heated slowly to **70°F** before use; warm batteries give good results if used promptly on exposure to cold. The conventional dry cell loses efficiency rapidly below 70°F: at **0°F it is 40 percent effective; at −10°F, 20 percent; at −30°F, only 8 percent effective**. Carrying batteries inside clothing, or using insulated containers or heating devices, keeps them serviceable as long as battery temperature does not exceed 100°F. Battery vests worn under outer clothing, with flat-cell temperate batteries, should be used with man-packed radio sets during extreme cold. Batteries inactive from cold may be reactivated by thorough warming below 100°F; a battery no longer serviceable in the cold may be used indoors where temperatures are warmer.

**5.5. Techniques for range and reliability.** Operators require special training and refresher training before commitment to northern operations; tactical FM radio operators need special techniques training. Visual communication, messenger, and photography are covered as alternates when radio fails — in the north, the messenger is a communications system, not a fallback. → `references/communications.md`

## 6. Other Tactical Operations

**6.1. Airborne operations.** The mobility and flexibility of airborne forces suit the diversified areas of the north, but limitations are amplified: weather (blizzards, ice fog, whiteout); navigational difficulties from inadequate maps and lack of landmarks; lack of marshalling areas with warm shelters; few hours of daylight for airlanded operations in winter; the bulkiness of cold-weather clothing and equipment requires more aircraft space per individual, necessitating more aircraft; maintenance difficulties from insufficient shelters and decreased personnel efficiency; heavy snow or deep drifts on airfields and landing zones with associated snow removal.

**6.2. Operations on permanent ice and snow.** Movement, shelter, and supply on ice caps and permanent snowfields, where there is no ground at all — only ice, and the crevasse hazard. See FM 31-72 for the mountaineering detail.

**6.3. Amphibious and riverine operations.** Northern coasts and rivers are usable in summer and frozen in winter; the seasonal change reverses the tactical problem. Boat procedures are in Appendix D.

**6.4. Raids, rear area operations, special forces, psychological operations.** Small, self-sufficient forces operating in the enemy's rear, exploiting the same mobility and concealment the north gives the defender. Use of non-air defense weapons against aircraft — in the north, where air defense assets are scarce, every weapon is an air defense weapon when aircraft appear.

## 7. Training

**7.1. Winter training.** Training is divided into general, winter training, training in other seasons, and hints for instructors. Appendix B gives a suggested training program for winter indoctrination. The manual's position is that northern competence is a training product, not an equipment product: a unit that has trained in the cold can operate in it; a unit that has not cannot be equipped into competence.

**7.2. Commander's guide.** Appendix C is a commander's guide for northern operations — a checklist of the decisions and preparations a commander must make before committing a unit to the north, from clothing and shelter through communications, medical support, and evacuation.

## Operating Rules

1. **The climate allows no margin of error.** Leadership, training, and attention to detail are the only counters.
2. **Mobility is cardinal.** Integrate every means of transport; distance is measured in time, not space.
3. **Night is the rule in winter.** Move and attack in darkness; daylight columns are targets.
4. **Shelter denial is a weapon.** Deny the enemy shelter and he becomes a cold casualty; lose your own and you are defeated by weather.
5. **Communications will fail.** Plan for auroral blackout, static, and dead batteries — messenger, visual, and redundant radio.
6. **Evacuate by air.** Surface evacuation is difficult in winter and virtually impossible in summer; the helicopter is the evacuation system.
7. **Current doctrine wins.** ATP 3-90.96 (2025) supersedes FM 31-71 where they conflict; FM 31-71 is the deep northern-operations reference, not the authority.
