# Snow Shelters and Fire — MWTC Winter Survival (2002), Chapter 4

## Shelter requirements

- Stable (withstand severe weather); ventilated (burning fuel = carbon monoxide; breathing = carbon dioxide); heat-retaining (insulation prevents wasting fuel); drying facility for wet clothes.
- Site: avoid exposed hilltops, valley floors, moist ground, avalanche paths; near firewood, water, signaling; low silhouette and small living area retain heat; tactical: concealment + escape routes.

## Natural shelters

- Caves/rock overhangs (walls of rock, logs, branches across openings); hollow logs (cleaned out, ponchos/tarps/parachutes across openings).
- Hazards: animals already inside (bears, coyotes, lions, rats, snakes; disease from scat/carcasses); inadequate ventilation (smoke buildup dangerous); natural gas pockets in mountain caves; apparent stability that is actually a collapse waiting to happen.

## Man-made snow shelters

- **Snow wall** — extremely expedient, 1–2 men. **Snow cave** — 1–16 men, extended periods; a candle can raise inside temperature. **Tree-pit snow shelter** — 1–3 men, short term. **Snow trench** — short-term, extremely hard-packed snow. **A-frame shelter. Fallen tree bivouac. Snow coffin.**
- Selection table: compacted snow over 6 feet deep → snow cave; iced snow → snow trench (2–3 men).
- Construction: group size; low silhouette; apply snow to roof and sides for a thermal shelter; time/effort required; can it actually protect from the elements?

## Fire in winter

- Fire pit and tactical Dakota hole; tinder/kindling/fuel progression; flint and steel, magnifying glass, bow drill, fire plow. Double handful of tinder + an extra double handful stored dry for morning.
- Winter problem: everything is wet, cold, or buried. First job is often drying clothes and melting snow, not cooking.
- Ventilation is life-safety: a snow shelter with a fire and no ventilation is a carbon-monoxide trap.
