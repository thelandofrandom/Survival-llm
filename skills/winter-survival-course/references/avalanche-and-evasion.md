# Avalanche, Ice Hazards, Evasion — MWTC Winter Survival (2002), Chapters 16–17 and Appendices

## Avalanche — parts

- **Starting zone:** usually steeper than 30°, receives large amounts of snow; where unstable snow breaks loose.
- **Avalanche track:** path under the starting zone, above the runout. Channeled tracks are confined gullies/couloirs; unchanneled are open slopes. Multi-branch tracks may run several times in quick succession — rescuers have been killed working a runout when a second avalanche ran within hours. Wet-snow avalanches tend to follow the track boundary; dry-snow avalanches can jump terrain barriers.
- **Runout zone:** bottom of the path, where debris piles up and comes to rest.

## Hazard evaluation — four variables (ongoing, starts before the mission)

- **Snowpack:** can it slide? Each storm adds a layer with its own texture/strength; some bond well, some do not. Determine whether a weak bond exists and how much snow could release.
- **Weather:** all natural avalanches occur during or shortly after a storm — the pack cannot handle the new weight. Main factors: precipitation, wind, temperature.
  - **Signs of instability:** recent avalanche activity on similar slopes; small avalanches underfoot; booming (audible collapse of snow layers); visible cracks shooting out from underfoot; sluffing debris.
  - **Signs of stability:** settlement cones around trees/obstacles; creep and glide (ripple effect at the bottom of a slope — snow gaining equilibrium); absence of wind during storms (snow accumulating in trees); snow temperatures 25–32°F (settles rapidly into a denser, stronger pack). Cold snow (≤25°F) slows settlement, letting unstable conditions persist longer.
- **Terrain:** avalanches can occur on any slope — assuming only big slopes slide is a common mistake. Most slab avalanches release on slopes of 35–40°; loose-snow avalanches on slopes of 60° and above. Orientation: leeward wind-loaded slopes increase stress; moderate sun can strengthen a pack, direct sunlight weakens and lubricates bonds; weak layers persist on shaded slopes — suspect instability there. Anchors (trees, rocks) make slopes less likely to slide than open slopes. Vegetation evidence: paths of fallen trees aligned in the same direction and sheared at the same height; "flagged" trees (branches void on the uphill side); cleared strips in dense forest. Seasonal: once a path has slid in a season, it may slide again along the same path. Elevation: temperature, wind, precipitation vary significantly with elevation — never assume conditions at one elevation reflect another. Local population is a good source, but beware short-term observations.
- **Human factor:** what are your alternatives and their possible consequences? The variable that kills experienced people who read the other three correctly.

## Ice hazards

- Frozen water crossings, thin ice, and the cold-water immersion after a breakthrough — which returns the survivor to the hypothermia problem with wet clothing and no shelter.
