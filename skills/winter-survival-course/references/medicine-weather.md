# Medicine and Weather — MWTC Winter Survival (2002), Chapters 14–15

## Survival medicine (Chapter 14)

- **Five ways the body loses heat:** radiation (nude man ~60% by radiation); conduction (contact with cold ground/snow/rock — insulate with mat or pack); convection (moving air or water); evaporation (1 quart of sweat in an hour of hard work ≈ 600 calories); respiration (warmed, saturated air exhaled).
- **Responses:** heat — vessels dilate, then sweating. Cold — vessels constrict (blood to core; fingers/toes go cold), then shivering.
- **Hypothermia:** core ≤95°F; number-one killer; most cases at 30–50°F. Contributing factors: ambient temperature, wind chill (improperly clothed only), wet clothing, cold-water immersion, improper clothing, exhaustion, alcohol/nicotine/drugs, injuries (immobility, major bleeding, major burns, head trauma). Number-one sign: altered mental status — confusion, slurred speech, strange behavior, irritability, impaired judgment. Worsening: unconsciousness; severe victims may appear dead. Prevention: stay dry, fed, out of the wind; buddy-system checks. Treatment: rewarm gently and gradually; never give alcohol; handle gently (cold heart can arrest).
- **Frostbite:** actual freezing of tissues; entirely preventable — keep extremities warm, dry, moving; no direct high heat on frozen tissue. Super-cooled liquid ingested can cause immediate frostbite of the throat and possible death — why snow is melted, never eaten.
- Chapter also covers heat injuries and dehydration. For current clinical detail, defer to the TCCC/first-aid skills.

## Mountain weather (Chapter 15)

- Pressure: read the trend, not the absolute value — falling pressure means deteriorating weather. Mountain effects: orographic lift, valley winds, storm systems and wind loading in winter. Field forecasting from clouds, wind, temperature.
- In winter, weather reading feeds the avalanche evaluation (Chapter 17): new snowfall, high winds, temperature trends are avalanche inputs, not comfort inputs.
