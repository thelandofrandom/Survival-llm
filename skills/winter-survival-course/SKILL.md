# USMC MWTC Winter Survival Course Handbook (2002)

## Purpose

Winter Survival Course Handbook, U.S. Marine Corps Mountain Warfare Training Center (MWTC), Bridgeport, California, 2002, 187 pages — the student handout set for the winter survival course. Seventeen chapters plus six appendices. It shares Chapters 1–11 and 13–16 with the Summer Survival Course Handbook (`summer-survival-course` skill) — same SURVIVAL framework, same kit logic, same priorities of work — and rewrites them for cold-weather mountainous environments, then adds two chapters the summer course does not have: **expedient snowshoes (Chapter 12)** and **avalanche and ice hazards (Chapter 17)**. Winter tracking (Chapter 13) replaces summer tracking with snow-sign reading.

The governing fact is stated in Chapter 1 and repeated in the medicine chapter: hypothermia — core temperature **95°F or less** — is the number-one killer in a survival situation, and most cases occur at **30–50°F**, the normal MWTC temperature range outside the harshest winter. In winter, that danger band is the baseline, not the exception. Everything in this handbook is organized around not losing heat faster than you can make it.

## 1. Requirements for survival — same framework, colder stakes

**The SURVIVAL acronym** (Size up the situation/surroundings/physical condition/equipment; Undue haste makes waste; Remember where you are; Vanquish fear and panic; Improvise; Value living; Act like the natives; Learn basic skills) and the **survival stressors** (injury, illness, death; uncertainty; environment; hunger and thirst; fatigue; isolation) carry over unchanged — the winter difference is that the environment stressor is always active and always winning unless countered.

**Priorities of work, in time blocks:**

- **First 24 hours (critical):** initial estimate (enemy, weather, terrain, time of day, resources), then shelter, fire, water, signaling.
- **Second 24 hours:** tools and weapons, traps and snares, pathguards on the avenues of approach into the shelter area.
- **Remainder:** continuously improve until rescue.

**Group survival** depends on organizing activity; morale comes from internal cohesiveness. In winter, the group's first test is usually the shelter build — a group that cannot build a snow shelter together before dark is a group in trouble. → `references/requirements-kit-water.md`

## 2. Survival kit — layered for cold

Same six components as summer — fire starting, water procurement, food procurement, signaling, first aid, shelter — layered with the most important items on the body, multipurpose items preferred, case water-repellent/waterproof, easy to carry, durable. The winter differences are in emphasis: fire-starting items and shelter items move to the top of the value ranking (a fire you cannot start and a shelter you cannot build are the two fastest ways to die in winter), and the metal container for boiling is essential because most winter water comes from melted snow and ice, which costs fuel. Potassium permanganate with sugar or antifreeze, flint and steel, and prepackaged tinder (commercial, or cotton balls and petroleum jelly) are in the kit for the same reason: matches fail when wet and cold, redundancy does not. → `references/requirements-kit-water.md`

## 3. Water procurement — snow and ice are the source, and the trap

**Water intake.** Same rule as summer: thirst is not a reliable gauge; use the over-drink method — drink plenty whenever water is available, particularly when eating. **Dehydration scale:** 5% body-fluid loss — thirst, irritability, nausea, weakness; 10% — dizziness, headache, inability to walk, tingling limbs; 15% — dim vision, painful urination, swollen tongue, deafness, numb skin; over 15% — can be fatal. Cold suppresses thirst, so winter dehydration is a silent threat: you lose water through respiration and sweat under layers and never feel thirsty.

**Snow and ice as water sources:**

- **Snow** can be melted for potable water; melting snow costs more fuel. Uncontaminated snow does not need to be disinfected.
- **Ice** can be melted for potable water; melting ice is preferable to snow because of the higher concentration of water per volume — but ice is frozen water, so it needs to be disinfected.
- **Sea ice:** in time it loses its salinity. Identify it by rounded corners and bluish color. Gray ice has not yet lost its salt content.

**Rules for snow and ice water:**

1. **Never melt snow or ice inside your mouth.** It removes body heat and increases the chance of cold-weather injuries.
2. When on the move, use body heat to melt snow — place snow or ice in a water container carried against the body.
3. Do not waste fuel melting snow or ice when drinkable water (a stream) is available.
4. When melting snow in a container by a fire, use a hot rock to speed the process — and start with a little water in the container so the snow does not scorch.

**Hazardous fluids** are the same as summer — seawater, alcohol (which dehydrates, clouds judgment, and as a super-cooled liquid can cause immediate frostbite of the throat), blood, urine — none of them is water. → `references/requirements-kit-water.md`

## 4. Expedient shelters and fires — snow is building material

**Shelter requirements:** stable (withstand severe weather), ventilated (especially if burning fuel — carbon monoxide, and carbon dioxide from breathing), heat-retaining (insulation to prevent wasting fuel), with a drying facility for wet clothes. Site selection: avoid exposed hilltops, valley floors, moist ground, and avalanche paths; locate near firewood, water, and signaling; low silhouette and reduced living area improve heat retention; in a tactical environment, provide concealment and plan escape routes.

**Natural shelters:** caves or rock overhangs (walls of rock, logs, or branches across the open sides); hollow logs (cleaned out, enhanced with ponchos/tarps/parachutes). Hazards: animals already inhabiting them (bears, coyotes, lions, rats, snakes; disease from scat or carcasses), inadequate ventilation (smoke buildup dangerous), natural gas pockets in mountain caves, and apparent stability that is actually a collapse waiting to happen.

**Man-made snow shelters:** **snow wall** (extremely expedient, 1–2 men); **snow cave** (1–16 men, extended periods — a candle can raise the inside temperature); **tree-pit snow shelter** (1–3 men, short term); **snow trench** (short-term, extremely hard-packed snow); **A-frame shelter**; **fallen tree bivouac**; **snow coffin**. The handbook's selection table matches snow conditions to shelter: compacted snow over 6 feet deep → snow cave; iced snow → snow trench (2–3 men). Construction considerations: group size, low silhouette, thermal shelter by applying snow to roof and sides, time and effort required, and whether the shelter can actually protect from the elements.

**Fires in winter** follow the summer pattern — fire pit and the tactical Dakota hole, tinder/kindling/fuel progression, flint and steel, magnifying glass, bow drill and fire plow — with the added winter problem that everything is wet, cold, or buried. Gather double handfuls of tinder and store an extra double handful dry for morning; the fire's first job in winter is often drying clothes and melting snow, not cooking. Ventilation is a life-safety item, not a comfort item: a snow shelter with a fire and no ventilation is a carbon-monoxide trap. → `references/snow-shelters-fire.md`

## 5–7. Leadership, signaling, navigation — the shared chapters, winter-applied

**Core values and mountain leadership (Chapter 5).** Altitude, cold, steep terrain, and isolation degrade judgment before they degrade the body. The leader's job is to keep the group on the priorities and to make decisions with incomplete information — in winter, the most common leadership failure is delaying the shelter decision until it is too dark and too cold to build well.

**Signaling and recovery (Chapter 6).** Pyrotechnics (star clusters, smoke grenades — in snowpack, smoke grenades need flotation or they sink out of sight), mirror, lights (flashlight, strobe, chemlight), signaling fires, ground-to-air panels. Snow is a high-contrast background for signals — dark panels, trampled patterns, and smoke show well against it. Recovery discipline is unchanged: be findable, follow the recovery force's instructions, do not abandon a signaled position without a trail and message.

**Survival navigation (Chapter 7).** Daytime (sun, shadows, terrain association), nighttime (stars, moon, slow movement with frequent checks), and survival techniques (dead reckoning with pace count, natural direction indicators). Winter adds snow-specific problems: whiteout destroys terrain association, tracks fill in behind you, and a compass that works fine in summer can be hard to read with gloves and blowing snow. → `references/fieldcraft.md`

## 8–11. Traps, game, tools, foraging — winter food

**Traps and snares (Chapter 8).** Snaring remains the most effective means to take game; location, presentation, construction. Wire snares with a locking device, split-stick support, anchor or drag, quick firing, loop sized to the animal. Deadfalls crush where snares cannot be set. In winter, set on trails animals must use — food and water are concentrated, so trails are predictable.

**Uses of game (Chapter 9).** Butchering, thorough cooking (parasites), specific parts (hides, bones, sinew, fat). In winter, fat is the highest-value part — calories are heat.

**Expedient tools, weapons, equipment (Chapter 10)** and **foraging (Chapter 11).** Same framework as summer: improvise from the environment; multipurpose and durable. The foraging avoid list is unchanged (mushrooms/fungi, umbrella flowers, legumes, 3-leafed/whorled patterns, bulbs, white/yellow berries, milky sap, shiny leaves, skin irritants); boil any unknown plant before testing. In winter, foraging is a marginal source — plan on traps, snares, and fishing, not plants. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 12. Expedient snowshoes — floatation is mobility

Without over-the-snow mobility, movement becomes difficult and dangerous: clothing gets wet, and "post holing" (sinking to the knees or deeper with every step) consumes energy that is vital in a survival situation. Increasing the surface area under the body is the whole idea.

**Advantages:** mobility (floatation), limited maintenance, heavy loads are relatively easy to carry and pull on gentle terrain, movement in confined areas is relatively easy. **Disadvantages:** construction consumes a lot of material (about 70 feet of 550 cord to build one shoe); movement over moderate to steep slopes is extremely difficult; thick forest and branches protruding through snow are difficult; green staves shrink as they dry, so all lashing must be retied.

**Nomenclature:** tip (front of frame), tail (rear), crossbars (three, lashed for reinforcement), webbing (cord latticework — provides floatation and releases snow when the shoe lifts), window (opening letting the boot toe pivot — without it, the boot forces the tip into the snow), frame (resilient green saplings: willow, aspen), binding (8–10 foot piece of cord attaching boot to shoe).

**Construction:** select three straight, resilient green staves, 5 feet long, 1–1.5 inches in diameter; cut one into three 15-inch sections. Join the two 5-foot staves at tips and tails with a shear lash (the frame). Attach the 15-inch sections as crossbars — first 12–14 inches from the tip, second 4–6 inches below the first, last 15 inches below the second — each secured with a square lash. Affix latticework webbing from the first crossbar toward the tip, and a second latticework from the second crossbar toward the tail. If cord or wire is limited, space the latticework out; branches or boughs interwoven increase floatation. Attach the boot with the cord binding system; secure all pigtails with a square knot and two overhand knots.

**Stability is the most important characteristic** — snowshoes must undergo great strain and pressure, so material selection matters. Materials can vary: green saplings, aluminum aircraft skin and ribbed framing, cordage, wire, cargo strapping. **Canadian emergency snowshoes** are the low-cordage alternative: five poles, 6 feet long (the individual's height), ¾ inch at the base tapering to ¼ inch at the tip, per shoe, with 10-inch cross-sticks — an excellent method when saplings are available and cordage is limited. → `references/snowshoes.md`

## 13. Winter tracking — reading snow sign

Snow is the best tracking medium there is: every track is fresh, dated by snowfall, and readable. The chapter teaches reading snow sign for game location (where to set traps and snares), water, and people — and counter-tracking, because your own tracks in snow are a signed confession of where you went. Tracks age visibly (edges soften, snow fills in), so a tracker can judge how old a trail is; that judgment is the winter tracker's core skill. → `references/food-and-tracking.md`

## 14. Survival medicine — cold injuries in depth

**Five ways the body loses heat:** radiation (nude man loses ~60% of total body heat by radiation); conduction (contact with cold ground/snow/rock — insulate with mat or pack); convection (moving air or water); evaporation (one quart of sweat in an hour of hard work carries away ~600 calories); respiration (warmed, saturated air exhaled). Responses: to heat, vessels dilate then sweating; to cold, vessels constrict (blood to the core; fingers and toes go cold), then shivering.

**Hypothermia** (core ≤95°F, number-one killer, most cases at 30–50°F): causes are heat loss exceeding production, with contributing factors of ambient temperature, wind chill (improperly clothed only), wet clothing, cold-water immersion, improper clothing, exhaustion, alcohol/nicotine/drugs, and injuries (immobility, major bleeding, major burns, head trauma). Number-one sign: altered mental status — confusion, slurred speech, strange behavior, irritability, impaired judgment. Worsening brings unconsciousness; severe victims may appear dead. Prevention: stay dry, fed, out of the wind; buddy-system checks. Treatment: rewarm gently and gradually; never give alcohol; handle gently (cold heart can arrest).

**Frostbite** is the actual freezing of tissues and is entirely preventable: keep extremities warm, dry, and moving; no direct high heat on frozen tissue. The chapter also covers heat injuries and dehydration — and in winter, the handbook's warning about super-cooled liquid applies directly to drinking: ingesting super-cooled liquid can cause immediate frostbite of the throat and possible death, which is why snow is melted, never eaten. For current clinical detail, defer to the TCCC/first-aid skills. → `references/medicine-weather.md`

## 15. Weather — winter mountain forecasting

Pressure trends (falling pressure = deteriorating weather), mountain effects (orographic lift, valley winds, afternoon storms in summer; storm systems and wind loading in winter), and field forecasting from clouds, wind, and temperature. In winter, weather reading feeds directly into the avalanche evaluation (Chapter 17): new snowfall, high winds, and temperature trends are avalanche inputs, not just comfort inputs. → `references/medicine-weather.md`

## 17. Avalanche and ice hazards — the winter-only chapter

**Parts of an avalanche:** **starting zone** (usually steeper than 30°, receives large amounts of snow — where unstable snow breaks loose); **avalanche track** (the path under the starting zone, above the runout — channeled tracks are confined gullies and couloirs; unchanneled tracks are open slopes; multi-branch tracks may run several times in quick succession, and rescuers have been killed working a runout when a second avalanche ran within hours; wet-snow avalanches tend to follow the track boundary, dry-snow avalanches can jump terrain barriers); **runout zone** (where debris piles up and comes to rest at the bottom).

**Hazard evaluation — four critical variables:** snowpack, weather, terrain, and the human factor. The evaluation is ongoing and starts before the mission.

- **Snowpack:** can it slide? Each storm adds a layer with its own texture and strength; some bond well, some do not. The task is to determine whether a weak bond exists and how much snow could release.
- **Weather:** all natural avalanches occur during or shortly after a storm — the snowpack cannot handle the new weight. The three main factors are precipitation, wind, and temperature. **Signs of instability:** recent avalanche activity on similar slopes and small avalanches underfoot; booming (audible collapse of snow layers); visible cracks shooting out from underfoot; sluffing debris. **Signs of stability:** settlement cones around trees and obstacles; creep and glide (ripple effect at the bottom of a slope, indicating the snow is gaining equilibrium); absence of wind during storms (snow accumulating in trees); snow temperatures between 25°F and 32°F, which ordinarily settle snow rapidly into a denser, stronger pack. Cold snow (≤25°F) slows settlement, letting unstable conditions persist longer.
- **Terrain:** avalanches can occur on any slope — assuming they only occur on big slopes is a common mistake. **Slope angle:** most slab avalanches release on slopes of **35–40°**; loose-snow avalanches occur on high-angle slopes of **60° and above**. **Orientation:** leeward, wind-loaded slopes increase stress; moderate sun can strengthen a pack, direct sunlight weakens and lubricates bonds; weak layers persist on shaded slopes — suspect instability there. **Anchoring:** slopes with anchors (trees, rocks) are less likely to slide than open slopes. **Vegetation:** the most convincing evidence of past activity is a path of fallen trees aligned in the same direction and sheared at the same height; "flagged" trees (branches void on the uphill side); cleared strips in dense forest. **Seasonal:** once a path has slid in a season, it may slide again along the same path. **Elevation:** temperature, wind, and precipitation vary significantly with elevation — never assume conditions at one elevation reflect another. **Local population** is a good information source, but beware short-term observations.
- **Human factor:** what are your alternatives and their possible consequences? The evaluation must weigh the mission against the risk — the human factor is the variable that kills experienced people who read the other three correctly.

**Ice hazards** accompany the avalanche material: frozen water crossings, thin ice, and the cold-water immersion that follows a breakthrough — which returns the survivor to the hypothermia problem with wet clothing and no shelter. → `references/avalanche-and-evasion.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a survival problem in winter mountain conditions, answer in the handbook's order: attitude and SURVIVAL assessment first, then the priorities of work by time block (first 24 hours: shelter, fire, water, signaling), then the chapter technique — with the winter specifics (melt snow, never eat it; snow shelters; snowshoes; avalanche evaluation). Give the numbers the handbook gives (dehydration percentages, hypothermia at 95°F core, slab release at 35–40°, starting zone steeper than 30°, 70 feet of 550 cord per snowshoe, 25–32°F settling band) and say when a number comes from the handbook versus general knowledge. ## Operating Rules

1. **Attitude first, heat second.** Without the will to survive, technique does not matter; without heat retention, nothing else matters.
2. **Work the priorities in time blocks.** First 24 hours: shelter, fire, water, signaling.
3. **Melt snow; never eat it.** Eating snow removes body heat and invites cold injury; melt it, preferably starting with a little water and a hot rock.
4. **Over-drink — cold hides thirst.** Respiration and sweat under layers dehydrate you without a thirst signal.
5. **Snow is shelter and transport.** Build with it (snow cave, trench, wall), move on it (snowshoes), read it (tracking) — and respect it (avalanche).
6. **Evaluate avalanches on four variables, always.** Snowpack, weather, terrain, human factor — before the mission and continuously during it.
7. **Signal early.** Snow is a high-contrast background; use it.
