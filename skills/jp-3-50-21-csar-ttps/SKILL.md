# JP 3-50.21 Joint TTPs for Combat Search and Rescue

## Purpose

JP 3-50.21, *Joint Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures for Combat Search and Rescue* (23 March 1998), is the execution companion to JP 3-50.2. Where JP 3-50.2 assigns responsibility and builds the JSRC/RCC system, this publication tells commanders, staffs, and aircrews how a CSAR operation is actually run: the five stages from notification to mission conclusion, how the search is conducted, how the recovery force is built and protected, and who does what inside a CSAR task force. Its epigraph is a radio call from Bosnia in 1995 — "Basher 52 reads you loud and clear" — the transmission that began the successful recovery of Captain Scott O'Grady. The manual is written around that kind of event: a real aircrew down in hostile territory, a real clock running, and a real enemy trying to get to them first.

Two rules govern everything in this publication. First, **recovery forces will not normally launch until they have determined a positive location and verified a CSAR situation, and recovery is not normally initiated until the isolated person has been authenticated.** Second, **speed is essential** — the rescue location and coverage section states it plainly: speed is essential to the successful rescue of personnel lost in a threat environment. Every procedure here exists to reconcile those two rules: go fast, but go to the right place, for the right person, with the right force. → `references/stages-of-a-csar-operation.md`

## 1. The Five Stages

CSAR operations divide into five interrelated stages (Figure III-1):

1. **Awareness and notification** — someone learns an aircraft is down or a person is isolated, and the word reaches the component RCC and the JSRC.
2. **Situation assessment** — all available information is analyzed to refine the isolated person's location and status.
3. **Mission planning** — courses of action are developed, forces selected, and the go/no-go decision made.
4. **Execution** — the search refines the location, the task force launches, and the recovery is made.
5. **Mission conclusion** — the mission is closed out, the recovered person is debriefed, and the record is kept.

The stages overlap in practice — assessment continues through execution, and planning continues until launch — but the sequence is the mental model every CSAR practitioner uses. Rescue forces may be notified through any portion of the joint or component force command-and-control structure; the notification does not have to arrive through the "right" channel to count.

## 2. Awareness and Notification

**2.1. How notification starts.** Notification of a downed aircraft or isolated person begins the rescue process and is forwarded through the component RCC or its equivalent to the JSRC. If recovery requires forces from components other than the isolated person's own, the JSRC or RCC coordinates the appropriate CSAR force and assets. The time from notification to mission launch varies with the threat level and other operational requirements. All aircraft should monitor emergency frequencies and acknowledge or record personnel distress transmissions if possible.

**2.2. In flight — the distressed aircrew.** When pilots detect significant aircraft problems, or when bailout, ejection, crash landing, or ditching appears imminent, they should (conditions permitting): attempt radio contact by calling "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY" on the frequency of last contact, an established common frequency, or the international emergency frequencies, and transmit tactical call sign, type of aircraft, position, course, speed, altitude, nature of difficulties, and intentions. In a nonpermissive environment, give position in a way that reduces the likelihood the enemy will ascertain the location — for example, position from a BULLSEYE point, or from the mission's target as designated in the air tasking order. In a permissive environment, maintain the transmission long enough to permit a direction-finder plot. If communications have not been established or are uncertain, broadcast the information in the blind. Momentarily set IFF to emergency — weighing transponder use and duration against the enemy's probability of detection.

**2.3. On the ground.** Ground personnel should be prepared to request extraction through normal communications channels or directly through airborne command-and-control platforms, and to provide authentication information.

**2.4. Communications relay.** Any friendly force receiving information about distressed aircraft or isolated personnel should forward the details (by secure means if possible) to the nearest monitoring agency — ABCCC, AWACS, E-2C, or JSRC. Extreme care is taken to ensure the isolated person's situation is not compromised and that relay transmissions do not interfere with distress calls.

**2.5. The witness aircrew.** An aircrew observing a bailout, crash, or ditching should: remain in the area as conditions permit or until relieved (but do not circle directly over the survivor — that marks the position for hostile forces); keep the parachute(s) in sight; note the approximate ejection site and winds at altitude so planners can compute the probable landing position; consider switching IFF to emergency and transmitting "MAYDAY" on GUARD (weighed against enemy detection); provide communications relay and defensive cover if possible; assume the role of on-scene commander as threat and mission allow, until relieved by an OSC of the arriving task force; and provide updates — possible injuries, disposition and movement of hostile forces, terrain factors, possible recovery sites. If a bailout, crash, or ditching is observed by another aircrew or by ground personnel, the information in Figure III-2 (call sign, type and color of aircraft, location, day and time, cause, number on board, medical status, radio frequencies, authentication information, weather, enemy activity) should be provided to the JSRC.

## 3. Situation Assessment — Refining Location and Status

**3.1. What assessment is.** Situation assessment is analyzing all available information to refine the isolated person's location and their status. Upon confirmation of an isolated person, the JSRC should immediately send out the incident report with the Central Intelligence Agency and National Reconnaissance Office addresses included, so national systems can begin working immediately to refine the location. The JSRC develops courses of action and uses all available assets to refine the location of the downed aircrew and determine their status, and should be prepared to launch the recovery effort as soon as possible after determining location and status.

**3.2. The objective area.** The objective area is designated as a predetermined radius — usually **5 to 10 minutes of helicopter flying time** — surrounding the isolated person's expected location. Assets the JSRC should consider tasking to refine the location include the wingman, AWACS, E-2C, ABCCC, Rivet Joint, JSTARS, COMPASS CALL, ground control intercept, direction finding, FAC(A)s, intelligence, and electronic and visual search. This assumes an electronically permissive environment; denial of the communications environment by the enemy hampers location efforts. Standoff electronic support aircraft should be used for electronic search if possible, and faster-moving direction-finding-capable aircraft only if the enemy's direction-finding capabilities will not contribute to the capture of the isolated person.

**3.3. The airborne mission commander in assessment.** The AMC should be positioned on airborne alert as close to hostile areas as possible, with orbit locations specified in fragmentary orders or SPINS — and the AMC has the prerogative to move the orbit in response to operational situations. Visual coverage of the rescue scene is not required for AMC operations as long as visual coverage is maintained by the OSC. The AMC mission is normally conducted at high altitude, but threat dictates actual altitudes; the AMC needs radio line-of-sight with the OSC and HF or SATCOM communications with the JSRC. The AMC must be proactive and aggressive in taking control of the situation, keeping the OSC and/or CSARTF and RCC informed of threats, aborts, fades, and electronic warfare information. AMC-capable aircraft must be prepared to assume AMC duties in a combat environment.

**3.4. Isolated personnel considerations.** Effective search planning needs, in addition to charts and imagery of the objective area: the isolated person's last known location, physical condition, direction of travel, and proximity to population centers, threats, or safe areas (SAFEs). The last known direction of travel is superimposed on a chart of the objective area; natural boundaries and features — hills, foliage, streams, lakes — may enhance or restrict movement. The EPA provides the proposed route of travel, expected concealment, and logical actions on entering a survival situation. The EPA and ISOPREP should be readily available to the JSRC or RCC.

## 4. Mission Planning — Forces, Threat, and the Go/No-Go

**4.1. Planning factors.** In conjunction with component RCCs, the JSRC establishes broad CSAR planning guidelines. Selecting forces — type and composition — requires: an overall situation assessment; a review of employment options that may satisfy mission requirements; and the tactics, training, capabilities, and limitations of available CSAR or supporting forces. Once the isolated person's location is known, planning focuses on avoiding or degrading anticipated en route and objective-area threats.

**4.2. Go/no-go criteria.** Each component should develop a CSAR go/no-go criteria matrix, and joint CSAR planners must understand the interrelationships of the various component decision matrices. The matrices give commanders a framework for informed go/no-go or abort decisions. Component CSAR go/no-go criteria are forwarded to the JSRC.

**4.3. Threat conditions.** The JSRC and CSARTF participants carefully analyze two different threats — the threat to aircraft and the threat to ground forces — because they drive different force requirements. Joint intelligence preparation of the battlespace defines the threat, divided into three general intensity levels: **low, medium, and high** (Figure III-3). Force composition scales with the level: a low-threat recovery may be a single helicopter with minimal escort; a high-threat recovery is a full task force with suppression, escort, and command-and-control aircraft.

**4.4. Preplanned CSAR options.** Figure III-4 lays out preplanned CSAR options — alert postures and force packages prepared in advance so the response does not start from zero when the call comes. The doctrine's planning bias is toward having the package built before the incident: alert helicopters, designated escorts, AMC aircraft, and communications plans already assigned.

## 5. Execution — The Recovery

**5.1. Responsibilities of isolated personnel and/or evaders.** Figure III-5 lists them: establish communications when possible and safe; authenticate; follow the EPA; use signaling (including alternate signaling methods in Figure III-6 — panels, mirrors, smoke, lights, ground signals, and improvised markers); conserve resources; avoid capture; and move toward the recovery site or SAFE as directed. The isolated person's discipline in the execution phase — short transmissions, correct authentication, visible but not enemy-revealing signals — is often the difference between a recovery and a compromise.

**5.2. Execution sequence.** The task force launches when location is positive, the situation is verified, and authentication is established. The AMC controls the airborne effort; the OSC controls the scene; the helicopter recovery force makes the pickup; RESCORT protects it. The mission is executed against the plan but adapted continuously to threat, weather, and the isolated person's status. Abort criteria from the go/no-go matrix apply throughout — a mission can be aborted at any stage if the threat or the situation no longer supports success.

## 6. Mission Conclusion

**6.1. Closing the mission.** Mission conclusion covers: confirming the recovery (or the decision to suspend/terminate the effort), accounting for all personnel and equipment, reporting the outcome through the JSRC/RCC chain, and standing down alert forces. A CSAR mission is never closed simply because it is hard — but it can be placed on hold or terminated by the appropriate commander when the criteria for execution are no longer met and further effort is not supportable.

**6.2. Debriefing and records.** Recovered personnel are debriefed by intelligence personnel in accordance with JP 3-50.3. The file on each isolated person is maintained until recovery is complete, then forwarded to the Joint Services SERE Agency and not destroyed. Lessons from the mission feed back into planning, SPINS, and training — the next incident inherits the last one's lessons only if someone writes them down.

## 7. Search and Recovery Operations (Chapter IV)

**7.1. Initial assessment and risk reduction.** Before any search aircraft crosses into threat territory, the initial assessment weighs the threat, the quality of the location, and the risk to the search force. Risk reduction measures include standoff electronic search, using intelligence and surveillance assets before committing manned search aircraft, and deconflicting airspace and altitudes.

**7.2. Search and locating methods.** Figure IV-1 catalogs search and locating methods — electronic (direction finding, homing, radar, beacon location) and visual (air and ground visual search, search patterns). Search patterns (Figure IV-2) are the standard geometries — track, parallel, creeping line, expanding square, sector — chosen by the size and shape of the search area, the terrain, and the sensor. The search force works the objective area systematically; random searching wastes the one resource that matters most, time.

**7.3. Objective area search operations.** Inside the objective area (that 5–10 minute helicopter radius around the expected location), search operations refine the location to a point: electronic homing on the survivor's beacon or radio, visual acquisition of signals or the survivor, and confirmation by authentication. The AMC establishes search areas and controls the search aircraft; the OSC, once on scene, directs the final location effort.

**7.4. Single-ship helicopter operations.** A single helicopter can execute a recovery in a low-threat environment or when no other assets are available. The single ship must do everything itself — locate, communicate, defend, and recover — which limits it to permissive conditions and short exposure. The manual treats single-ship operations as the fallback, not the norm, for combat recovery.

**7.5. Multi-ship helicopter operations.** Multi-ship formations are the norm for combat recovery: mutual support, redundancy if one aircraft is hit or has a malfunction, and the ability to split roles (one aircraft recovers while another provides cover, communications relay, or a second pickup option). Formations are planned with deconflicted altitudes, routes, and roles briefed before launch.

**7.6. Other recovery modes.** Conventional ground force recovery — a ground unit moves to the isolated person and extracts them, used when the threat or terrain rules out aviation or when ground forces are already closest. Special operations recovery — SOF elements conduct the recovery when the mission is beyond conventional forces, in denied areas, or requires specialized capabilities. Fixed-wing recovery — fixed-wing aircraft recover isolated personnel (including Fulton-type or air-to-air retrieval concepts where applicable) or support the recovery. Naval vessel recovery — ships and boats recover personnel in the maritime environment, coordinated with air search.

## 8. Task Force Operations (Chapter V)

**8.1. The CSAR task force.** The CSARTF is a tailored package built for one mission: recovery aircraft, escort, command and control, suppression, and support, sized to the threat (low/medium/high) and the objective area. Figure II-5 shows typical CSARTF compositions. The task force exists to do two things at once: recover the isolated person and protect the recovery force while doing it.

**8.2. Airborne mission commander (AMC).** The AMC is the airborne commander of the CSAR effort: controls the task force in the air, coordinates all participating aircraft, maintains communications with the JSRC/RCC and the OSC, manages altitude deconfliction and airspace, and makes real-time decisions on search, threat response, and abort. Appendix A is the AMC CSAR checklist. The AMC's authority and aggressiveness matter — the manual expects the AMC to take control of the situation, not wait for direction from the ground.

**8.3. Rescue combat air patrol (RESCAP).** Fighter aircraft assigned to protect the recovery force and the isolated person from air and surface threats. RESCAP holds air superiority over the objective area for the duration of the recovery, engages threats, and deters enemy interference.

**8.4. Forward air controller, airborne (FAC(A)).** The airborne FAC controls close air support and strike aircraft in support of the recovery, marks targets, and coordinates fires so that suppression lands where it helps and nowhere near the survivor or the helicopters.

**8.5. On-scene commander (OSC).** The OSC is the commander at the recovery scene — often the first aircraft or ground element to arrive. The OSC directs the immediate recovery: locates and authenticates the survivor, controls aircraft in the objective area, coordinates the pickup, and reports to the AMC. Appendix B is the OSC checklist. The OSC role can pass from the witness aircrew to the arriving task force's designated OSC.

**8.6. Helicopter recovery force.** The helicopters that make the pickup, with their crews, pararescuemen or rescue specialists, and equipment (hoists, ropes, litters, medical gear). The recovery force's procedures cover approach, hover, hoist or landing pickup, casualty handling, and egress — all under the OSC's direction and the AMC's control.

**8.7. RESCORT — rescue escort.** RESCORT is escort specifically for the recovery aircraft: fixed-wing or helicopter gunship escort that flies with, ahead of, or above the helicopters to suppress threats en route and in the objective area. Types of RESCORT (Figure V) include fixed-wing RESCORT, helicopter gunship RESCORT, and helicopter RESCORT tactics; hover cover is the technique of an escort aircraft holding overhead while the recovery helicopter hovers for the pickup; night vision goggle/device escort adapts RESCORT to night operations. General RESCORT concepts cover positioning, weapons employment, communications, and the handoff between en route escort and objective-area cover. The escort's job is to make the helicopter's few minutes of maximum vulnerability — the hover over the survivor — survivable.

## 9. Organization and Planning (Chapter II)

**9.1. General characteristics.** CSAR is a joint mission that depends on interoperability: common procedures, common communications, and forces that can work together without rehearsal. The JSRC is the hub; the RCCs are the spokes; the task force is the muscle.

**9.2. JSRC organization.** The JSRC's responsibilities and functions differ between peacetime (Figure II-1: developing SOPs, plans, training) and combat operations (Figure II-2: threat matrices, SPINS, incident monitoring, coordination). Staffing and experience levels matter: the JSRC needs trained, experienced personnel who know CSAR plans and the capabilities of each component's forces. Documentation requirements include the CSAR appendix to Annex C, SPINS, ISOPREPs, EPAs, and the incident files. Physical requirements include communications facilities (Figure II-4: interoperable communications facilities), plotting and display, and space for continuous operations. Logistics planning covers the sustainment of the JSRC and of deployed CSAR forces. Coordination procedures cover the links between JSRC, RCCs, the Joint Air Operations Center, component staffs, and adjacent JSRCs.

**9.3. CSAR force composition and capabilities.** Figure II-5 and Appendix G catalog CSAR-capable assets — which aircraft, units, and equipment can do CSAR, and what each can and cannot do. Planners build the task force from this catalog, not from a wish list. The force must match the threat level, the environment, the distance, and the isolated person's condition.

## 10. The Checklists (Appendixes A–H)

The publication's most directly usable content is its checklists, and they are the reason this volume is carried into operations:

- **Appendix A — Airborne Mission Commander CSAR Checklist.** The AMC's step-by-step: notification, assessment, planning, launch, search control, recovery control, egress, and conclusion.
- **Appendix B — On-Scene Commander Checklist.** The OSC's steps at the scene: locate, authenticate, control, recover, report.
- **Appendix C — CSAR Communications Planning Checklist.** Frequencies, call signs, authentication procedures, reporting formats, and backup communications.
- **Appendix D — ISOPREP Data Collection Checklist and Procedures.** What to collect, how to verify it, and how to keep it current.
- **Appendix E — Evasion Plan of Action Format.** The standard EPA structure.
- **Appendix F — Sample Air Tasking Order.** How CSAR tasking appears in the ATO, including SPINS.
- **Appendix G — CSAR-Capable Assets.** The catalog of forces and their capabilities.
- **Appendix H — Mission Briefing Guide.** The standard brief for a CSAR mission.

A practitioner who has never read the chapters can still execute from these appendixes — and a commander who has read the chapters will still use the checklists to make sure nothing is skipped under pressure.

## Output Contract

When asked how a CSAR operation is executed, answer in the five stages: awareness and notification, situation assessment, mission planning, execution, mission conclusion. For each stage, name who acts (JSRC, RCC, AMC, OSC, witness aircrew, isolated person), what information moves, and what the decision is. When asked about the task force, name its elements (AMC, RESCAP, FAC(A), OSC, helicopter recovery force, RESCORT) and each element's job. When asked about search, give the objective-area radius (5–10 minutes of helicopter flying time), the search methods, and the pattern logic. When asked about the go/no-go, describe the component criteria matrices and the low/medium/high threat levels. Cite the checklists (Appendixes A–H) as the executable form of the doctrine. Distinguish TTP (this publication) from doctrine (JP 3-50.2) and from evasion-and-recovery doctrine (JP 3-50.3).

## Operating Rules

1. **No launch without a positive location and a verified CSAR situation; no recovery without authentication.** Both rules admit rare exceptions, but they are the default and the burden of proof is on deviating.
2. **Speed is essential — but not at the cost of the wrong place or the wrong person.** Assessment and planning run in parallel with notification, not after it.
3. **The AMC takes control.** The airborne mission commander is proactive and aggressive in taking control of the situation; the OSC owns the scene.
4. **Do not circle over the survivor.** A witness aircrew that hovers over the survivor marks the position for the enemy.
5. **Match the force to the threat.** Low, medium, and high threat levels drive different task forces; a single ship is the fallback, not the plan.
6. **Use the checklists.** Appendixes A–H exist so that nothing is skipped under pressure. Brief from Appendix H; execute from Appendixes A and B.
7. **Close the mission properly.** Debrief the recovered person, keep the file, and feed the lessons back into the next plan.
