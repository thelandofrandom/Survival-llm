# Stages of a CSAR Operation — JP 3-50.21

Distilled from JP 3-50.21, Chapter III (23 March 1998). Paraphrase with short quotes only; full text in `full-text.md`.

## The five stages (Figure III-1)

1. Awareness and notification
2. Situation assessment
3. Mission planning
4. Execution
5. Mission conclusion

Stages overlap in practice, but the sequence is the mental model. Rescue forces may be notified through any portion of the joint or component C2 structure.

## Stage 1 — Awareness and notification

- Notification of a downed aircraft or isolated person begins the process; forwarded through the component RCC or equivalent to the JSRC.
- If recovery needs forces from other components, the JSRC/RCC coordinates them.
- Time from notification to launch varies with threat and operational requirements.
- All aircraft monitor emergency frequencies and acknowledge or record distress transmissions if possible.

### Distressed aircrew (in flight)

- Call "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY" on frequency of last contact, an established common frequency, or international emergency frequencies.
- Transmit: tactical call sign, type of aircraft, position, course, speed, altitude, nature of difficulties, intentions.
- In a nonpermissive environment, give position indirectly — from a BULLSEYE point or from the mission target as designated in the ATO — so the enemy cannot fix the location.
- In a permissive environment, hold the transmission long enough for a direction-finder plot.
- If no contact is established, broadcast in the blind.
- Momentarily set IFF to emergency; weigh transponder use and duration against enemy detection probability.

### Ground personnel

- Request extraction through normal communications channels or directly through airborne C2 platforms.
- Be prepared to provide authentication information.

### Communications relay

- Any friendly force receiving distress information forwards details (secure means if possible) to the nearest monitoring agency: ABCCC, AWACS, E-2C, or JSRC.
- Do not compromise the isolated person's situation; do not interfere with distress calls.

### Witness aircrew

- Remain in the area as conditions permit or until relieved. **Do not circle directly over the survivor** — it marks the position for hostile forces.
- Keep the parachute(s) in sight.
- Note the approximate ejection site and winds at altitude (planners compute probable landing position).
- Consider IFF to emergency and "MAYDAY" on GUARD, weighed against enemy detection.
- Provide communications relay and defensive cover if possible.
- Assume the OSC role as threat and mission allow, until relieved by an OSC of the arriving task force.
- Provide updates: possible injuries, hostile force disposition and movement, terrain, possible recovery sites.
- Information to send the JSRC (Figure III-2): call sign, type and color of aircraft, location or approximate location (landmarks/nav aids), day and time, cause, number on board, medical status, radio frequencies, authentication information, weather, enemy air/naval/surface activity, other pertinent information.

## Stage 2 — Situation assessment

- Analyze all available information to refine the isolated person's location and status.
- On confirmation of an isolated person, the JSRC immediately sends the incident report with CIA and NRO addresses included so national systems begin refining the location at once.
- The JSRC develops courses of action and uses all available assets to refine location and determine status; be prepared to launch as soon as possible after location and status are determined.
- **Objective area:** a predetermined radius, usually **5–10 minutes of helicopter flying time**, around the isolated person's expected location.
- Assets to refine location: wingman, AWACS, E-2C, ABCCC, Rivet Joint, JSTARS, COMPASS CALL, ground control intercept, direction finding, FAC(A)s, intelligence, electronic and visual search. Assumes an electronically permissive environment; enemy denial of communications hampers location efforts. Use standoff electronic support aircraft if possible; use faster DF-capable aircraft only if enemy DF will not contribute to the isolated person's capture.
- AMC: positioned on airborne alert as close to hostile areas as possible; orbit locations in FRAGOs or SPINS; AMC may move the orbit as the situation requires. Normally high altitude, threat dictates actual altitudes. Needs radio LOS with the OSC and HF or SATCOM with the JSRC. Visual coverage of the scene is not required for the AMC as long as the OSC maintains it. The AMC is proactive and aggressive in taking control, keeping OSC/CSARTF/RCC informed of threats, aborts, fades, and EW information (Appendix A checklist).
- Isolated personnel data needed: last known location, physical condition, direction of travel, proximity to population centers, threats, or SAFEs. Last known direction of travel is superimposed on a chart of the objective area; natural boundaries (hills, foliage, streams, lakes) may enhance or restrict movement. The EPA gives proposed route, expected concealment, and logical actions on entering a survival situation. EPA and ISOPREP must be readily available to the JSRC/RCC.

## Stage 3 — Mission planning

- JSRC with component RCCs establishes broad planning guidelines. Force selection considers: overall situation assessment; employment options that may satisfy mission requirements; tactics, training, capabilities, and limitations of available CSAR/supporting forces.
- Once location is known, planning focuses on avoiding or degrading anticipated en route and objective-area threats.
- **Go/no-go criteria:** each component develops a CSAR go/no-go criteria matrix; joint planners must understand the interrelationships of the component matrices; criteria are forwarded to the JSRC. The matrices frame informed go/no-go and abort decisions.
- **Threat conditions:** analyze the threat to aircraft and the threat to ground forces separately — they drive different force requirements. JIPB defines the threat in three intensity levels: **low, medium, high** (Figure III-3).
- **Preplanned CSAR options** (Figure III-4): alert postures and force packages prepared in advance.

## Stage 4 — Execution

- Isolated personnel/evader responsibilities (Figure III-5): establish communications when possible and safe; authenticate; follow the EPA; use signaling (alternate methods in Figure III-6: panels, mirrors, smoke, lights, ground signals, improvised markers); conserve resources; avoid capture; move toward the recovery site or SAFE as directed.
- Task force launches when location is positive, situation verified, authentication established.
- AMC controls the airborne effort; OSC controls the scene; helicopter recovery force makes the pickup; RESCORT protects it.
- Abort criteria from the go/no-go matrix apply throughout — the mission can be aborted at any stage if threat or situation no longer supports success.

## Stage 5 — Mission conclusion

- Confirm the recovery (or the decision to suspend/terminate); account for all personnel and equipment; report the outcome through the JSRC/RCC chain; stand down alert forces.
- A mission is never closed simply because it is hard, but it can be placed on hold or terminated by the appropriate commander when execution criteria are no longer met.
- Recovered personnel are debriefed by intelligence personnel per JP 3-50.3. Files on each isolated person are maintained until recovery is complete, then forwarded to the Joint Services SERE Agency — never destroyed.
- Lessons feed back into planning, SPINS, and training.
