# Search and Recovery Operations — JP 3-50.21

Distilled from JP 3-50.21, Chapter IV (23 March 1998). Paraphrase with short quotes only; full text in `full-text.md`.

## Initial assessment and risk reduction

- Before search aircraft enter threat territory, weigh the threat, the quality of the location, and the risk to the search force.
- Risk reduction: standoff electronic search; intelligence and surveillance assets before committing manned search aircraft; deconflicted airspace and altitudes.

## Search and locating methods (Figure IV-1)

- **Electronic:** direction finding, homing, radar, beacon location.
- **Visual:** air and ground visual search, using standard search patterns.
- Choice depends on the objective area, terrain, threat, weather, time of day, and available assets.

## Search patterns (Figure IV-2)

Standard geometries, chosen by the size and shape of the search area, terrain, and sensor:

- **Track** — along a known route or track line.
- **Parallel** — parallel sweeps across a rectangular area.
- **Creeping line** — parallel sweeps advancing along a track.
- **Expanding square** — outward spiral from a known point; used when the location is fairly well fixed.
- **Sector** — radial sweeps from a central point.

Work the objective area systematically. Random searching wastes time, the one resource that matters most.

## Objective-area search operations

- The objective area is the 5–10 minute helicopter radius around the expected location.
- Inside it, the search refines location to a point: electronic homing on the survivor's beacon or radio, visual acquisition of signals or the survivor, confirmation by authentication.
- The AMC establishes search areas and controls search aircraft; the OSC, once on scene, directs the final location effort.
- AMC discretion: use standoff electronic support aircraft for electronic search if possible; commit DF-capable aircraft only if enemy DF will not help capture the isolated person.

## Single-ship (helicopter) operations

- One helicopter locates, communicates, defends, and recovers by itself.
- Feasible in a low-threat environment or when no other assets are available.
- Limited to permissive conditions and short exposure. The fallback, not the norm, for combat recovery.

## Multi-ship (helicopter) operations

- The norm for combat recovery.
- Mutual support; redundancy if one aircraft is hit or malfunctions; role split (one aircraft recovers while another covers, relays communications, or offers a second pickup option).
- Formations planned with deconflicted altitudes, routes, and roles briefed before launch.

## Conventional ground force recovery

- A ground unit moves to the isolated person and extracts them.
- Used when threat or terrain rules out aviation, or when ground forces are already closest.
- Requires coordination with the AMC/OSC for airspace, fires, and communications.

## Special operations recovery

- SOF elements conduct the recovery when the mission is beyond conventional forces, in denied areas, or requires specialized capabilities.
- Coordinated through the JSRC with the J-3 and the special operations component.

## Fixed-wing recovery

- Fixed-wing aircraft recover isolated personnel or support the recovery (including air-to-air retrieval concepts where applicable).
- Fixed-wing assets also provide search, communications relay, and escort.

## Naval vessel recovery

- Ships and boats recover personnel in the maritime environment, coordinated with air search.
- Maritime search planning factors (drift, water temperature) are in JP 3-50 Vol. I and JP 3-50.1 Vol. II.
