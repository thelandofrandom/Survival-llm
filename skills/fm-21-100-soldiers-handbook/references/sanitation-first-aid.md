# Sanitation, First Aid, Ration, Pay, Will — FM 21-100 (Chapters 14–17, ¶¶237–295)

## Military Sanitation (¶¶237–252)

Report illness to a medical officer at once — never treat yourself for disease; the danger of infecting comrades is greatest at the start of an illness. Bathe at least twice a week; wash hands before eating and after the toilet; change underwear, shirts, and socks at least twice a week; sun clothing 2 hours if water is unavailable; watch for lice. Change wet clothes and shoes promptly. Brush teeth twice daily. Use the latrine, never the ground — flies carry germs to food and water. Drink from your own cup only. Use the mosquito bar, tucked in, without holes. Kill flies; keep screens closed; keep food and garbage containers shut.

**In the field (¶251):** drink only water a medical officer has passed as pure (sign posted); when ordered to boil, boil **20 minutes**; drink only from the Lyster sterilizing bag, letting water run from the faucet into your own cup — never dip a cup in or put lips to the faucet. Wash mess gear in hot soapy water, rinse in boiling water. Do not sit or lie on damp ground; avoid drafts while hot or wet. Air blankets and clothing in the sun daily; ditch the tent as soon as it is up. "The most important thing in your marching ability as a soldier is the care of your feet" (¶252).

## First Aid (¶¶253–275)

**Governing rules (¶253):** do not get excited — act quickly but quietly; be gentle; do not try to do too much; call a Medical Department man as soon as possible; make the casualty sit or lie down; never move him until help comes unless he cannot be kept warm where he is or must be moved to reach help; warmth is most important against shock, even on a warm day (hot-water canteens under armpits and between legs, outside clothing; blankets, coats, newspapers); a conscious casualty not wounded in stomach, throat, or mouth may have a hot drink; never pour liquids into an unconscious man's mouth; do not try to bring an unconscious wounded man to consciousness; never touch a wound with anything unclean and do not wash the wound.

**First-aid packet (¶¶254–255):** airtight container in a web pouch on the belt, ring down so it cannot open accidentally; two sterile dressings (gauze bandage 4 in. wide, 84 in. long, compress sewn in the middle) and two safety pins; never opened until needed.

**Topics covered:** hemorrhage and control of bleeding (¶¶256–257), tourniquet (¶258), fractures (¶¶259–260), poisoned wounds incl. snakebite (¶261), fainting (¶262), burns (¶263), sunstroke (¶264), heat exhaustion (¶265), freezing (¶266), abdominal pain (¶267), athlete's foot (¶268), war gases and peacetime gases (¶¶269–270), artificial respiration (¶271), drowning — under water over 5 minutes (¶272), electrical shock (¶273), litters and other transport (¶¶274–275).

**Historic caution:** tourniquet guidance, snakebite treatment, and burn care in this chapter are obsolete. Use TCCC (already a skill in this vault) for current casualty care.

## Ration, Pay, Will (Chapters 15–17, ¶¶276–295)

- **Ration (¶¶276–282):** one man's food for one day; kinds of ration; mess in garrison and on campaign; fire for cooking — a small, hot fire, tended so it cooks without smoke and waste.
- **Pay and allowances (¶¶283–294):** 1940 rates — entry pay, specialist and Air Corps additions, decorations, furlough allowance, deposits, allotments, government insurance, deductions, travel pay on honorable discharge, burial expenses. Historical interest only.
- **Last will and testament (¶295):** every soldier should have his will drawn up, witnessed, and kept where it can be found.
