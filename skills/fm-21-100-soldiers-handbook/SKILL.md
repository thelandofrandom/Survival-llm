# FM 21-100 Soldier's Handbook

## Purpose

FM 21-100, *Soldier's Handbook* (War Department, 11 December 1940, printed 1941, 262 pages) — the Basic Field Manual written for the newly enrolled member of the United States Army, to give him "a convenient and compact source of basic military information and thus to aid him to perform his duties more efficiently." It was prepared under the direction of the Chief of Staff and signed by order of the Secretary of War, G. C. Marshall. Its foreword tells the new soldier plainly what is expected: know your own job and be ready to step into the job of the man ahead of you — promotion will be rapid, and "at a moment's notice you may have to take charge of your squad as a corporal."

The handbook runs 17 chapters plus an appendix glossary: general information and the Articles of War (Ch. 1), discipline and courtesy (Ch. 2), insignia (Ch. 3), organization (Ch. 4), clothing (Ch. 5), arms and equipment (Ch. 6), school of the soldier without arms (Ch. 7) and with arms (Ch. 8), squad and platoon drill (Ch. 9), interior guard duty (Ch. 10), marches, camps, and bivouacs (Ch. 11), compasses and maps (Ch. 12), security and protection (Ch. 13), military sanitation and first aid (Ch. 14), the ration (Ch. 15), pay and allowances (Ch. 16), and last will and testament (Ch. 17).

**Historic — read this first.** This is a 1940–41 manual for a draftee army on the eve of World War II. Much of it is period-bound and must not be followed as current practice: the weapons are the U.S. rifle caliber .30 M1 and the automatic pistol caliber .45 M1911; the organization is the pre-war Army with horse-mounted units still in the text; pay rates, allowances, and the Articles of War are those of 1940; and some medical advice (notably the first-aid chapter) has been superseded by modern doctrine — tourniquet use, snakebite care, and burn treatment in particular have changed substantially, and the manual's own instruction "do not try to treat yourself" for illness sits alongside first-aid procedures a modern reader must check against current training. Its durable value is the basic-soldier core: discipline and courtesy, care of clothing and equipment, foot care and marching, camp sanitation, compass and map reading, guard duty, and security of the individual — the parts that change least. Where this handbook and current Army doctrine disagree, current doctrine governs. The manual is written entirely in the masculine language of its time ("him," "his," "the soldier"); this skill preserves its content without updating its voice.

## 1. The Soldier in the Army (Chapter 1, ¶¶1–25)

**1.1. Group life.** The soldier's first duty is to fit into group life: responsibilities to his squad, his mess, and his barracks. The Army, the foreword says, is "made up of free citizens chosen from among a free people," and the soldier is part of upholding "the glorious reputation of the Army."

**1.2. Relations with NCOs, officers, and civilians.** Respect for noncommissioned officers and officers is the working basis of discipline; the soldier obeys promptly and without argument, and takes up questions through proper channels. With civilians he is courteous and restrained — civilians will judge the whole Army by the conduct and appearance of its members in public, a point the manual repeats in the marching chapter.

**1.3. Military obligations and the Articles of War.** The soldier's obligations include obedience to lawful orders, loyalty, and readiness. Chapter 1 summarizes the Articles of War — the governing military law of the time, later replaced by the Uniform Code of Military Justice (1951) — so the new soldier knows what offenses (including absence without leave and desertion) and what punishments the law provides.

**1.4. Post and station activities.** The chapter closes with the routines of post life: reveille, mess, duty assignments, and the daily round the soldier must learn to move through without confusion. → `references/discipline-organization-insignia.md`

## 2. Military Discipline and Courtesy (Chapter 2, ¶¶26–33)

**2.1. Discipline.** Military discipline is prompt, willing obedience — not fear, but habit formed by training. It shows in small things: being on time, keeping equipment ready, doing the job without being told twice.

**2.2. Courtesy.** Military courtesy is the outward form of discipline: saluting officers, addressing them by rank, standing at attention when addressed, and the courtesies owed to the flag and to the national anthem. The manual treats courtesy not as ceremony for its own sake but as the visible sign that discipline is working — and as the thing civilians notice first. → `references/discipline-organization-insignia.md`

## 3. Insignia and Organization (Chapters 3–4, ¶¶34–53)

**3.1. Insignia.** Chapter 3 teaches the insignia of arms and services and of officers and noncommissioned officers, so the new soldier can recognize at a glance what branch a man belongs to and what rank he holds — the minimum literacy for functioning in a large organization.

**3.2. Organization.** Chapter 4 lays out the structure from squad upward: the squad is the smallest fighting unit; squads form platoons, platoons companies, companies battalions, battalions regiments, and so on through brigade, division, corps, and army. The manual explains the arms and services — infantry, cavalry, field artillery, coast artillery, engineers, signal, medical, quartermaster, ordnance, chemical warfare, air corps — and what each does, so the soldier understands where his own unit sits in the whole. → `references/discipline-organization-insignia.md`

## 4. Clothing (Chapter 5, ¶¶54–59)

**4.1. Allowances.** Clothing is issued on an allowance basis; the soldier is responsible for what he draws and must account for it.

**4.2. Care.** Keep clothing clean, mended, and properly stored. Brush uniforms, press when required, and repair small tears and loose buttons promptly — a stitch in time is literally the rule, since the marching chapter requires needles, thread, safety pins, and spare buttons in the personal kit.

**4.3. Wearing the uniform.** The uniform is worn as prescribed: complete, clean, and correctly adjusted. The soldier in uniform represents the Army to every civilian who sees him. → `references/drill-arms-equipment.md`

## 5. Arms and Equipment (Chapter 6, ¶¶60–85)

**5.1. Responsibility for care.** The soldier is personally responsible for his arms and equipment. Neglect — rust, dirt, a lost part — is a disciplinary matter as well as a practical failure: a rifle that will not fire when needed is a casualty.

**5.2. The U.S. rifle, caliber .30, M1 (¶63).** The manual gives the nomenclature of the M1 Garand and its care and cleaning: disassembly as authorized, cleaning the bore and chamber, oiling lightly, and keeping the weapon free of dirt and moisture. The M1 was the standard U.S. service rifle of WWII; the manual's cleaning discipline — clean after firing, clean after exposure to rain or dust, inspect before use — is the durable lesson, not the specific disassembly steps, which belong to the weapon's own manual.

**5.3. The automatic pistol, caliber .45, M1911 (¶64).** Nomenclature, care and cleaning, and safety devices of the M1911 pistol, the Army's sidearm from 1911 into the 1980s.

**5.4. Safety precautions (¶65).** Treat every weapon as loaded; never point it at anything you do not intend to shoot; keep the finger off the trigger until ready to fire; know what is beyond the target. These rules are stated in 1940 language but are the same rules taught today.

**5.5. The gas mask (¶¶66–72).** Fitting, wearing, care, and drill with the service gas mask — a central concern in 1940, when gas warfare was a live expectation. The manual covers adjustment, the drill for putting it on quickly, and care of the filter and facepiece.

**5.6. Field equipment and the infantry pack (¶¶73–84).** The pack, haversack, canteen, mess kit, entrenching tool, shelter half, and other field equipment; how the infantry pack is made up and carried; packing individual equipment on horseback for mounted units; and the formal display (layout) of equipment for inspection. → `references/drill-arms-equipment.md`

## 6. Drill — School of the Soldier, Squad, and Platoon (Chapters 7–9, ¶¶86–184)

**6.1. Without arms (Ch. 7).** Positions (attention, rest, parade rest), facings, steps and marchings — the close-order drill that teaches the soldier to move as part of a body and to respond instantly to commands.

**6.2. With arms (Ch. 8).** The manual of arms for the rifle (order arms, present arms, port arms, sling arms, and the rest), loadings and firings, carrying the automatic rifle, and the manual of the pistol.

**6.3. Squad and platoon drill (Ch. 9).** The squad (¶¶152–168) and the platoon (¶¶169–184): formations, movements, and the commands that move them. The point of drill, the manual implies throughout, is not parade — it is that a unit which can move together on the drill field can move together under fire. → `references/drill-arms-equipment.md`

## 7. Interior Guard Duty (Chapter 10, ¶¶185–193)

Guard duty is the soldier's trust: the guard protects the post, its property, and its people while others sleep. The chapter covers the orders a guard must know — general orders and special orders — the challenge and countersign, the duties of sentinels on post, relief procedures, and the seriousness of sleeping on post or leaving it. A guard who does not know his orders is not a guard. → `references/fieldcraft-marches-camps.md`

## 8. Marches, Camps, and Bivouacs (Chapter 11, ¶¶194–202)

**8.1. Marching is the daily job (¶194).** "Battles take place at indefinite intervals, but marches occur daily. To win battles troops must arrive on the battlefield on time and in good physical condition."

**8.2. Preparations (¶195).** The evening before a march: canteen clean and filled with fresh water; personal equipment checked, including towel, soap, toothbrush, pocket comb, small mirror, needles, thread, safety pins, and spare buttons; pack straps and belt adjusted — "a poorly adjusted pack adds to the discomfort and fatigue of a march"; at least two pairs of woolen socks without holes or mends; shoes or boots comfortable, in good repair, and well broken in — "never start a march with a new pair of shoes or boots." Before dark, dispose of trash around the tent area; the Army "takes pride in always leaving a camp site in better condition than we found it." On the morning of the march: turn out promptly at reveille, make up the roll and pack, eat breakfast, wash and pack the mess kit, and fall in fully equipped.

**8.3. Conduct of individuals (¶197).** Drinking water on a march is largely a matter of habit: drink plentifully before the start, then sparingly — the manual says drink none at all for the first 3 or 4 hours, then only a few mouthfuls at the end of a rest period. (Modern hydration doctrine differs sharply — drink before, during, and after, in small quantities, and do not wait for thirst; see FM 21-18 Foot Marches, already a skill in this vault. Follow the modern rule.) A small pebble carried in the mouth keeps it moist and reduces thirst. Do not drink or eat unwholesome food or drink; use only water approved by the commander. Sweets increase thirst and should be avoided. Excessive perspiration loses the body salts needed to prevent fatigue and heat exhaustion; eating common table salt or salt tablets helps, as does cold coffee or tea. Do not use profane or obscene language or make remarks to civilians on the march. Do not enter private property without permission or take fruit or vegetables from orchards and gardens — "serious military offenses."

**8.4. Halts (¶197c–d).** A halt of 15 minutes is usually made at the end of the first 45 minutes of marching; after the first halt, foot or mounted columns usually halt 10 minutes each hour — to rest, relieve themselves, adjust equipment, and inspect animals, motors, vehicles, and loading. Attend to these promptly; do not wait until the command to march again. A mounted man attends to his horse before himself, inspecting each foot for loose shoes and stones. During the halt, rest as much as possible: if the ground is dry, remove the pack and stretch out at full length; next best, sit with a good back rest against a tree, fence, or embankment. Never sit or lie on wet ground. To answer the calls of nature, dig a small pit and immediately refill it after use.

**8.5. Road discipline (¶198).** Troops usually keep to the right of the road, leaving the left free for other traffic — or the reverse if the commander so directs for concealment or other reasons; sometimes both sides, leaving the middle clear. Stay at all times on the side directed; do not straggle back and forth across the road. Motor units may move as rapidly as 60 miles an hour past the column, so keeping place and distance is a safety matter, not a formality. At halts, immediately clear the road by moving off to the side marched on.

**8.6. Camps and bivouacs (¶¶199–202).** The ideal camp site has plenty of water, wood, and level, dry, well-drained ground, with concealment and good sanitation. Personal care and comfort: the shelter tent, the bed (level the ground, scrape a hollow for the hips, use straw, dry grass, leaves, or small branches, sleep on the raincoat to keep dampness off), and the rules of camp sanitation — ditch the tent as soon as it is put up, even for one night; air blankets and clothing in the sun daily; keep the tent ventilated at night. Mounted organizations have their own camp routines for horses, picket lines, and forage. → `references/fieldcraft-marches-camps.md`

## 9. Compasses and Maps (Chapter 12, ¶¶203–216)

**9.1. The prismatic compass (¶¶203–207).** The soldier must be thoroughly familiar with the compass and know how to use it by day and by night. The prismatic compass — the type most generally used — indicates magnetic north by a magnetized dial-needle graduated into 360 degrees, 0 at north, 90 east, 180 south, 270 west, reading clockwise. The magnetic azimuth of an object is the compass reading, in degrees, of a line from the center of the compass toward the object. To take an azimuth by day: raise the eyepiece and cover, release the dial, hold the compass as shown in figure 56, turn slowly until the object is lined up by the slit on the eyepiece and the hair-line on the glass cover, let the dial come to rest, and read the azimuth through the eyepiece. At night: set the radiolite marker on the movable index ring to the required azimuth (e.g., 55°, halfway between 50 and 60), clamp the ring, hold the compass horizontally, and turn until the dial-needle points to the marker — the course is then indicated by the radiolite markers. On a dark night, send a comrade forward to the limit of visibility and direct him right or left by compass until he is on the azimuth, then move up abreast and repeat.

**9.2. Map reading (¶¶208–216).** A map represents part of the earth's surface on paper, drawn to scale: a given distance on the map always represents a given distance on the ground (e.g., 1 inch = 1 mile). Scale is shown by a divided bar line, by words or figures (3 inches = 1 mile), or by representative fraction (RF, e.g., 1/63,360 for 1 inch to 1 mile). The soldier learns orientation — orienting the map so its north matches ground north, and orienting himself by knowing his own position — and the conventional signs of FM 21-30, which the handbook cross-references. He learns to read relief and to put positions on a map. → `references/fieldcraft-marches-camps.md`

## 10. Security and Protection (Chapter 13, ¶¶217–236)

**10.1. Security of individuals (¶¶221–228).** Security in the Army is the same idea as police and fire protection in civil life, applied to the enemy. The individual soldier's duties: conduct on the march and in bivouac, sentinels (a sentinel may be posted to give warning of the enemy's approach), messengers, the connecting file that keeps touch between units, antiaircraft security and protection (dispersion, concealment, and what to do when aircraft appear), antitank security and protection (warning and the use of cover), and protection against gas (mask drill, warning, and decontamination as then taught).

**10.2. Security of small units (¶¶229–236).** Scouts — the smallest security detachment; patrols (the squad or part of a squad sent out to gain information or to protect); security at halt, on the march (advance guard, flank and rear guards), while defending, and while attacking. The pattern is constant: somebody must always be watching in every direction the enemy could come from, and somebody must always be ready to give the alarm. → `references/fieldcraft-marches-camps.md`

## 11. Military Sanitation and First Aid (Chapter 14, ¶¶237–275)

**11.1. Sanitation (¶¶237–252).** Keep well and you keep the unit well. Report to a medical officer at once if you do not feel well — never try to treat yourself for illness; the danger of giving disease to another man is greatest when the illness is just starting. Bathe at least twice a week (armpits, crotch, feet); wash hands before eating and after using the toilet; change underwear, shirts, and socks at least twice a week; if water is unavailable, shake clothing out and hang it in sunlight at least 2 hours, and watch for lice. Change wet clothing and shoes as soon as possible. Brush teeth at least twice a day. Use the latrine, never the ground — flies carry germs from exposed waste to food and water. Drink plenty of water at intervals but not a large amount at one time, especially when overheated; drink from your own cup, never a shared one. Use the mosquito bar when mosquitoes are present, tucked in and without holes. Kill flies, keep screens closed, keep food containers and garbage cans tightly closed. Keep barracks clean; report bedbugs. In the field: never drink stream, spring, well, or faucet water until a medical officer has passed it as pure and a sign is posted; when orders say boil, boil for 20 minutes, not merely heat; drink only from the Lyster sterilizing bag when one is provided, letting water run from the faucet into your own cup — never dip a cup in the bag or put lips to the faucet. Wash mess kit, knife, fork, and spoon in hot soapy water and rinse in boiling water. Do not sit or lie directly on damp ground; do not stand in a draft while hot or wet. Air blankets and clothing in the sun daily; ditch the tent as soon as it is put up. And the sentence the manual itself highlights: "The most important thing in your marching ability as a soldier is the care of your feet" (¶252; see §8 and FM 21-18).

**11.2. First aid (¶¶253–275).** The governing rules: do not get excited — act quickly but quietly; be gentle; do not try to do too much; call a Medical Department man as soon as possible; make the injured man sit or lie down; never move an injured man until medical help comes unless he cannot be kept warm where he is or must be moved to reach help; warmth is most important to prevent chilling and shock, even on a warm day (hot-water canteens under the armpits and between the legs, outside the clothing; blankets, coats, newspapers); if he is conscious and not wounded in stomach, throat, or mouth, he may have a hot drink; never pour liquids into the mouth of an unconscious man; do not try to bring an unconscious wounded man to consciousness; never touch a wound with anything unclean and do not wash the wound — you may cause blood poisoning. The first-aid packet (¶¶254–255) is an airtight container in a web pouch on the belt, ring down so it cannot open accidentally; it holds two sterile dressings (gauze bandage 4 inches wide, 84 inches long, with a compress sewn in the middle) and two safety pins, and is never opened until needed. The chapter then covers hemorrhage and control of bleeding (¶¶256–257), the tourniquet (¶258), fractures (¶¶259–260), poisoned wounds including snakebite (¶261), fainting (¶262), burns (¶263), sunstroke (¶264), heat exhaustion (¶265), freezing (¶266), abdominal pain (¶267), athlete's foot (¶268), gases used in warfare and peacetime gases (¶¶269–270), artificial respiration (¶271), drowning — under water over 5 minutes (¶272), electrical shock (¶273), and litters and other means of transporting the injured (¶¶274–275).

**Historic caution.** Several procedures in this chapter are obsolete: the tourniquet guidance, snakebite treatment, and burn care of 1940 do not match current Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC — already a skill in this vault). Use this chapter to understand what a WWII soldier was taught, and use TCCC for what to do today. → `references/sanitation-first-aid.md`

## 12. The Ration, Pay, and the Will (Chapters 15–17, ¶¶276–295)

**12.1. The ration (¶¶276–282).** A ration is the allowance of food for one man for one day. The manual describes the kinds of ration and how the mess sergeant and cooks feed the company in garrison and on campaign, and how the soldier should behave at mess and in the field — including fire for cooking: the best fire for cooking is a small, hot fire, built and tended so it cooks without smoke and waste. The government, the manual notes, spends more money on the soldier's food than on any comparable civilian feeding — and expects him not to waste it.

**12.2. Pay and allowances (¶¶283–294).** Rate of pay on entering service, specialist and Air Corps additions, pay for decorations, furlough allowance, deposits, allotments of pay, government (National Service Life) insurance, deductions, travel pay on honorable discharge, and burial expenses. All figures are 1940 rates and are of historical interest only.

**12.3. Last will and testament (¶295).** Every soldier should have his will drawn up, witnessed, and kept where it can be found — a duty the manual places alongside pay and insurance, not after them.

## Operating Rules

1. **Feet first.** The care of your feet is the most important thing in your marching ability — two pairs of good woolen socks, boots broken in, never new boots on a march.
2. **Sanitation is a weapon.** Wash hands, use the latrine, drink only approved water, air your bedding — disease has killed more soldiers than bullets.
3. **Know your job and the next one up.** Be ready to step into the job of the man ahead of you.
4. **Do not try to do too much.** In first aid: keep the casualty warm, stop the bleeding, do not move him, and get a medic.
5. **Historic, not current.** 1940 weapons, pay, law, and some first aid are superseded; the soldier skills are not.
