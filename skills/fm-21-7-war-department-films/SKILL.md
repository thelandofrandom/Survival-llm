# FM 21-7 List of War Department Films, Film Strips, and Film Bulletins

## Purpose

FM 21-7, *List of War Department Films, Film Strips, and Recognition Film Slides* (War Department, 25 January 1945, 333 pages) — the master catalogue of the U.S. Army's projected training aids as of 1 December 1944. It supersedes FM 21-7 of 1 January 1944, including Changes 1–8 (1 March through 12 December 1944). It is one of a series of manuals on instructional materials: FM 21-5 and TM 21-250 summarize the principles of military training and instruction; FM 21-6 lists War Department training publications; FM 21-8 covers other training aids (models, charts, graphic portfolios, special training devices). FM 21-7's purpose is to provide a complete list of War Department films, film strips, and recognition film slides available as of 1 December 1944, and to tell how these aids may be obtained and used effectively in military training. Army Air Forces activities are referred to in paragraph 86 (the manual's AAF paragraph).

**What this is — and is not.** This is a catalogue and an instructor's guide, not a procedures manual. It contains no training doctrine of its own beyond how to *use* films well; the films themselves are the training. The bulk of the book — some 300 pages — is lists: serial number, year released, title, and running time for each item. This skill is therefore a guide to the catalogue: what it covers, how it is organized, how to find material in it, and what representative titles exist in each category. It does not summarize the content of individual films beyond what the catalogue itself states (title, and where the catalogue gives a synopsis or subject note, that note). Do not invent film content from a title alone — a title like "Crack That Tank" tells you the subject, not the lesson. For the content of any film, consult the film itself or its Film Reference (see §2.5); for the catalogue entry, consult `references/full-text.md`.

**Historic — read this first.** Every item in this catalogue is a 1940–44 War Department production on 16-mm or 35-mm film, film strip, or 2×2-inch slide, distributed through a wartime film-library system that no longer exists. The films are historical artifacts: the "Why We Fight" series, the "Fighting Men" series, combat bulletins from the Italian and Pacific theaters, and training films on weapons, vehicles, and procedures of the time. Some of their doctrine is superseded, some of their language and attitudes (including the segregated-era titles) are of their time, and the distribution system (Signal Corps Photographic Center, service command film libraries, film circuits) is gone. The catalogue's durable value is as a finding aid for WWII training-film research and as a record of what the Army taught, and how, in 1944 — and its guidance on using visual aids in instruction (preview, introduce, show, follow up) is still sound teaching practice.

**A note on the extracted text.** The lists are dense two- and three-column tables. Plain-text extraction preserves the serial numbers, titles, years, and running times, but column alignment is lost and some running times and characters are garbled (e.g., "9" for a 9-minute runtime rendered as "g", "13" as "j3"). Where a running time looks garbled in the full text, this skill omits it rather than guessing. Titles are quoted as extracted; a few carry OCR misreadings of the original print.

## 1. What the Catalogue Lists — Definitions (Section I, ¶¶1–4)

**1.1. Film (¶2a).** "Film" is the general term for all motion pictures produced for or by the War Department, in five classes:

- **Training Films (TF):** deal with approved War Department doctrine; cover specific topics; intended for use at a definite time and place in the training program. Nearly all are available in both 16-mm and 35-mm sizes.
- **Film Bulletins (FB):** deal with new military developments, not necessarily based on approved doctrine; produced for the information of officers and enlisted men. Both 16-mm and 35-mm.
- **Combat Bulletins (CB):** selections of the latest and most significant combat scenes from all battle fronts, with descriptive commentary; designed to give the soldier a picture of what is happening in all theaters of war from week to week.
- **Miscellaneous Films (Misc.):** special productions fitting no other category, including films adopted from other services whose doctrine does not completely conform to approved War Department training doctrine.
- **War Information Films (OF):** designed to acquaint all military personnel with the background of the present war, the history of the war to date, its current progress, and information concerning allies and enemies.

**1.2. Film Strips (¶2b).** Series of still pictures printed on strips of 35-mm film, for use with lectures, demonstrations, or other training procedures. Like training films, they deal with specific topics and are intended for a definite time and place in the training program. Some are produced with accompanying sound recordings. They are numbered FS (Army Air Forces) and SFS (the general series).

**1.3. Recognition Film Slides (¶2c).** Sets of 2×2-inch film slides for recognition courses in aircraft, armored vehicles, and naval vessels — silhouettes and views from various angles.

**1.4. Film Library and Distribution Division (¶¶2d–e).** A film library is a depository for projection equipment and films, providing loan service to troop units. The Distribution Division is a division of the Signal Corps Photographic Center, Long Island City, New York, which supplies films, film strips, slides, and related film literature to service commands, defense commands, and theaters of operation, and supervises film libraries and oversea film and equipment exchanges.

**1.5. Purpose and advantages of visual aids (¶¶3–4).** Films and film strips present military subjects vividly, interestingly, and accurately. They supplement but do not supplant the instructor: by themselves films have only limited value, but in the hands of a resourceful instructor following sound principles of military instruction they are invaluable. Improper use endangers a training program — soldiers marched into a hot classroom and forced to sit through a series of unrelated films for a prolonged period, with no indication of what to look for or how the films relate to their duties, will learn little; and material is not learned well unless the showing is followed by examinations, discussions, or other applicatory exercises. The advantages the manual claims: instruction is standardized; films emphasize fundamentals; demonstrations of tactical exercises or equipment are brought to the troops instead of moving troops to demonstration areas; the same demonstration can be repeated without repeating the field labor; every man in a group can see and hear all phases of an action, with enlarged close-ups of minute details; the most highly trained troops and expert instructors demonstrate; and animation, slow motion, time lapse, miniature, and micro-photography show normally hidden action. → `references/using-training-films.md`

## 2. How to Use Training Films (Section II, ¶¶5–7)

**2.1. The five-step procedure (¶5).** (a) **Integrate the film in the training program.** A training film orients the soldier and introduces new duties, operations, or procedures; develops proper attitudes, builds morale, or stimulates interest; and in many cases instructs in the specific details of a subject — but a soldier seldom becomes proficient merely by looking at a film; he learns a job by doing it, and mastery of details follows swiftly once he has seen how the procedure should be carried out. (b) **Preview the film.** The instructor cannot use a film effectively without carefully previewing and studying it in advance. No training film is perfect: select the key points, the items to omit or touch lightly, the portions that are obsolete or need explanation; plan the introductory and follow-up activities; decide in advance whether to break the showing into parts. (c) **Introduce the film.** Tell the soldiers what it is about, why it is shown, its battle importance, the key points to observe, and its relation to their earlier training and duties — or raise questions and set problems the film will solve, or give a demonstration. This is the difference between merely showing training films and using them effectively. (d) **Show the film.** The instructor remains with the group during screening, ready to adjust for unforeseen difficulties. Do not show films for too long; break a long film into parts. Variations to maintain interest are allowed — for example, turning off the sound at a prearranged point and giving his own oral commentary, for an obsolete portion or other local adaptation — but such commentary must conform to approved War Department doctrine. (e) **Follow-up activities.** Apply the lesson immediately in practice where possible (a gas-mask film followed at once by gas-mask drill; a map-reading film followed by map reading). Where immediate practice is impractical, hold an oral discussion immediately after, or use oral or written quizzes built around the key points — not nonessentials — or "Quiz Cards" for yes/no questions. Demonstrations also work: a camouflage film followed by exhibits of good and bad camouflage. **2.2. Film References (¶5f).** A Film Reference is published for training films, and for certain film bulletins, released or revised after 1 July 1944. It includes, besides a synopsis of the film, a statement of its use and place in the training program, suggested introductory remarks, suggested follow-up activities, a list of correlated training materials, a suggested quiz, and special notes — and thus aids the instructor in using the film effectively. Film References replace the Film Digests formerly published by Army Pictorial Service; they are published and distributed by The Adjutant General to all film libraries for redistribution to holders of Film Digest Catalogs, supplemented by standard distribution to units, schools, and training centers.

**2.3. The "Fighting Men" series (¶5g).** In addition to films integrated in the training program, the following films of the "Fighting Men" series should be seen by every combat soldier — commanders are responsible that they are not shown too early in the training cycle: TF 21-1007 Snafu; 21-1018 Keep It Clean; 21-1019 Crack That Tank; 21-1020 How To Get Killed In One Easy Lesson; 21-1021 Wise Guy; 21-1024 Kill or Be Killed; 21-1026 On Your Toes; 21-1027 Latrinograms; 21-1028 Heroes; 21-1029 On Your Own; 21-1375 Time Out; 21-2014 Baptism of Fire; 21-2015 Secret Weapon; 21-2056 By Your Command.

**2.4. Films required for all military personnel (¶6).** War Department directives require certain films to be shown to all military personnel. Special required films include TF 30-1315 Postal Censorship (War Department letter AG 000.73, 23 December 1943). War Information Films required for all include the "Why We Fight" series — OF 1 Prelude to War, OF 2 The Nazis Strike, OF 3 Divide and Conquer, OF 4 The Battle of Britain, OF 5 The Battle of Russia — plus OF 16 Know Your Ally Britain and OF (RF) 51 The Negro Soldier (War Department Circulars 368 of 9 November 1942, 247 of 9 October 1943, and 208 of 25 May 1944).

**2.5. Use of film strips (¶7).** Careful previewing and study are essential; Illustrated Instructor's References are issued for some film strips (marked "Reference" in the numerical listing, ¶26). Some film strips develop a subject continuously like a training film — build the lesson around them the same way. Others are like a series of charts — use the strip, or only selected frames, to implement a specific phase of a lesson. Success depends on advance planning, the manner of presentation, the instructor's commentary, and application to practical situations; film strips work most effectively with small groups. Projection is simple: with a shadow box or similar arrangement, strips can be shown in partially darkened rooms or outdoors where current is available (see TM 21-250). → `references/using-training-films.md`

## 3. Distribution — How Films Reached the Troops (Section III, ¶¶8–15)

**3.1. General (¶8).** Films, film strips, slides, and projection equipment are distributed by the Chief Signal Officer within the continental United States to Army Ground Forces and Army Service Forces units (and AAF units at GF/SF stations) through a film-library system operated by the service commands. Under War Department Memorandum 850-44 (12 August 1944), commanders of U.S. Army Forces in all oversea theaters, departments, bases, and defense commands are authorized to establish film and equipment exchanges serving the same function. AAF production and distribution is a function of the Commanding General, Army Air Forces (AAF Regulations 50-19, 65-1, 95-5); AAF activities follow AAF procedures and approved lists.

**3.2. Libraries and exchanges (¶9).** A **central library** (or central film exchange overseas) is maintained at each service command headquarters, stocked with all films approved for showing to troops, lending on a temporary basis to all organizations in the command; loan requests go to the commanding general, stating number, title, and size (16-mm or 35-mm). **Sublibraries/subexchanges** at points of major troop concentration stock films of relatively constant need; occasional-demand films come from the central library on loan. **Auxiliary libraries** serve schools and units in specialized training with a recurring need for a selected group of films. **Reception center libraries** stock a basic-training set in recommended order: TF 21-2067 Introduction to the Army; 8-1238 Sex Hygiene (or 8-2060 Pick Up in lieu); 8-155 Personal Hygiene; 21-2048 Military Courtesy; 11-235 Articles of War; 19-2034 A.W.O.L. and Desertion; 8-2047 First Aid for Battle Injuries; 8-2049 First Aid for Non-Battle Injuries; 7-248 Instruction of the Soldier, Dismounted, Without Arms, Position and Facings; 30-2033 Sucker Bait.

**3.3. Circuits, supply, and stock control (¶¶10–13).** Distribution also runs through **film circuits**: activities in a given area are booked on the same circuit, a route sheet assigns play dates, a copy is pasted in the film can, and each installation forwards the print on the shipping date to the next location; posters and booking notices go out about 2 weeks ahead. Initial distribution of films such as the "Why We Fight" series, G.I. Movie Weekly, "Brief for Invasion," and "War Speeds Up" runs through circuits. The Signal Corps Photographic Center supplies only films approved by the commanding generals of AGF and ASF; it cannot procure commercial films unless first approved for training — the current approved list is this manual. For initial distribution, a summary of each training film goes to each central and sublibrary about 40 days before release; the officer in charge, with training officers, appraises it and requests prints from the central library, which consolidates the service command's order to the Signal Corps Photographic Center, 35-11 35th Avenue, Long Island City, New York. Combat Bulletins, certain Film Bulletins, and miscellaneous releases cannot be pre-notified (content is unknown until approval), so initial distribution is set at the Photographic Center. Reorders go from sublibrary to central library; stock and use reports go periodically to the central library, and inventory is kept on Monthly Film Library Report SAU-12. Film strips follow the same supply pattern (advance notification about 20 days before release; projectors requisitioned at one per battalion or higher headquarters; units may draw strips and retain them as long as needed). Obsolete prints are withdrawn at once, unwound from metal reels, and disposed of per paragraph 32b, AR 380-5 (15 March 1944); empty reels and cans return to the central library. Minor breaks and tears are spliced in the sublibrary; severely damaged prints are exchanged at the central library. **3.4. Recognition slides and projection equipment (¶¶14–15).** Recognition slides are distributed by the Signal Corps Photographic Center to central film libraries, which reissue sets to sublibraries on the basis of issue in ¶26; oversea distribution only on request of base defense or theater commanders. Projection equipment is distributed by requisition through Signal channels on the Chief Signal Officer (ASF Circular 258, 11 Aug 44; T/A 20-2 for domestic, T/O&E 11-500 for oversea). → `references/distribution-and-film-strips.md`

## 4. The Lists — How the Catalogue Is Organized (Section IV, ¶¶16–27)

The lists give serial number, year released, title, and running time in minutes. An asterisk (*) marks films authorized for ASF and AGF training and for permanent retention by film libraries.

**4.1. Training Films, ¶16 (TF 1- through TF 44- and others).** The largest list, ordered by the branch prefix number: 1- Air Corps/Air Forces subjects (aircraft engines, aerodynamics, celestial navigation, aerial bombs, propellers, hydraulics, machine guns, aircraft identification — e.g., TF 1-294 Messerschmitt 109; TF 1-438 Spitfire); 7- infantry and dismounted drill; 8- medical and hygiene; 11- signal and communications; 17- armored force; 19- military police and personnel; 21- general military training (the "Fighting Men" series and basic subjects); 30- military intelligence; and other branch prefixes through the 40s. Representative early entries: TF 1-133 Modern Weather Theory — Primary Circulation (1940, 19 min); 1-136 Aircraft Engines — Elements of Electricity as Applied to Ignition Systems (1941, 28 min); 1-222 Small Arms Ammunition — .30 Cal., .50 Cal., and .45 Cal. (1942, 9 min); 1-326 Aerial Navigation — Dead Reckoning (1942, 26 min). The TF list runs from page 13 to page 44 of the manual. **4.2. Foreign versions, ¶17.** Films rescored in Italian for use at Italian Service Units (IT numbers), e.g., IT 165 Principles of Oxy-acetylene Welding and Cutting; IT 200–203 Pioneer Equipment (wood cutting tools, manila rope, hitches, knots and bends); IT 235 Articles of War; IT 551–559 The Motor Vehicle Driver series; IT 1238 Sex Hygiene; IT 2048 Military Courtesy.

**4.3. Film Bulletins, ¶18 (FB).** New developments, not necessarily approved doctrine — e.g., FB 1 The 90-mm Antiaircraft Gun; The 37-mm Remote Control Antiaircraft Gun; The 16-inch Howitzer (Firing Tests); FB 2 A New Type Trench Digger; Experimental Type Flame Thrower; Rubber Boats; FB 3 Experimental Carriers for 37-mm Gun; The 75-mm New Gun Half-Track Carrier; Experimental Incendiary Bomb. Multiple short items are commonly grouped under one FB number.

**4.4. Miscellaneous Films, ¶19 (Misc.).** Special productions fitting no other category, including adopted films from other services.

**4.5. Military Intelligence Service Films (Japanese language), ¶20.** Special-distribution Japanese-language films: Misc. 526 Vow in the Desert; 585 Prayer at Dawn; 1013 Atarashi Kazoku; 1043 Earth and Soldier; 1051 Mother (all 1944).

**4.6. Combat Bulletins, ¶21 (CB 1–27+).** Numbered combat newsreels with contents described in the catalogue — e.g., CB 1 Combat Shots, Pacific and Italian Theaters (1944, 13 min); CB 2 Bridging the Volturno River, Italy; Landings in the South Pacific; Invasion of Cape Gloucester (14 min); CB 4 The Battle for Cassino; Japs and Jungle; Tanks and Infantry Face Enemy Fire (19 min); CB 13 Capture of Saipan; Seized Luftwaffe Films (17 min); CB 14 Allied Drive Breaks Nazi Normandy Defenses; Capture of Leghorn (16 min); CB 27 Clean-up on Peleliu and Angaur; Landings on Leyte (28 min). CB 13 onward were also distributed as SFR (Special Film Release) for domestic distribution. **4.7. Combat Reports, ¶22 (CR).** CR 1 Liberation of Rome (1944, 21 min) — further distribution requires special authorization.

**4.8. Information and Education Division Films, ¶23.** (a) War Information Films: the "Why We Fight," "Know Your Enemy," and "Know Your Ally" series, circulated for exhibition at post theaters during daytime hours — not as part of evening entertainment programs — in 16-mm and 35-mm, available later through service command film libraries. (b) Other I&E subjects listed by issue number, year, and running time.

**4.9. Bureau of Public Relations Films, ¶24.** Films produced for public relations purposes, listed by number and title.

**4.10. Film Strips, ¶25 (FS and SFS).** AAF film strips (FS 1-4 Machine Tools — The Lathe; FS 1-5 Milling Machine; FS 1-6 Planers; FS 1-7 Shaper; FS 1-8 Aerodynamics; FS 1-9 Classification of Engine Types; FS 1-11 Forced Landings; FS 1-12 Using an Aircraft Machine Gun; FS 1-14 Browning Aircraft Machine Gun, .30 Caliber M2) and the general SFS series covering the full range of training subjects — the catalogue's film-strip list runs from page 62 to page 210. Each entry gives the strip number, year, and a subject note describing what it covers.

**4.11. Recognition Film Slides, ¶26.** 2×2-inch slides in sets with stated bases of issue: Set No. 1 (U.S. Army aircraft only) — one set per 3,000 men other than antiaircraft artillery; Set No. 2 (U.S. Army, U.S. Navy, British, Russian, German, Italian, Japanese aircraft) — one set per antiaircraft artillery battalion; Set No. 3 (British RAF, German, Italian aircraft) — staging areas shipping to the European and North African theaters, one per 3,000 men other than AAA; Set No. 4 (U.S. Navy and Japanese aircraft) — staging areas shipping to Alaska, Hawaii, and the Southwest Pacific, one per 3,000 men other than AAA; armored vehicle sets No. 1 (U.S.) and No. 2 (British, Russian, German, Italian, Japanese); naval vessel Set No. 1 for coast artillery harbor defense units (two sets to the Coast Artillery School; five to the Coast Artillery Replacement Training Center). Slides on each aircraft include silhouettes and flight views from various angles; armored vehicle slides list vehicle numbers (e.g., T303 Light Tank M5/M5A1 "Gen. Stuart"; T307 Medium Tank M3; T313 Medium Tank M4; T320 105-mm Howitzer Motor Carriage M8; T321 3-in. Gun Motor Carriage M10) with view numbers for each set. **4.12. Phonograph Records, ¶27.** A set of three double-faced 12-inch 78 rpm records with six 5-minute dramatized incidents of Radio Transmission Security Violations: (1) Ex-Sergeant; (2) Hawaiian Islands; (3) West Coast Air Base; (4) Bombing Mission; (5) South Sea Island; (6) Secret Flight. Played one incident every day or two during code practice or radio procedure periods — not all six at once — on any ordinary phonograph, including over headphone circuits of code practice tables. Distributed to interested units on request.

**4.13. Subject Index.** The manual closes with a subject index of films and film strips (e.g., "Abandon ship, procedures, precautions, and safety measures — TF 21-1244"; "Aerial bombs (see Bombs, Aerial)"; "Aerodynamics of gliders — FS 1-91") — the fastest way into the catalogue when you know the subject but not the serial number. → `references/film-lists-by-category.md`

## Output Contract

When asked to find WWII training material on a subject: (1) check the subject index first, then the branch-prefix TF list, then the SFS film-strip list; (2) report the serial number, title, year, and running time exactly as the catalogue lists them — and say when a running time is garbled in the extraction rather than guessing it; (3) state what the catalogue does and does not say about content — a title and a subject note are not a synopsis; (4) name the distribution facts (16-mm/35-mm, library loan, circuit) only when the question is about how the film reached troops. Never invent a film's lesson from its title.

## Operating Rules

1. **The catalogue is the source, not the films.** It lists titles and times; it does not teach the subjects.
2. **Use the index.** Subject first, serial number second — the TF numbers are branch-prefixed, not topical.
3. **Preview before you teach.** The manual's own rule for instructors applies to researchers too: look at the thing before describing it.
4. **Historic system, historic films.** The library/circuit distribution is gone; the films are 1944 artifacts.
5. **Quote titles exactly; flag garbles.** Running times and characters damaged in extraction are reported as damaged, not repaired by guesswork.
