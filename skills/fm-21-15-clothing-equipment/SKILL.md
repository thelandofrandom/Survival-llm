# FM 21-15 Individual Clothing and Equipment

## Purpose

FM 21-15, *Individual Clothing and Equipment* (War Department Field Manual, April 1945, 93 pages) — the WWII Army manual for the individual soldier's kit: the care of clothing and individual equipment, bivouac equipment, the method of assembling and packing equipment, and the display of basic equipment for formal inspection. Together with FM 20-15 of 24 February 1945, it supersedes FM 21-15, *Equipment, Clothing, and Tent Pitching*, of 1 October 1940 and its six changes and TB QM 20 of 18 July 1944.

> **HISTORIC WWII-ERA DOCTRINE — READ THIS FIRST.** This is a 1945 manual. Its equipment is the WWII individual kit — the M-1928 haversack and pack carrier, the M-1936 canvas field bag, the rucksack, the jungle pack, the field pack, and the cargo and combat field pack; the M-1943 field jacket; the shelter half and the woolen sleeping bag — and its materials, treatments (dubbing, neat's-foot oil, saddle soap), and inspection customs are of its era. Its durable value is in principles and fieldcraft: why kit care is a combat matter, how to keep clothing, leather, webbing, and metal serviceable in the field, how to pack and carry a load, and how to pitch and live in a shelter tent. Where a current manual governs, it governs: current individual equipment, packing, and field-craft guidance lives in modern references such as **ATP 3-21.18, *Foot Marches***, **TC 21-3** (cold weather), and the current **FM 21-76 / ATP 3-50.21** survival series — use those as the authority and FM 21-15 as the historical field reference.

## 1. Responsibility — Kit Care Is a Combat Matter

**1.1. The issue.** On enlistment or induction, enlisted personnel receive a prescribed initial issue of clothing and equipment; other items are subsequently issued according to mounted or dismounted status, occupation, arm or service, and duty assignment. Specialized items have been designed to meet combat needs and climatic conditions (see T/E 21 and AR 615-40).

**1.2. The soldier owns the condition of his kit.** Soldiers are responsible for the clothing and individual equipment issued to them, including proper care and maintenance. Articles that become excess because of changed status must be turned in. Responsibility is fixed so that economy may be promoted: if, through carelessness, officers and enlisted personnel lose or damage issued clothing and equipment, the cost of replacing or repairing the articles is charged to them. In the combat zone the availability of supplies is of primary importance to every fighting man; while accountability of property may be suspended temporarily during combat, responsibility for protection from loss or damage continues, except in cases known to be beyond the individual's control.

**1.3. Why it matters.** It is the duty of every soldier to conserve clothing and equipment so items will be available and in serviceable condition when needed. The manual states the stakes plainly: a field jacket, M-1943, without buttons and waist drawstring will give inadequate protection in changeable weather; a torn sleeping bag provides inadequate warmth on cold nights; a rifle that has been neglected may not fire. **Casualties have been increased because soldiers' essential equipment was not at hand and in good condition when it was needed.** → `references/care-of-clothing-and-equipment.md`

## 2. Care of Clothing

**2.1. Repair.** Each soldier must make such minor repairs as mending rips and tears and sewing on buttons. The commanding officer directs sending to quartermaster repair shops those items that cannot be repaired by individuals. **A damaged garment should be repaired immediately or immediately turned in, for delay may render it irreparable.**

**2.2. Mildew and moths.** Clothing should be thoroughly brushed and dried before it is hung in barracks or packed away. Leave air spaces between garments hung in barracks. At intervals take garments out and air them, preferably in the sunshine. If mildew forms, brush clothing thoroughly and hang it in the sun. Woolen garments should be dry-cleaned before they are stored.

**2.3. Cleaning.** Each soldier must keep his clothing clean. A uniform that gets dirty and stays dirty wears out quickly because the dirt cuts the fibers of the cloth and collects and holds moisture. It is impossible to keep the uniform from getting dirty, but it need not stay dirty: brush and clean it, or have it cleaned, regularly. Remove all spots immediately — the longer they remain, the harder they are to get out — and do not have uniforms pressed unless all spots have been removed. The manual's spot guide: meat juice or gravy — cold water, then cleaning fluid, with a pad underneath the fabric; do not rub woolens excessively, as rubbing damages the finish. Fresh fruit stains on cotton khakis — boiling or even warm water while the stains are still moist; cooked fruit or berry stains — stretch the stained portion over a bowl and pour boiling water on it so the water strikes the stain with force. Grease or oil spots — scrape off the top, then wash with mild soap and warm water.

**2.4. Pressing.** For any pressing job, moisture is always necessary. For woolens, use a damp cloth between the iron and the fabric; for cotton khakis, dampen the surface of the fabric and apply the iron directly. Do not get the iron too hot.

**2.5. Waterproof and water-repellent clothing.** After wear in inclement weather, dry thoroughly. To detect holes, hold waterproofed garments to the light and examine them; if defective, turn them in for repair. When water-repellent garments lose their water repellency, turn them in for treatment by a quartermaster laundry. For the raincoat and poncho: when packing, keep the folds smooth and even, and leave nothing in the pockets — articles left in pockets will tear and rub the garment in the pack and cause it to sag when hung. To prevent rust, hang the raincoat on a wooden or cardboard hanger, never a metal one. When soiled, wipe with a clean cloth and wash with soap and water. Do not leave raincoat or poncho exposed to the sun for long periods (heat dries them out and cracks them), near flames or artificial heat of any kind even for short periods, or contaminated with oil, grease, acid, or insect repellent.

**2.6. The helmet.** The helmet is a very important item of clothing: do not use it as a seat (the chin-strap loops may break off); do not use it as a shovel (the paint will be rubbed off); do not cook in it — it is made of steel specially treated for maximum protection against bullet penetration, and heating may weaken it. Do not let it get scratched: every helmet is demagnetized so the steel will not interfere with the use of a compass, and serious abrasion of the surface may affect the compass. Take care of the liner: if it is put in the barrack or duffel bag, protect it well with blankets or clothing and pack something soft inside the liner as well as around it to keep it from cracking or breaking; do not sit on the liner or use it for a pillow.

**2.7. Socks.** Change socks often, every day if possible, and wash them regularly, because perspiration rots the fibers. Socks must fit: if too large, they wrinkle inside the shoes, rub the feet, and cause blisters; if too small, they wear out at the toes. **Small socks are particularly bad in cold weather because they reduce circulation of blood and cause trench foot and frostbite.** → `references/care-of-clothing-and-equipment.md`

## 3. Care of Leather, Metal, and Other Materials

**3.1. Leather — general.** Leather is one of the most important materials in Army clothing and equipment, and in wartime there is usually a critical shortage of the first-grade material from which shoes, boots, and other leather articles must be made. The soldier should give his leather equipment such care as will prolong its usefulness. Bleaching agents, dyes, and other chemicals used to restore or alter the original color of leather are prohibited.

**3.2. Boots and shoes.** Shoes must be broken in before they are used on long marches; breaking-in is easier if dubbing is applied frequently, and during the first few days a new pair should be worn for short periods only. **If practicable, a pair of shoes should not be worn on two successive days.** Dubbing must be applied **once a week** to the service shoes and combat boots of all troops in temperate climates and **at least twice a week** in wet or extremely dry climates; dubbing makes shoes softer and more comfortable as well as water-resistant, and is more effective if frequently and liberally applied and well rubbed into the leather. Shoes need not be dried before application. Shoes to which dubbing has been applied cannot be polished — footwear should be inspected for dubbing rather than for high polish. Cleaning: scrape dirt or mud off with a dull instrument that will not cut the leather, then wash with soap and water. **Drying by direct exposure to hot sun, fire, or strong heat is prohibited** because it is injurious to the leather. Get repairs early — do not keep wearing shoes or boots until the damage becomes serious. Turn them in for repair at the first sign of: rips in upper or sole stitching; shanks damaged or badly worn; any part of the upper or sole pulled loose from the welt; cracked soles; the first evidence of a hole in the outer sole, or an outer sole too thin that will evidently soon be worn through; heels missing or worn down more than ¼ inch; or any other wear that interferes with comfort or economical repairability.

**3.3. Harness and saddlery.** Harness and saddlery must have daily care. Separate all parts, unbuckle straps, and remove detachable parts. With a damp (not wet) sponge, remove surface dust, mud, and foreign substances; do not immerse in water. Rinse the sponge and, using as little water as possible, make a heavy lather by rubbing the sponge vigorously on saddle soap; clean each piece thoroughly, drawing straps through the lathered sponge. After the leather is partly dry, rub it vigorously with a soft cloth. If oiling seems necessary, apply neat's-foot oil sparingly on the flesh or unfinished side, using light quick strokes for even distribution — **excessive oil is injurious to leather equipment**.

**3.4. Metal.** When not in use, bits, curb chains, belt buckles, and all other unpainted metal parts must be oiled lightly at intervals; when in use, they must be kept clean and free from rust. Removal of paint from metal parts is prohibited unless the responsible officer authorizes it for renovation. To restore dark finish on brass, dip the parts in a solution of ammonium hydroxide saturated with copper carbonate (saddle fittings may be painted with it by small brush, taking care it does not contact leather). Gold-finish insignia and buttons are cleaned with ammonia and water; abrasives are prohibited because they remove the plating. Rubber, manila paper, and sulfur-containing materials tarnish medals, metal insignia, and metal buttons.

**3.5. Horse covers, saddle blankets, cinchas, web, canvas, and wood.** Brush hair, manure, and foreign material off horse covers and saddle blankets and frequently expose the under side to the sun; the part of the folded saddle blanket next to the horse's back should be cleaned daily by drying in the sun, working caked areas between the hands, and finally brushing. A stiff brush or curry-comb is prohibited on saddle blankets — it wears off the nap. The manual also covers care of cinchas or girths, web equipment, canvas, wood, mess equipment, the canteen, knives, and miscellaneous equipment (paragraphs 7–14). → `references/care-of-clothing-and-equipment.md`

## 4. Bivouac Equipment

**4.1. What is issued.** Except in extremely cold or extremely warm, moist climates — where the two-man mountain tent and the jungle hammock (FM 20-15) are issued — men in the field use the **shelter tent**, composed of two shelter halves. Woolen sleeping bags (one per individual) may be issued in lieu of blankets in temperate-climate theaters. In mosquito-infested areas, the mosquito bar is issued when authorized.

**4.2. The shelter tent.** The shelter half is issued on the basis of one per enlisted man, with one tent pole, five tent pins, and one guy line; two per officer, with two tent poles, ten tent pins, and two guy lines. The shelter half is also used as a fly. The tent is made of light, water-repellent cotton canvas and is constructed in two sections buttoned together to accommodate two men; the old-type tent has a **ridge height of 43 inches** and a single closure provided by a triangular piece of canvas sewed to one end, while the new-type has a double closure with triangular pieces at both ends. The tent may be ventilated by opening the closed end.

**4.3. Pitching.** Instruction in formal tent pitching is given so commanders can establish units quickly and men can be taught to pitch tents in a short time (see FM 22-5). Except at inspection, tents are pitched on ground that will make cover and concealment possible and contribute to the men's comfort. **Two men should be able to erect this tent in about 5 minutes.** Pairs of odd- and even-numbered men pitch tents together (1 and 2, 3 and 4, and so on): each odd-numbered man marks the ground with his left heel where the front tent pole will go; at "Pitch tents," each man lays his rifle on the ground (muzzle to the front, barrel to the left, butt near the toe of his right foot), unslings his equipment, places his pack or roll two paces in front of him, and takes out his shelter half, pole, and pins. Each odd-numbered man drives a pin on his mark; the pair spreads the shelter halves with buttons to the center, the even-numbered man's half on the left, one half right side out and the other wrong side out so the rows of buttons may be inserted into the buttonholes (the letters "U.S." are stamped on the right side). The halves are buttoned together, the odd-numbered man slips his pole into the front eyelets and holds it upright, the even-numbered man pins down the front corners in line with the pins, drives a pin in front of the pole at a distance equivalent to a tent-pole length, loops the guy line over that pin, runs the other end through the loops of the shelter halves, and ties it, making sure the pole is vertical when the line is taut; then the rear pole is adjusted through the rear eyelets.

**4.4. Sleeping bag and mosquito bar.** The woolen sleeping bag, with its water-repellent case, and the mosquito bar are covered in paragraphs 17 and 18 of the manual; the sleeping bag is rolled with the shelter half as described in §5. → `references/bivouac-equipment.md`

## 5. Assembling and Packing Equipment

**5.1. The carriers.** Clothing and equipment are carried in duffel bags, canvas field bags, haversacks and pack carriers, rucksacks, jungle packs, field packs, and cargo and combat field packs. **The cargo and combat field pack is designed to supplant all other packs.**

**5.2. The duffel bag.** A large, durable container, easily carried, in which the soldier places all clothing not carried in his pack. Closing: after packing, fold the flaps envelope-fashion, slip the eyelets over the U-hook, and secure with the snap hook on the carrying strap; a padlock, though not issued, may be used to safeguard the contents. Carrying: by the handle as a suitcase, or slung over the shoulder caddy-style.

**5.3. The blanket roll (haversack M-1928).** Spread the shelter half on the ground or floor, buttons up, and convert it into a rectangle by folding in the triangular end (or both triangular ends, with the new-type shelter half). Halve the length of the tent by folding at the center. Fold the blanket to halve its width, fold again to one-fourth of its original width, bring the ends together, and place the blanket in the center of the folded shelter half. Place clothing neatly in the folds of the blanket, spread out to prevent bunching. Lay the tent pole at the folded end of the blanket, alternating the tent pins (or placing them together against the pole, heads and points alternated). Fold the sides of the shelter half and the pole end over the blanket; make a **10-inch fold** at the other end; begin at the pole and roll tightly; insert the roll between the layers of the 10-inch fold, making an envelope fold.

**5.4. The sleeping-bag roll.** Lay the shelter half on the ground or floor; fit the sleeping bag into its waterproof case and draw the straps through the slits in the case; with the new-type shelter half, fold in one triangular end at the base of the triangle to match the old type; stretch the sleeping bag so the zipper is at the side and lay it in the center of the shelter half, the top of the bag along the squared end and the straps extended — then roll as with the blanket roll.

**5.5. The other packs.** Paragraphs 21–27 of the manual give the step-by-step assembling and attaching of the haversack M-1928 with pack carrier M-1928, the canvas field bag M-1936, the rucksack, the jungle pack, the field pack, and the cargo and combat field pack, plus the packboard and the method of packing individual equipment on horseback. ## 6. Display of Basic Equipment for Formal Inspection

**6.1. The layout.** Section IV of the manual prescribes the display of basic equipment for formal inspection: for the dismounted enlisted man with haversack and pack carrier, canvas field bag or rucksack, jungle pack, field pack, or cargo and combat field pack (paragraph 29); for mounted enlisted men or those similarly equipped (paragraph 30); and for formal inspection in a barracks (paragraph 31). If shelter tents are not pitched, the relative positions of basic articles are as shown in the manual's figures; special items not shown are added to the display without disturbing the relative positions of the basic articles. If shelter tents are pitched, the blanket sits in the position shown for the shelter half and blanket, with its rear edge on line with the entrance of the shelter tent.

**6.2. Inspection in barracks.** The appearance of the enlisted man, the condition and arrangement of his clothing and equipment, and the cleanliness of his barracks are the principal considerations. Such inspections are usually held on Saturday mornings in most posts, camps, and stations; the details of preparation are laid down by the commanding officer. The foot-locker serves as the soldier's bureau and contains clothing and personal articles currently being used; **only clean clothing is kept in the foot-locker**, as dirty clothing tends to contaminate clean clothing. The lower part of the foot-locker is used for underwear, towels, and similar items; personal items and the contents of the foot-locker are arranged neatly. → `references/packing-and-inspection.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Repair immediately or turn it in.** Delay renders a damaged garment irreparable — and a missing button or a torn sleeping bag is a casualty in waiting.
2. **Clean and dry, always.** Dirt cuts fibers and holds moisture; damp leather, wool, and webbing rot, mildew, and fail.
3. **Fit matters.** Socks that are too small cause trench foot and frostbite; shoes not broken in cause march casualties.
4. **Pack the same way every time.** The roll, the fold, and the envelope fold are habits, not chores — they put the kit where your hands expect it.
5. **Current doctrine wins.** Modern individual equipment, packing, and field-craft guidance supersedes FM 21-15; this manual is the historical field reference.
