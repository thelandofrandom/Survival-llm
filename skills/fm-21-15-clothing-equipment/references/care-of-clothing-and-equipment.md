# Care of Clothing and Equipment — FM 21-15

## Responsibility

- On enlistment or induction, enlisted personnel receive a prescribed initial issue of clothing and equipment; other items are subsequently issued according to mounted or dismounted status, occupation, arm or service, and duty assignment. Specialized items have been designed to meet combat needs and climatic conditions (see T/E 21 and AR 615-40).
- Soldiers are responsible for the clothing and individual equipment issued to them, including proper care and maintenance. Articles that become excess because of changed status must be turned in. If, through carelessness, officers and enlisted personnel lose or damage issued clothing and equipment, the cost of replacing or repairing the articles is charged to them.
- In the combat zone the availability of supplies is of primary importance to every fighting man; while accountability of property may be suspended temporarily during combat, responsibility for protection from loss or damage continues, except in cases known to be beyond the individual's control.
- The stakes: a field jacket, M-1943, without buttons and waist drawstring will give inadequate protection in changeable weather; a torn sleeping bag provides inadequate warmth on cold nights; a rifle that has been neglected may not fire. **Casualties have been increased because soldiers' essential equipment was not at hand and in good condition when it was needed.**

## Care of Clothing

### Repair

Each soldier must make such minor repairs as mending rips and tears and sewing on buttons. The commanding officer directs sending to quartermaster repair shops those items that cannot be repaired by individuals. **A damaged garment should be repaired immediately or immediately turned in, for delay may render it irreparable.**

### Mildew and moths

Clothing should be thoroughly brushed and dried before it is hung in barracks or packed away. Leave air spaces between garments hung in barracks. At intervals take garments out and air them, preferably in the sunshine. If mildew forms, brush clothing thoroughly and hang it in the sun. Woolen garments should be dry-cleaned before they are stored.

### Cleaning

Each soldier must keep his clothing clean. A uniform that gets dirty and stays dirty wears out quickly because the dirt cuts the fibers of the cloth and collects and holds moisture. It is impossible to keep the uniform from getting dirty, but it need not stay dirty: brush and clean it, or have it cleaned, regularly. Remove all spots immediately — the longer they remain, the harder they are to get out — and do not have uniforms pressed unless all spots have been removed.

- **Meat juice or gravy:** cold water, then cleaning fluid, with a pad underneath the fabric; do not rub woolens excessively, as rubbing damages the finish.
- **Fresh fruit stains on cotton khakis:** boiling or even warm water while the stains are still moist.
- **Cooked fruit or berry stains:** stretch the stained portion over a bowl and pour boiling water on it so the water strikes the stain with force.
- **Grease or oil spots:** scrape off the top, then wash with mild soap and warm water.

### Pressing

For any pressing job, moisture is always necessary. For woolens, use a damp cloth between the iron and the fabric; for cotton khakis, dampen the surface of the fabric and apply the iron directly. Do not get the iron too hot.

### Waterproof and water-repellent clothing

After wear in inclement weather, dry thoroughly. To detect holes, hold waterproofed garments to the light and examine them; if defective, turn them in for repair. When water-repellent garments lose their water repellency, turn them in for treatment by a quartermaster laundry. For the raincoat and poncho: when packing, keep the folds smooth and even, and leave nothing in the pockets — articles left in pockets will tear and rub the garment in the pack and cause it to sag when hung. To prevent rust, hang the raincoat on a wooden or cardboard hanger, never a metal one. When soiled, wipe with a clean cloth and wash with soap and water. Do not leave raincoat or poncho exposed to the sun for long periods (heat dries them out and cracks them), near flames or artificial heat of any kind even for short periods, or contaminated with oil, grease, acid, or insect repellent.

### The helmet

- Do not use it as a seat (the chin-strap loops may break off).
- Do not use it as a shovel (the paint will be rubbed off).
- Do not cook in it — it is made of steel specially treated for maximum protection against bullet penetration, and heating may weaken it.
- Do not let it get scratched: every helmet is demagnetized so the steel will not interfere with the use of a compass, and serious abrasion of the surface may affect the compass.
- Take care of the liner: if it is put in the barrack or duffel bag, protect it well with blankets or clothing and pack something soft inside the liner as well as around it to keep it from cracking or breaking; do not sit on the liner or use it for a pillow.

### Socks

Change socks often, every day if possible, and wash them regularly, because perspiration rots the fibers. Socks must fit: if too large, they wrinkle inside the shoes, rub the feet, and cause blisters; if too small, they wear out at the toes. **Small socks are particularly bad in cold weather because they reduce circulation of blood and cause trench foot and frostbite.**

## Care of Leather

### General

Leather is one of the most important materials in Army clothing and equipment, and in wartime there is usually a critical shortage of the first-grade material from which shoes, boots, and other leather articles must be made. The soldier should give his leather equipment such care as will prolong its usefulness. Bleaching agents, dyes, and other chemicals used to restore or alter the original color of leather are prohibited.

### Boots and shoes

- Shoes must be broken in before they are used on long marches; breaking-in is easier if dubbing is applied frequently, and during the first few days a new pair should be worn for short periods only. **If practicable, a pair of shoes should not be worn on two successive days.**
- **Dubbing must be applied once a week** to the service shoes and combat boots of all troops in temperate climates and **at least twice a week** in wet or extremely dry climates; dubbing makes shoes softer and more comfortable as well as water-resistant, and is more effective if frequently and liberally applied and well rubbed into the leather. Shoes need not be dried before application. Shoes to which dubbing has been applied cannot be polished — footwear should be inspected for dubbing rather than for high polish.
- Cleaning: scrape dirt or mud off with a dull instrument that will not cut the leather, then wash with soap and water. **Drying by direct exposure to hot sun, fire, or strong heat is prohibited** because it is injurious to the leather.
- Get repairs early — do not keep wearing shoes or boots until the damage becomes serious. Turn them in for repair at the first sign of: rips in upper or sole stitching; shanks damaged or badly worn; any part of the upper or sole pulled loose from the welt; cracked soles; the first evidence of a hole in the outer sole, or an outer sole too thin that will evidently soon be worn through; heels missing or worn down more than **¼ inch**; or any other wear that interferes with comfort or economical repairability.

### Harness and saddlery

Harness and saddlery must have daily care. Separate all parts, unbuckle straps, and remove detachable parts. With a damp (not wet) sponge, remove surface dust, mud, and foreign substances; do not immerse in water. Rinse the sponge and, using as little water as possible, make a heavy lather by rubbing the sponge vigorously on saddle soap; clean each piece thoroughly, drawing straps through the lathered sponge. After the leather is partly dry, rub it vigorously with a soft cloth. If oiling seems necessary, apply neat's-foot oil sparingly on the flesh or unfinished side, using light quick strokes for even distribution — **excessive oil is injurious to leather equipment**.

## Care of Metal

- When not in use, bits, curb chains, belt buckles, and all other unpainted metal parts must be oiled lightly at intervals; when in use, they must be kept clean and free from rust.
- Removal of paint from metal parts is prohibited unless the responsible officer authorizes it for renovation.
- To restore dark finish on brass, dip the parts in a solution of ammonium hydroxide saturated with copper carbonate (saddle fittings may be painted with it by small brush, taking care it does not contact leather).
- Gold-finish insignia and buttons are cleaned with ammonia and water; abrasives are prohibited because they remove the plating.
- Rubber, manila paper, and sulfur-containing materials tarnish medals, metal insignia, and metal buttons.

## Horse Covers, Saddle Blankets, and Other Materials

- Brush hair, manure, and foreign material off horse covers and saddle blankets and frequently expose the under side to the sun; the part of the folded saddle blanket next to the horse's back should be cleaned daily by drying in the sun, working caked areas between the hands, and finally brushing. A stiff brush or curry-comb is prohibited on saddle blankets — it wears off the nap.
- The manual also covers care of cinchas or girths, web equipment, canvas, wood, mess equipment, the canteen, knives, and miscellaneous equipment (paragraphs 7–14).
