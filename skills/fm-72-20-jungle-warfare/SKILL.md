# FM 72-20 Jungle Warfare

## Purpose

FM 72-20, *Jungle Warfare* (War Department Field Manual, 27 October 1944, 146 pages) — the WWII Army manual for fighting in the jungle, superseding FM 31-20 of 15 December 1941. It covers the nature of the jungle, its effect on troops and operations, health, hygiene, and sanitation, training and practical living in the jungle, dealings with native inhabitants, and jungle operations: marches and bivouacs, attack, defense, patrols, special operations, supply, evacuation, and communication. Two appendices cover native plants and the use of the jungle machete.

> **HISTORIC WWII-ERA DOCTRINE — READ THIS FIRST.** This is a 1944 manual. Its weapons (M1 rifle, submachine gun, 60-mm and 81-mm mortars, rocket launchers, flame throwers), rations (C, D, and K rations), insecticides, and medical treatments (sulfur, trichloracetic acid cauterization, halazone) are dated, and some of its language about native inhabitants reflects its era. Its durable value is in principles and fieldcraft: how jungle terrain and weather shape movement and combat, disease discipline, jungle living, and small-unit jungle tactics. Where a current manual governs, it governs: the current jungle reference is **MCTP 12-10C, *Jungle Operations* (2020, also published as ATP 3-90.98)** — already a skill in this vault. Use MCTP 12-10C as the authority and FM 72-20 as the deep historical field reference.

## 1. The Nature of the Jungle

**1.1. Terrain and vegetation.** "Jungle" is frequently applied to the whole area where jungles predominate, although they may cover only part of the terrain. The jungle itself is dense tropical growth — underbrush, trees, vines, and giant ferns — found between the lower foothills or beaches and the high areas above the timber line. The sequence of growth in areas where jungle operations are likely:

- **Shore lines** fringed with almost impenetrable mangrove trees, or open beaches lined with bamboo or coconut groves. Mangrove swamps are tidal waters over mud one to four feet deep; twisting roots, mud, sharp coral, and low-hanging branches make them difficult and dangerous to traverse.
- **Beyond the shore:** pineapple, coconut, sugarcane, or rubber plantations, or rice paddies, extending a considerable distance inland; then low foothills, open to sunlight, with brush or long grasses 3 to 10 feet high.
- **The jungle proper:** undergrowth density follows the sunlight that penetrates the canopy — where it penetrates, underbrush is thicker. Where undergrowth is thick, progress except along the few existing trails is exceedingly slow and laborious; a path can be hacked out only with the greatest difficulty, and columns are in great danger of being attacked in the flanks or isolated and surrounded. The jungle is frequently interrupted by broad grassy savannas, by water courses (in mountainous terrain, streams normally shallow may become raging torrents within an hour after a heavy rain), and, at higher elevations, by sparse stunted growth above the timber line.

**1.2. Weather.** Jungle weather is hot, humid, and characterized by sudden changes. Within minutes, clear hot weather may change to a torrential downpour; with equal suddenness the rain may cease and sunshine on the thickly matted vegetation produces maximum relative humidity. Humidity is constantly high because of the swamps and the shading effect of vegetation. There is a rainy season of monsoons and storms; seasonal changes are noticeable but not pronounced. The humid heat of the day is often relieved by cool air from the mountains in the late evening.

**1.3. Wildlife.** Plant and insect life flourishes; the danger from wild animals is largely a creation of imagination. Most animals have a natural fear of man and keen senses that let them keep out of sight; there are relatively few dangerous animals in the Pacific islands and tropical America. Snakes are seldom seen, though they may be fairly abundant, and most poisonous snakes are encountered only while clearing ground for a camp — they present no more menace than in temperate climates, but reasonable precautions must be taken. Crocodiles are a menace in some regions, inhabiting swamps, lagoons, rivers, and occasionally salt water near stream mouths. The military significance of animal life: rapid movement of animals or birds may give scouts evidence of the presence of men. Do not rely on hunting for food — it is so difficult to come within range of jungle animals that edible fruits, nuts, and plants are more easily obtained (see Appendix I). → `references/jungle-terrain-and-health.md`

## 2. Effect of the Jungle on Troops and Operations

**2.1. Physical efficiency.** Troops newly arriving in jungle areas face an unaccustomed climate and unfamiliar diseases. Commanders must assure troops become adjusted to the climate and that health and combat effectiveness are maintained. The outstanding effect of jungle operations on troops is **fatigue** — overcome only by constant physical and tactical training under combat conditions. Physical training produces stamina; tactical training preserves stamina by teaching precision and preventing wasted effort. A day and a night in the jungle do not produce the war atmosphere of fatigue: exercises must be conducted for long periods and over extensive distances.

**2.2. Conditioning and acclimation.** Loss of body fluids by perspiration, increased concentration of blood plasma and urine, elevation of body temperature from exertion at high external temperatures, and the effects of the sun all lower the body's resistance. Thorough hardening to jungle service takes time; training must be long, hard, and realistic.

**2.3. Effect on operations.** The jungle limits observation and fields of fire, canalizes movement to the few trails, makes control difficult, and makes supply and evacuation slow and laborious. It also favors the defender, the ambush, and the small unit: columns can be cut off, flanks attacked, and positions approached unseen. Commanders must plan for reduced rates of march, for units becoming separated, and for fighting at close range with little warning. → `references/jungle-terrain-and-health.md`

## 3. Health, Hygiene, and Sanitation

**3.1. Disease is the main enemy.** Chapter 2 of the manual is devoted to the diseases of the jungle and their prevention: malaria and other mosquito-borne diseases, water-borne diseases (typhoid, paratyphoid, bacillary and amoebic dysentery, cholera), intestinal infections and worms, lice, mites (including the Kedani mite that transmits scrub typhus), bloodsucking flies, cone-nose (assassin or kissing) bugs that may transmit Chagas disease, and vampire bats that transmit rabies on Trinidad. Prevention is discipline: mosquito nets and repellent, treated water only, frequent bathing and washing of clothing, keeping out of native huts, bazaars, and cantinas, clearing and burning grass and scrub about a camp, and never sleeping on the ground where it can be avoided.

**3.2. Water purification — the numbers.** Water for human consumption should come from as clean a source as possible; at a stream, the men's water point is above the points for animals, bathing, laundry, vehicle washing, and fords. Purification methods the manual prescribes:

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Boiling | Boil **5 minutes** (except at high altitudes) renders water safe |
| Halazone tablets | **Two ½-grain, or one ⅛-grain, tablets per canteen**; double if the water is very cloudy; shake to dissolve; **wait one-half hour** before drinking |
| Calcium hypochlorite ampules | One ampule per canteen makes a purification solution; one canteen capful of solution purifies one canteen of water; **wait one-half hour** before drinking |
| Chlorination (unit level) | Mobile purification units, water carts, small reservoirs, or the water-sterilizing bag (Lyster bag) |

Heavy organic or inorganic matter is removed first by filtering, straining, or settling; an infiltration basin can be dug 6 or 8 feet from the edge of a stream or lake and 5 to 6 feet below the main water level.

**3.3. Insects and vermin.** Louse powder (army louse insecticide powder) is applied with special attention to the seams of clothing; frequent bathing and washing of clothing discourages lice. Against mites: clear and burn grass and scrub about camp, avoid sleeping on the ground, bathe with thorough soaping and scrubbing, impregnate clothing with standard quartermaster insect repellent by hand spray (collar, shirt front, waist, socks, trouser legs) — or, if repellent is unavailable, flowers of sulfur on skin and clothing. Against cone-nose bugs: keep out of huts, native shops, stables, barns, and chicken houses; burn abandoned huts or barns; use mosquito nets and protective clothing. A vampire-bat bite requires antirabic treatment; immediate first aid in 1944 was cauterization with trichloracetic or nitric acid, then sterile tannic-acid ointment and a tight compression bandage — **a historic treatment; follow current rabies post-exposure protocol**. → `references/jungle-terrain-and-health.md`

## 4. Training, Weapons, Clothing, and Equipment

**4.1. Training for jungle service.** Jungle training must be long, hard, and realistic — conducted for long periods and over extensive distances, under combat conditions. Men should be required in training to use only the items of clothing and equipment they will take into combat; this teaches them to live with a minimum of essentials and may show some things originally thought necessary to be nonessential. Clothing worn in training should be the same color and pattern as in combat — changing it creates an identification problem when reduced visibility already makes identification hard.

**4.2. Weapons.** Weapons must often be reduced to those that, with the required ammunition, can be carried by the troops themselves or on the limited transport that can move with them. Suitable jungle weapons, where observation and fields of fire are very limited, are short-range arms easily supplied with ammunition and readily transported: rifle and bayonet, automatic rifle, carbine, pistol, submachine gun, hand and rifle grenades, machete, and trench knife. The submachine gun and pistol share lightweight ammunition. The bayonet should be short enough not to entangle in vines and foliage. **Hand grenades are the most important defensive weapons for night attacks** and are invaluable against dug-in positions; they may also be used in booby traps. Each rifleman should carry **five or six hand grenades**. The machete, a tool indispensable to the jungle soldier, is also an excellent close-combat weapon (Appendix II covers its use). Light and heavy machine guns and 60-mm and 81-mm mortars are less maneuverable and harder to supply, but very valuable and transportable by pack, small carts, or on men's backs; lightweight mortars specially designed for jungle use are as effective as heavier models. Rocket launchers (high explosive or white phosphorus) and flame throwers are effective against caves and well-constructed defensive positions.

**4.3. Clothing and equipment.** Lightness of weight is essential because of transport difficulties; serviceability is essential because of resupply problems. Reduce equipment to the minimum, but omit no essential item. Tight-fitting clothing is unsatisfactory — hot and movement-restricting. **Wool, leather, and felt have proven unsatisfactory for jungle use** because of heat retention, moisture absorption, and susceptibility to mold and fungus rot; replace them with cotton, rubber, and canvas. The two-piece, dark green, herringbone-twill combat suit was found highly satisfactory as outer clothing. Rubber-soled canvas boots are desirable for stealthy movement but hard on the feet if worn long; the issue field shoe with composition sole is satisfactory. → `references/jungle-service.md`

## 5. Practical Hints for Jungle Living

**5.1. Sleeping.** In the dry season men can sleep comfortably on the ground — but always under mosquito nets. In the rainy season a man must be **off the ground and under a mosquito net**: best in an insect-proof hammock of light, durable, waterproof material with a rainproof adjustable top and attachable insect netting, not exceeding six pounds complete with netting. Lacking that, improvise hammocks or jungle beds from blankets, canvas, or shelter halves. A two-man off-the-ground jungle bed: four forked posts firmly planted, forks one foot above the ground; a frame of 2-inch poles laid in the forks; thinner poles across, fastened with vines or bark strips; the platform covered with light branches and leaves (checked free of insects) as a mattress; a blanket over all to hold it in place; shelter tent and mosquito net pitched above, or in dry weather only the net. In dry weather a man can sleep on the ground in a sleeping bag improvised from ponchos.

**5.2. Food.** All food carried should give maximum food value for minimum weight, and to the maximum extent possible should be ready to eat with minimum cooking — utensils create contamination danger, and cooking or heating during jungle fighting is usually impracticable. Rations for long-distance foot operations should be essentially dry; water obtained locally can be made safe with purification tablets, and its increased intake makes up for the lack of water in the rations. All components of the C and K rations may be eaten unheated; only the cocoa, lemon, and coffee powders need preparation, and they dissolve in unheated water. Field ration D is an individual reserve/emergency ration, but it creates thirst and should not be eaten except when drinking water is available. Small cans of fruit juice may supplement the basic rations; soy-bean foods, if obtainable, provide high nutritional value. Learn to cook rice — a staple of most oriental diets, usually contained in captured rations — with a small quantity of water, steamed; boiled rice with the meat-and-vegetable component of a C ration and soy sauce makes a good field meal.

**5.3. Protecting food.** Waterproof food bags carry dry rations under humid conditions and should be kept for the many months of service they give. C and K ration containers protect their contents from insects and humid air — but that protection ends as soon as the container is opened. All wet or damp foods must be eaten immediately after opening; dry foods may be kept several days if placed at once in waterproof food bags, securely closed by binding the string tightly around the mouth and securing it with a simple bow knot. Waterproof food bags are also useful for carrying foraged foods such as rice, dried meat, and nuts. Clean food is essential — a lifelong disease can follow one careless meal.

**5.4. Dealings with native inhabitants.** Section V of chapter 3 covers relations with the local population: their knowledge of the country, its plants, trails, and water is a resource; their goodwill is essential; and their customs, property, and persons must be respected. The manual's framing and some of its terms are of its era — read it as history, and apply current rules of engagement and cultural-awareness doctrine in any real operation. → `references/jungle-service.md`

## 6. Jungle Operations

**6.1. General.** The principles of offensive combat in the jungle do not differ from those in other areas; the methods of applying them are specialized. Forms of attack — penetrations, infiltrations, close and deep envelopments, encircling movements — are the same as elsewhere. In the jungle, as in all combat, every effort is made to gain surprise, and any measures that increase mobility are usually adopted. Because control is so difficult to maintain, **the mission and the plan of attack must be understood by all personnel**.

**6.2. Meeting engagements.** The leader who most rapidly estimates the situation, decides, issues orders, and executes will be successful — rapidity seizes the initiative and gains surprise. Emplaced artillery should be used as soon as possible if the enemy appears in strength; 60-mm mortars bring short-range fire to bear as quickly as possible, controlled by voice; 81-mm mortars are assigned targets once emplaced, controlled by forward observers on sound-powered telephones. Reconnaissance must be made even when closely limited; scouts and patrols investigate the extent and approximate strength of the enemy position. To await additional information might result in loss of initiative. Orders are always oral, given as briefly as possible with maps, air photographs, hasty sketches, or diagrams traced on the ground. An enveloping force signals when in position and ready to attack — by wire and radio if wire teams can keep up, otherwise by prearranged colored flares or other visual means, repeated — so friendly fires can be lifted; flares disclose the enveloping force's position and lose the psychological factor of total surprise, which the jungle frequently makes possible.

**6.3. Marches and bivouacs.** March rates are reduced by heat, humidity, undergrowth, and the need to cut trail; columns are vulnerable to flank attack and to being isolated and surrounded, and bivouacs must be sited and guarded with that in mind. Streams normally shallow can become raging torrents within an hour of heavy rain — a standing hazard for marches and bivouacs alike.

**6.4. Defense, patrols, and special operations.** Defense in the jungle relies on all-round positions, mutual support, and the hand grenade at night. Patrols are the commander's eyes where observation is measured in meters. Special operations, supply, evacuation, and communication are all slowed and complicated by the terrain: supply moves by pack, small cart, and man-carry; evacuation is slow and laborious; communication depends on wire, radio, and visual signals that the jungle degrades. → `references/jungle-operations.md`

## Operating Rules

1. **Disease first.** In the jungle, disease and fatigue are the main enemy; discipline about water, nets, and hygiene decides combat effectiveness.
2. **Fatigue is the outstanding effect.** Only long, hard, realistic training under combat conditions overcomes it.
3. **Travel light, carry only what you will fight with.** Train with exactly what you will carry in combat.
4. **Surprise is the jungle's gift.** Use it — and protect it: oral orders, minimal signals, silent movement.
5. **The jungle punishes haste and rewards the prepared.** Cut trail slowly, cross streams with care, sleep off the ground in the rains.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** MCTP 12-10C (2020) governs modern jungle operations; FM 72-20 is the deep historical field reference.
