# Jungle Operations — FM 72-20

## General

The principles of offensive combat in the jungle do not differ from those in other areas; the methods of applying them are specialized. Forms of attack — penetrations, infiltrations, close and deep envelopments, encircling movements — are the same as elsewhere. In the jungle, as in all combat, every effort is made to gain surprise, and any measures that increase mobility are usually adopted. Because control is so difficult to maintain, **the mission and the plan of attack must be understood by all personnel**.

## Marches and Bivouacs

March rates are reduced by heat, humidity, undergrowth, and the need to cut trail; columns are vulnerable to flank attack and to being isolated and surrounded, and bivouacs must be sited and guarded with that in mind. Streams normally shallow can become raging torrents within an hour of heavy rain — a standing hazard for marches and bivouacs alike.

## Attack

- The attack against the jungle enemy may result from a meeting engagement, a coordinated attack against a hastily organized position, or a coordinated attack against prepared positions of great strength.
- **Meeting engagements:** the leader who most rapidly estimates the situation, decides, issues orders, and executes will be successful — rapidity seizes the initiative and gains surprise.
- Emplaced artillery should be used as soon as possible if the enemy appears in strength; 60-mm mortars bring short-range fire to bear as quickly as possible, controlled by voice; 81-mm mortars are assigned targets once emplaced, controlled by forward observers on sound-powered telephones.
- Reconnaissance must be made even when closely limited; scouts and patrols investigate the extent and approximate strength of the enemy position. To await additional information might result in loss of initiative.
- Orders are always oral, given as briefly as possible with maps, air photographs, hasty sketches, or diagrams traced on the ground.
- An enveloping force signals when in position and ready to attack — by wire and radio if wire teams can keep up, otherwise by prearranged colored flares or other visual means, repeated — so friendly fires can be lifted. Flares disclose the enveloping force's position and lose the psychological factor of total surprise, which the jungle frequently makes possible.

## Defense

Defense in the jungle relies on all-round positions, mutual support, and the hand grenade at night — hand grenades are the most important defensive weapons for night attacks. Positions must be prepared against attack from any direction, since the jungle conceals approach, and against infiltration between positions.

## Patrols

Patrols are the commander's eyes where observation is measured in meters. They investigate the extent and strength of enemy positions, reconnoiter trails and stream crossings, and maintain contact in terrain where larger formations cannot see or control. Rapid movement of animals or birds may give scouts evidence of the presence of men.

## Special Operations, Supply, Evacuation, Communication

- **Special operations** exploit the jungle's concealment: infiltration, ambush, and raiding by small units that can move unseen where larger forces cannot.
- **Supply** is slowed and complicated by the terrain: it moves by pack, small cart, and man-carry, and the weight of rations, ammunition, and water dominates planning.
- **Evacuation** is slow and laborious; wounded must be carried over trails that vehicles cannot use, and stream crossings and sudden rain complicate every movement.
- **Communication** depends on wire, radio, and visual signals that the jungle degrades: wire is hard to lay and maintain through undergrowth, radio is limited by terrain and vegetation, and visual signals (flares) disclose positions.
