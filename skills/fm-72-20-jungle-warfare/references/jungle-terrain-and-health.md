# Jungle Terrain and Health — FM 72-20

## Terrain and Vegetation

"Jungle" is frequently applied to the whole area where jungles predominate, although they may cover only part of the terrain. The jungle itself is dense tropical growth — underbrush, trees, vines, and giant ferns — between the lower foothills or beaches and the high areas above the timber line. Sequence of growth:

- **Shore lines** fringed with almost impenetrable mangrove trees, or open beaches lined with bamboo or coconut groves. Mangrove swamps are tidal waters over mud one to four feet deep; twisting roots, mud, sharp coral, and low-hanging branches make them difficult and dangerous to traverse.
- **Beyond the shore:** pineapple, coconut, sugarcane, or rubber plantations, or rice paddies, extending a considerable distance inland; then low foothills, open to sunlight, with brush or long grasses 3 to 10 feet high.
- **The jungle proper:** undergrowth density follows the sunlight that penetrates the canopy. Where undergrowth is thick, progress except along the few existing trails is exceedingly slow and laborious; a path can be hacked out only with the greatest difficulty, and columns are in great danger of being attacked in the flanks or isolated and surrounded. The jungle is frequently interrupted by broad grassy savannas, by water courses (in mountainous terrain, streams normally shallow may become raging torrents within an hour after a heavy rain), and, at higher elevations, by sparse stunted growth above the timber line.

## Weather

Jungle weather is hot, humid, and characterized by sudden changes. Within minutes, clear hot weather may change to a torrential downpour; with equal suddenness the rain may cease and sunshine on the thickly matted vegetation produces maximum relative humidity. Humidity is constantly high because of the swamps and the shading effect of vegetation. There is a rainy season of monsoons and storms; seasonal changes are noticeable but not pronounced. The humid heat of the day is often relieved by cool air from the mountains in the late evening.

## Wildlife

Plant and insect life flourishes; the danger from wild animals is largely a creation of imagination. Most animals have a natural fear of man and keen senses that let them keep out of sight; there are relatively few dangerous animals in the Pacific islands and tropical America. Snakes are seldom seen, though they may be fairly abundant, and most poisonous snakes are encountered only while clearing ground for a camp. Crocodiles are a menace in some regions, inhabiting swamps, lagoons, rivers, and occasionally salt water near stream mouths. The military significance of animal life: rapid movement of animals or birds may give scouts evidence of the presence of men. Do not rely on hunting for food — edible fruits, nuts, and plants are more easily obtained (see Appendix I).

## Effect on Troops

- Troops newly arriving face an unaccustomed climate and unfamiliar diseases; commanders must assure troops become adjusted to the climate and that health and combat effectiveness are maintained.
- The outstanding effect of jungle operations on troops is **fatigue** — overcome only by constant physical and tactical training under combat conditions. Physical training produces stamina; tactical training preserves stamina by teaching precision and preventing wasted effort. A day and a night in the jungle do not produce the war atmosphere of fatigue: exercises must be conducted for long periods and over extensive distances.
- Loss of body fluids by perspiration, increased concentration of blood plasma and urine, elevation of body temperature from exertion at high external temperatures, and the effects of the sun all lower the body's resistance.

## Effect on Operations

The jungle limits observation and fields of fire, canalizes movement to the few trails, makes control difficult, and makes supply and evacuation slow and laborious. It favors the defender, the ambush, and the small unit: columns can be cut off, flanks attacked, and positions approached unseen. Commanders must plan for reduced rates of march, for units becoming separated, and for fighting at close range with little warning.

## Health, Hygiene, and Sanitation

Chapter 2 covers malaria and other mosquito-borne diseases, water-borne diseases (typhoid, paratyphoid, bacillary and amoebic dysentery, cholera), intestinal infections and worms, lice, mites (including the Kedani mite that transmits scrub typhus), bloodsucking flies, cone-nose (assassin or kissing) bugs that may transmit Chagas disease, and vampire bats that transmit rabies on Trinidad. Prevention is discipline: mosquito nets and repellent, treated water only, frequent bathing and washing of clothing, keeping out of native huts, bazaars, and cantinas, clearing and burning grass and scrub about a camp, and never sleeping on the ground where it can be avoided.

### Water purification — the numbers

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Boiling | Boil **5 minutes** (except at high altitudes) renders water safe |
| Halazone tablets | **Two ½-grain, or one ⅛-grain, tablets per canteen**; double if the water is very cloudy; shake to dissolve; **wait one-half hour** before drinking |
| Calcium hypochlorite ampules | One ampule per canteen makes a purification solution; one canteen capful of solution purifies one canteen of water; **wait one-half hour** before drinking |
| Chlorination (unit level) | Mobile purification units, water carts, small reservoirs, or the water-sterilizing bag (Lyster bag) |

At a stream, the men's water point is above the points for animals, bathing, laundry, vehicle washing, and fords. Heavy matter is removed first by filtering, straining, or settling; an infiltration basin can be dug 6 or 8 feet from the edge of a stream or lake and 5 to 6 feet below the main water level.

### Insects and vermin

- **Lice:** army louse insecticide powder, applied with special attention to the seams of clothing; frequent bathing and washing of clothing discourages lice.
- **Mites:** clear and burn grass and scrub about camp, avoid sleeping on the ground, bathe with thorough soaping and scrubbing, impregnate clothing with standard quartermaster insect repellent by hand spray (collar, shirt front, waist, socks, trouser legs) — or, if repellent is unavailable, flowers of sulfur on skin and clothing.
- **Cone-nose bugs:** keep out of huts, native shops, stables, barns, and chicken houses; burn abandoned huts or barns; use mosquito nets and protective clothing.
- **Vampire bats:** a bite requires antirabic treatment; the 1944 immediate first aid was cauterization with trichloracetic or nitric acid, then sterile tannic-acid ointment and a tight compression bandage — **a historic treatment; follow current rabies post-exposure protocol**.
