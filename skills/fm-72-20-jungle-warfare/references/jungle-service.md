# Jungle Service — FM 72-20

## Training for Jungle Service

Jungle training must be long, hard, and realistic — conducted for long periods and over extensive distances, under combat conditions. Men should be required in training to use only the items of clothing and equipment they will take into combat; this teaches them to live with a minimum of essentials and may show some things originally thought necessary to be nonessential. Clothing worn in training should be the same color and pattern as in combat — changing it creates an identification problem when reduced visibility already makes identification hard.

## Weapons

Weapons must often be reduced to those that, with the required ammunition, can be carried by the troops themselves or on the limited transport that can move with them. Suitable jungle weapons, where observation and fields of fire are very limited, are short-range arms easily supplied with ammunition and readily transported: rifle and bayonet, automatic rifle, carbine, pistol, submachine gun, hand and rifle grenades, machete, and trench knife. The submachine gun and pistol share lightweight ammunition. The bayonet should be short enough not to entangle in vines and foliage.

- **Hand grenades are the most important defensive weapons for night attacks** and are invaluable against dug-in positions; they may also be used in booby traps. Each rifleman should carry **five or six hand grenades**.
- **The machete**, a tool indispensable to the jungle soldier, is also an excellent close-combat weapon (Appendix II covers its use).
- Light and heavy machine guns and 60-mm and 81-mm mortars are less maneuverable and harder to supply, but very valuable and transportable by pack, small carts, or on men's backs; lightweight mortars specially designed for jungle use are as effective as heavier models.
- Rocket launchers (high explosive or white phosphorus) and flame throwers are effective against caves and well-constructed defensive positions.
- Light, mobile units may be insufficient for the attack of strongly organized positions; heavier troops must be moved up rapidly to reinforce advanced units once such a position is uncovered.

## Clothing and Equipment

Lightness of weight is essential because of transport difficulties; serviceability is essential because of resupply problems. Reduce equipment to the minimum, but omit no essential item. Tight-fitting clothing is unsatisfactory — hot and movement-restricting. **Wool, leather, and felt have proven unsatisfactory for jungle use** because of heat retention, moisture absorption, and susceptibility to mold and fungus rot; replace them with cotton, rubber, and canvas. The two-piece, dark green, herringbone-twill combat suit was found highly satisfactory as outer clothing. Rubber-soled canvas boots are desirable for stealthy movement but hard on the feet if worn long; the issue field shoe with composition sole is satisfactory.

## Practical Hints for Jungle Living

### Sleeping

- In the dry season men can sleep comfortably on the ground — but always under mosquito nets.
- In the rainy season a man must be **off the ground and under a mosquito net**: best in an insect-proof hammock of light, durable, waterproof material with a rainproof adjustable top and attachable insect netting, not exceeding **six pounds** complete with netting.
- Lacking that, improvise hammocks or jungle beds from blankets, canvas, or shelter halves. A two-man off-the-ground jungle bed: four forked posts firmly planted, forks **one foot above the ground**; a frame of 2-inch poles laid in the forks; thinner poles across, fastened with vines or bark strips; the platform covered with light branches and leaves (checked free of insects) as a mattress; a blanket over all to hold it in place; shelter tent and mosquito net pitched above, or in dry weather only the net.
- In dry weather a man can sleep on the ground in a sleeping bag improvised from ponchos.

### Food

- All food carried should give maximum food value for minimum weight, and to the maximum extent possible should be ready to eat with minimum cooking — utensils create contamination danger, and cooking or heating during jungle fighting is usually impracticable.
- Rations for long-distance foot operations should be essentially dry; water obtained locally can be made safe with purification tablets, and its increased intake makes up for the lack of water in the rations.
- All components of the C and K rations may be eaten unheated; only the cocoa, lemon, and coffee powders need preparation, and they dissolve in unheated water.
- Field ration D is an individual reserve/emergency ration, but it creates thirst and should not be eaten except when drinking water is available. Small cans of fruit juice may supplement the basic rations; soy-bean foods, if obtainable, provide high nutritional value.
- Learn to cook rice — a staple of most oriental diets, usually contained in captured rations — with a small quantity of water, steamed; boiled rice with the meat-and-vegetable component of a C ration and soy sauce makes a good field meal.

### Protecting food

- Waterproof food bags carry dry rations under humid conditions and should be kept for the many months of service they give.
- C and K ration containers protect their contents from insects and humid air — but that protection ends as soon as the container is opened. All wet or damp foods must be eaten immediately after opening; dry foods may be kept several days if placed at once in waterproof food bags, securely closed by binding the string tightly around the mouth and securing it with a simple bow knot.
- Waterproof food bags are also useful for carrying foraged foods such as rice, dried meat, and nuts.
- Clean food is essential — a lifelong disease can follow one careless meal.

## Dealings with Native Inhabitants

Section V of chapter 3 covers relations with the local population: their knowledge of the country, its plants, trails, and water is a resource; their goodwill is essential; and their customs, property, and persons must be respected. The manual's framing and some of its terms are of its era — read it as history, and apply current rules of engagement and cultural-awareness doctrine in any real operation.
