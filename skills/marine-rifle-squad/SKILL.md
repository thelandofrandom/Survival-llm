# Marine Rifle Squad

## Purpose

MCRP 3-10A.4 *Marine Rifle Squad* (August 2020, 284 pages) is the Marine Corps' manual for the smallest unit that fights as a team — the 13-Marine rifle squad. It is the foundation of all Marine infantry tactics: everything a battalion does is built from what squads do, and every Marine's first leadership experience is as a fire team leader or squad leader. The manual covers the squad's nature and organization, planning (troop-leading steps), offensive and defensive operations, patrolling, urban operations, and movement (mounted, mechanized, and air assault), with appendices on preparations for combat, hand-and-arm signals, orders formats, call for fire, site exploitation, fieldcraft, tactical tasks, and unit training management.

Who it's for: squad leaders, fire team leaders, and any Marine who must fight as part of a squad — and, for the survival-LLM reader, anyone who needs to understand how a small team of 13 organizes, moves, shoots, and survives as a unit. The core idea: the squad wins by fire and maneuver — one element fixes the enemy with fire while another maneuvers to destroy him — and it does so through disciplined fire control, simple formations, rehearsed immediate actions, and junior leaders who are trained to decide and act without waiting for orders. The squad is where maneuver warfare (MCDP 1) becomes a physical act.

## 1. The Nature of the Rifle Squad

**Mission and purpose.** The rifle squad's mission is to locate, close with, and destroy the enemy by fire and maneuver, or to repel the enemy's assault by fire and close combat. It is the basic maneuver element of the rifle platoon and the smallest unit capable of independent action in the offense, defense, and patrolling.

**Warfighting philosophy.** The squad fights by maneuver warfare at its smallest scale: it seeks the enemy's vulnerability, avoids his strength, uses speed and surprise, and relies on the initiative of its junior leaders. The squad leader's judgment matters more than any checklist, and the manual is written to build that judgment, not to replace it.

**Organization — 13 Marines.** The rifle squad totals 13 Marines, led by a squad leader, divided into three similarly outfitted fire teams of four Marines each, led by fire team leaders. The structure:

| Position | Role |
|---|---|
| Squad leader | Commands the squad; responsible for everything; directs fire teams; makes the plan |
| Fire team leader (x3) | Carries out the squad leader's orders; responsible for fire discipline and control of the fire team; positions himself to observe and control his team, typically close to the automatic rifleman |
| Grenadier (x3, one per fire team) | Grenade launcher (M320/M203 40 mm) plus rifle; engages point targets, suppresses, and marks targets |
| Automatic rifleman (x3, one per fire team) | Squad automatic weapon (M27 IAR in current organization, M249 SAW in earlier organizations); provides the fire team's base of fire — sustained, accurate automatic fire |
| Rifleman (x3, one per fire team) | Rifle; provides additional firepower, security, and maneuver mass |
| Designated marksman (x1) | Attached to the squad leader; provides additional situational awareness and precision fire at longer ranges; position in the formation is determined by the squad leader |

The senior fire team leader is the squad leader's second in command. The fire team is the building block: four Marines who train, move, and fight together, and who can operate independently for short periods.

**Preparation for combat.** The squad prepares through inspections, rehearsals, and the troop-leading steps (Chapter 2). Preparation is not a one-time event; it is the squad's daily discipline — weapons maintained, equipment checked, communications verified, and every member knowing the mission, the plan, and his role in it.

**Application of fire.** Fire is the squad's primary means of destroying the enemy, and the manual treats fire control as a leadership skill, not a technical one.
- *Fire commands.* When the squad or fire team leader decides to fire, he directs and controls the fires with a fire command: the elements of a fire command are the alert, the direction, the description of the target, the range, the target assignment, and the command to fire — given in a standard order so every Marine knows what to expect. Subsequent fire commands adjust fire (shift, add/drop, cease) without repeating the whole command.
- *Geometries of fire.* Fire is described by its geometry relative to the target and the friendly force: frontal fire (fired directly at the front of the target), flanking fire (fired at the side), enfilade fire (fired along the long axis of the target — the most effective, because every round that misses one target may hit another), and defilade (the target is protected by terrain from some directions of fire). Enfilade fire is the goal; frontal fire against a prepared enemy is the last resort.
- *Characteristics of fire.* Fire is characterized by its trajectory (flat for rifles at short range, plunging at long range), its beaten zone (the area where rounds strike the ground), its cone of fire (the spread of rounds from a weapon), and its danger space. Understanding these determines where to position weapons and how to use grazing fire (fire that stays close to the ground over its whole trajectory — the most lethal form of fire against an advancing enemy).
- *Effects of fires.* Suppression (forcing the enemy to keep his head down and stop shooting), neutralization (rendering the enemy temporarily ineffective), and destruction (killing or capturing the enemy). Suppression is the most common and most important effect at squad level: a suppressed enemy cannot shoot back, which lets your maneuver element move. The assaulting fire teams must recognize lulls in enemy incoming fire from effective suppression and use them to advance — and fire team leaders should increase rates of fire or improve positions when suppression is working.
- *Unit fires.* The squad's fires are coordinated: fire teams distribute their fire across the target (distributed fire) or concentrate it on a single point (concentrated fire), and they shift fire from one threat to another as the situation changes. The fire team organization lets the squad distribute fire on one threat and shift a fire team to an alternate threat without losing control.
- *Weapons to target match.* Match the weapon to the target: rifles against personnel, automatic weapons for suppression, grenade launchers against point targets and positions, the designated marksman against high-value or distant targets. Using the wrong weapon wastes ammunition and loses the initiative.
- *Range determination.* Accurate range estimation is essential for effective fire. Methods: the appearance of objects (a man is recognizable at certain distances), the use of known distances, the bracketing method, and the use of optics and laser rangefinders when available. The manual's range card and fire plan sketch (Chapter 4) record ranges to prominent terrain features so fires can be delivered quickly and accurately in the defense.
- *Line company weapons platoon capabilities.* The squad does not fight alone: the weapons platoon (machine guns, mortars, assault weapons) supports the rifle squads with heavier fires. The squad leader must know what support is available and how to request it.
- *Setting conditions.* Before the fight, the squad sets conditions: fields of fire cleared, range cards prepared, ammunition distributed, communications checked, and the plan rehearsed. Setting conditions is what lets a squad fight effectively from the first second of contact.

**Movement and maneuver.**
- *Base unit.* The base unit is the element around which the squad maneuvers — usually a fire team that stays oriented on the objective while the other teams move. The base unit provides the reference for formations and the base of fire for maneuver.
- *Fire team formations.* The fire team uses the wedge (default — good all-around security), the column (fast, easy to control), the line (maximum firepower forward), the echelon (protecting a flank), and the skirmishers formation (dispersed line for the assault).
- *Squad formations.* The squad's formations are built from the fire teams: squad column (fire teams in column — fast, easy to control), squad line (fire teams abreast — maximum firepower forward), squad wedge (fire teams in a V — good all-around security), squad vee (inverted wedge — firepower forward and to the flanks), squad echelon (angled to one side — protecting a flank), and squad file. The squad leader chooses the formation for the terrain, the enemy, and the mission — and changes it as the situation changes.
- *Changing formations.* Formations are changed by command and by signal; the change must be rehearsed so the squad can do it under fire without confusion.

**Actions on contact.**
- *Fire and movement.* One element fires (the base of fire) while another moves (the maneuver element), in alternating bounds. The moving element advances in short rushes (3–5 seconds, 5–10 meters) while the firing element suppresses the enemy. Fire and movement is the squad's basic technique for advancing under fire.
- *Fire and maneuver.* A more developed form: one element fixes the enemy with fire while another maneuvers to a position of advantage (a flank, a rear) and assaults. Fire and maneuver is the squad's basic technique for destroying an enemy position.

**Fighter leader.** The manual's term for the squad leader's role as a fighter — not a manager, but a leader who fights alongside his Marines, makes decisions under fire, and leads by example. The fighter leader's authority comes from competence, courage, and the trust of his Marines, not from rank alone.

## 2. Planning

**Preparation.** Planning begins with the mission: the squad leader receives the order, understands the mission and the commander's intent, and starts the troop-leading steps.

**Troop-leading steps.** The same sequence as the patrol manual, applied at squad level:
1. **Begin planning** — receive the mission, issue a warning order, make a tentative plan.
2. **Arrange for reconnaissance** — coordinate map, imagery, and ground reconnaissance.
3. **Make reconnaissance** — personally reconnoiter when possible.
4. **Complete the plan** — finalize, including contingencies (PACE for communications and movement).
5. **Issue the order** — five-paragraph order format (situation, mission, execution, administration/logistics, command/signal), briefed to every Marine.
6. **Supervise** — inspect, rehearse, verify readiness.

**Command and control planning.** Primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency plans (PACE) for communications and for movement. Attachments and detachments (who is attached to the squad, and what they bring). Fire support planning (what fires are available, how to call them, where the no-fire areas are, and how to mark the squad's position for supporting fires). Force protection (security, camouflage, dispersion, and the measures that keep the squad alive before the fight begins).

## 3. Offensive Operations

**Purpose of the offense.** To destroy the enemy, seize terrain, and gain the initiative. The offense is the decisive form of combat — the defense exists to set up the offense.

**Characteristics and fundamentals.** Offensive operations are characterized by surprise, concentration, tempo, and audacity. The fundamentals: orient on the enemy (not the terrain), attack his vulnerability, maintain momentum, and exploit success. The squad in the offense follows a cycle: preparation, conduct, consolidation and reorganization, exploitation, and pursuit.

**Squad in the offense — preparation.** The squad leader plans the attack: studies the objective, selects the approach, designates the assault and support elements, coordinates fires, and rehearses. Rehearsals cover the movement to the objective, actions on the objective, and the consolidation — and they are done on similar terrain, at the same time of day, with the same equipment, whenever possible.

**Conduct.** The attack moves in phases: movement to the objective (using formations and control measures, with security); the assault (the support element suppresses the objective while the assault element closes, breaches, and clears — using fire and maneuver); and the final assault (the violent, close-in fight for the objective itself, where grenades, close combat, and aggression decide). The assaulting fire teams recognize lulls in enemy incoming fire from effective suppression and use them to advance; fire team leaders increase rates of fire or improve positions when suppression is working.

**Consolidation and reorganization.** Immediately after taking the objective: establish security (all-around, with fields of fire), reorganize (redistribute ammunition, treat and evacuate casualties, replace key leaders lost in the assault), prepare for counterattack (the enemy's most likely response, and usually his most dangerous one), and report to higher headquarters. Consolidation is not rest — it is the most dangerous period of the attack, because the squad is disorganized, low on ammunition, and in a position the enemy wants back.

**Exploitation and pursuit.** If the enemy is breaking, the squad exploits: pushes forward, keeps pressure on, and prevents the enemy from reorganizing. Pursuit is the continuation of the attack against a fleeing enemy — fast, aggressive, and decisive.

**Attacks in limited visibility.** Night attacks follow the same principles with tighter control: closer spacing, more frequent halts, greater reliance on compass and pace count, and strict light and noise discipline. Preparation emphasizes rehearsal in the dark, and consolidation emphasizes security — a squad that has just taken an objective at night is at its most vulnerable.

**Infiltration.** Moving through or around the enemy's positions to attack from an unexpected direction. Planning and preparation emphasize stealth, route selection, and control measures; conduct emphasizes silence, patience, and the discipline to bypass the enemy rather than engage him prematurely.

**Attacking fortified positions.** Breaching protective obstacles (wire, mines, barriers — using engineers, demolitions, or manual breaching); attacking bunkers (suppress, breach, clear — using grenades and demolitions, and never entering a bunker from the front); assaulting trench systems (clearing trench by trench, using grenades and fire teams in sequence, and never getting caught in a trench without a plan for the next one).

## 4. Defensive Operations

**Purpose and characteristics of the defense.** To deny the enemy terrain, to buy time, to inflict casualties, and to set up the offense. The defense is not passive: it is an active fight from prepared positions, with fires planned to destroy the enemy as he approaches, and with counterattacks planned to destroy him when he is committed.

**Types of defensive operations.** The squad in the defense occupies a sector, prepares fighting positions, and fights from them. The defense is organized in depth, with mutually supporting positions and a reserve.

**Occupation of the defense.** The squad moves into its sector, establishes security, and begins priorities of work: security first, then fields of fire, then fighting positions, then obstacles, then communications, then logistics. The occupation is done in a planned sequence, with security posted before anything else.

**Priorities of work.** Security; clearing fields of fire; preparing fighting positions (foxholes, then improved positions, then overhead cover); emplacing obstacles and mines; establishing communications; and stockpiling ammunition, water, and medical supplies. The priorities are done in order, and they are never "finished" — the defense is continuously improved until the enemy attacks or the squad is ordered to move.

**Preparation of fighting positions.** A fighting position is dug to protect the Marine and to let him fire effectively: a foxhole (one- or two-man), improved with overhead cover, camouflaged, with a range card and a fire plan sketch. The position must have a primary and an alternate sector of fire, a grenade sump, and a withdrawal route.

**Squad fire plan.** The squad fire plan coordinates every weapon in the squad: sectors of fire, principal directions of fire, final protective fires (the last line of defense — a wall of fire laid across the enemy's most likely approach), and the fire plan sketch (a drawing of the position showing sectors, ranges, and target reference points). The range card is prepared for each fighting position: it records the range and direction to prominent terrain features, so fires can be delivered quickly and accurately, at night or in bad weather, without guessing.

## 5. Patrolling

The squad's patrolling chapter mirrors the patrol manual (see `scouting_patrolling`), with squad-level detail:
- **Purpose:** reconnaissance (gather information) and combat (raid, ambush, security, contact).
- **Patrol organization:** task organization, duties of personnel, and the patrol leader's preparation — the same troop-leading steps, applied to a patrol.
- **Types of patrols:** combat patrol, ambush patrol (with the ambush's planning, execution, and withdrawal), contact patrol, and reconnaissance patrol (zone, area, route).
- **Immediate actions upon enemy contact:** immediate action drills, danger areas (crossing by the procedures in the patrol manual), and the reactions to ambush, IED, air attack, and drone.
- **Patrol base operations:** planning considerations, occupation, base routine (security, rest, resupply, hygiene), and departing the patrol base quietly, leaving no sign.

## 6. Military Operations on Urbanized Terrain

The squad's urban chapter (see `mout` for the full treatment) covers the nature of urban operations, initiative-based tactics (the principle that small units in a city must act on initiative because communications and control will fail), ad hoc organization (reorganizing the squad for the urban task), exterior movement (danger areas — streets, intersections, open ground — satellite patrolling, cordon and search, ladder employment, overwatch), the cycle of reconnoiter, isolate, gain a foothold, seize, consolidate, and reorganize, interior movement (individual movement in buildings, commands, clearing procedures — room by room, floor by floor, top-down), defensive considerations (strongpoints, fighting positions in buildings), and stability actions (tactical callouts — using loudspeakers and interpreters to communicate with civilians and to call occupants out of a building — and checkpoints).

## 7. Movement

**Mounted operations.** Combat vehicles support the squad (firepower, mobility, protection) and the squad supports combat vehicles (security, dismounted reconnaissance, clearing terrain vehicles cannot enter). The squad must know the capabilities and limitations of the vehicles it works with.

**High Mobility Multipurpose Wheeled Vehicle (HMMWV).** Organization (how the squad rides), operations (movement, dismount drills, actions on contact from the vehicle), and immediate action and battle drills (reaction to ambush, IED, and rollover).

**Mechanized operations.** Maneuver considerations for fighting alongside armored vehicles; the Amphibious Assault Vehicle (AAV) as the Marine Corps' armored personnel carrier; and tank/infantry integration (how infantry and tanks work together — tanks provide firepower and shock, infantry provides security and close combat, and neither works without the other).

**Air assault operations.** Capabilities and limitations (speed and surprise, but dependence on aircraft availability and weather); planning; air assault plans (the air movement table, the landing plan, the ground tactical plan); terminology; task organization; considerations; and training. The squad must be able to load, ride, and dismount from a helicopter quickly and in the right order, and to fight immediately on landing.

## Appendices — The Working Tools

- **A. Preparations for Combat** — the inspection and preparation checklists.
- **B. Hand-and-Arm Signals** — the silent language of the squad: halt, advance, flank, rally, enemy in sight, and the rest. Signals are the primary means of control when noise discipline or radio silence applies.
- **C. Orders Formats** — the five-paragraph order, the warning order, and the fragmentary order.
- **D. Call for Fire** — how to call for indirect fire support: observer identification, warning order, target location, target description, method of engagement, method of fire and control. The call for fire is a skill every squad leader must be able to execute.
- **E. Site Exploitation** — searching a location systematically for intelligence, and documenting it before it is disturbed.
- **F. Fieldcraft** — camouflage, cover and concealment, individual movement, and range estimation (see `scouting_patrolling` for the full treatment).
- **G. Tactical Tasks** — the standard list of tactical tasks (seize, clear, suppress, destroy, block, fix, and the rest) with their definitions, so orders use the same words to mean the same things.
- **H. Squad Unit Training Management** — how to plan and run squad training: the training cycle, evaluation, and the standards the squad must meet.

## Output Contract

When answering from this skill, give the squad leader's answer: the mission, the task organization (which fire team does what), the formation, the fire plan, and the immediate action on contact — in that order. For any fire question, give the fire command structure, the geometry, and the expected effect (suppress, neutralize, destroy). For any movement question, name the formation, the base unit, and the control measures. For any attack question, give the phases (prepare, conduct, consolidate, exploit) and the consolidation checklist. Cite chapter and appendix (e.g., "MCRP 3-10A.4, Ch. 1 — application of fire").

## Operating Rules

1. **Fire and maneuver.** One element fires while another moves. Never move without a base of fire; never fire without a maneuver plan.
2. **Suppression is the key effect.** A suppressed enemy cannot shoot back. Recognize the lull and advance into it.
3. **Enfilade over frontal.** Attack the flank and the long axis; frontal assault against a prepared enemy is the last resort.
4. **Consolidation is the most dangerous period.** Security, reorganization, and preparation for counterattack come before rest.
5. **The fire team is the building block.** Four Marines who train, move, and fight together. Protect its cohesion.
6. **Decide and act without waiting.** The squad leader and fire team leaders are trained to decide under fire; initiative is the squad's advantage.
7. **Rehearse on similar terrain, at the same time of day, with the same equipment.** A rehearsal on different terrain is a different rehearsal.
8. **Know your range card.** In the defense, fires are delivered from prepared data, not from guessing.
9. **Tanks need infantry; infantry needs tanks.** Neither works alone in mechanized or urban operations.
10. **The fighter leader fights alongside his Marines.** Authority comes from competence, courage, and trust — not from rank alone.
