# Desert Operations

## Purpose

MCTP 12-10D, *Desert Operations* (also published as ATP 3-90.99; Headquarters, Department of the Army and Headquarters, U.S. Marine Corps, 7 April 2021, 176 pages), is the joint Army–Marine Corps doctrine for fighting in the desert. It supersedes FM 90-3/FMFM 7-27 (24 August 1993). The manual's governing idea is simple and brutal: the desert is an environment that punishes the unprepared more reliably than any enemy, and the side that masters water, heat, navigation, and maintenance wins before the first shot is fired.

The desert's common denominator is the lack of significant annual precipitation. Everything else — the heat, the cold nights, the dust, the distances, the mirages, the way sound and light behave — follows from aridity and open ground.

## 1. The Desert Environment

**Desert classifications.** Four types, and the leader must know which one the unit is in, because each fights differently:
- *Mountain deserts* — scattered ranges, barren hills or mountains separated by flat basins. Lakes in mountain deserts (the Great Salt Lake in Utah, the Dead Sea) are often salt. Movement is channeled by the ranges; observation is long from the heights.
- *Rocky plateau deserts* — extensive flat areas with solid or broken rock at or near the surface. The National Training Center and the Syrian Golan Heights are both rocky plateau desert. Trafficability is good, cover is poor, and the rock eats tires, tracks, and boots.
- *Polar deserts* — the Arctic and Antarctic. Cold deserts: precipitation is snow that never melts, the ground is icecap and glacier, and the temperature range is the widest on earth. The highest known ambient temperature is 136°F (58°C); the Antarctic polar desert has recorded −128°F (−88°C).
- *Sandy or dune deserts* — sand seas and dune fields. The worst trafficability, the worst dust, and the most deceptive terrain: a dune that looks crossable from a distance can swallow a vehicle.

**Temperature.** The highest known ambient temperature recorded in a desert was **136°F (58°C)**. In the Sahara during the Second World War, internal tank temperatures approached **160°F (71°C)**. Winter temperatures in Siberian deserts and the Gobi reach **−50°F (−45°C)**. The cloudless sky heats the earth by day and lets it cool to near freezing at night; in the inland Sinai the day-to-night swing is as much as **72°F**. A unit must be equipped and dressed for both extremes in the same 24 hours.

**Winds.** Desert winds reach near-hurricane force, and the dust and sand they carry make life intolerable, maintenance very difficult, and visibility a matter of meters. The Sahara's *khamseen* lasts for days in spring and summer; the deserts of Iran know the "wind of 120 days," sand blowing almost constantly from the north at up to **75 mph**. No one is buried alive by a sandstorm, but individuals are separated from their units in them — and separation in the desert is a casualty. Rapid temperature changes invariably follow strong winds. Even without wind, the dust raised by wheels, tracks, and marching troops betrays movement, and wind drags the signature downwind for miles.

**Water.** Water is the desert's defining scarcity and the commander's defining problem. Leaders must forecast how much water troops need and when they need it — before the mission (ensure troops are properly hydrating), during the mission (replace sweat loss, monitor the supply), and after (rehydrate, replenish sustainment stocks). When the water supply is limited, leaders must adjust their plans accordingly.

**Vegetation and wildlife.** Vegetation is sparse and thorny where it exists at all; it offers little cover and less shade. Wildlife is a hazard to be briefed, not a curiosity — venomous snakes and scorpions, and the insects that carry disease. The environment chapter's wildlife section exists so leaders can brief it, not so troops can study it.

**Environmental effects on personnel.** Heat is the primary casualty producer, and it works through water loss. A non-acclimatized Soldier or Marine sweats more profusely than an acclimatized one and needs more water. Units doing heavy sustained work — a forced march, constructing fighting positions — at **80°F WBGT** require nearly a **quarter gallon of water an hour**. A Soldier or Marine at rest in high desert temperatures may lose as much as **half a quart of water per hour** from sweating; in very high temperatures and low humidity, sweating is not noticeable — it evaporates so fast the skin appears dry. That is the trap: the person who looks dry is losing water fastest.

The manual's numbers on water loss are the ones to memorize:
- A loss of **two quarts (1.8 liters)** of body fluid — **2.5 percent of body weight** — decreases efficiency by **25 percent**.
- A loss of fluid equal to **15 percent of body weight** is usually fatal.
- Approximately **75 percent** of the human body is fluid.

**The conservation trap.** One of the biggest water problems is troops trying to conserve water. When troops recognize how valuable water is, they may not drink enough to sustain themselves. Leaders must enforce drinking — Soldiers and Marines may not drink their required amount and will need to be encouraged to drink more than they think is necessary, particularly during acclimatization. Consider water a tactical weapon: enforce consumption the way you enforce any other discipline.

**Hyponatremia.** The manual carries a warning box for a reason: consuming over **three gallons of water in a single day** may put Soldiers and Marines at risk for hyponatremia, a potentially fatal emergency where water flushes electrolytes from the body faster than they can be replenished. Do not overcorrect — enforce the table, not unlimited drinking.

**Heat injury.** The desert's heat-injury ladder runs from heat rash through heat exhaustion to heat stroke, and the manual's treatment is the same as the jungle manual's (see the companion skill): heat exhaustion is the most common and generally not associated with evidence of organ damage; heat stroke is the most dangerous and results from the body's heat-balance mechanism failing. Risk factors include prior heat injury (any heat stroke, or more than two episodes of heat exhaustion), lack of acclimatization, and the drugs and conditions that impair sweating.

**Acclimatization.** Minimize physical activity during the first days of heat exposure. Acclimatization is earned over days of graded exposure, not granted by arrival. Adjust work/rest periods by the WBGT table below.

**The WBGT work/rest and water consumption guide (Table 1-1).** This is the manual's most-used table. WBGT is the wet bulb-globe temperature index; heat categories run 1 through 5 (Green, Yellow, Red, Black). The table sustains performance and hydration for at least 4 hours of work in the stated category. Individual needs vary ±¼ qt/hr. Rest means minimal physical activity (sitting or standing), in the shade if possible.

| Heat category | WBGT (°F) | Easy work: work/rest, water | Moderate work: work/rest, water | Hard work: work/rest, water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 78–81.9 | No limit, ½ qt/hr | No limit, ¾ qt/hr | 40/20 min, ¾ qt/hr |
| 2 (Green) | 82–84.9 | No limit, ½ qt/hr | 50/10 min, ¾ qt/hr | 30/30 min, 1 qt/hr |
| 3 (Yellow) | 85–87.9 | No limit, ¾ qt/hr | 40/20 min, ¾ qt/hr | 30/30 min, 1 qt/hr |
| 4 (Red) | 88–89.9 | No limit, ¾ qt/hr | 30/30 min, ¾ qt/hr | 20/40 min, 1 qt/hr |
| 5 (Black) | > 90 | 50/10 min, 1 qt/hr | 20/40 min, 1 qt/hr | 10/50 min, 1 qt/hr |

Cautions that govern the table: hourly fluid intake should not exceed **1½ quarts**; daily fluid intake should not exceed **12 quarts**. Wearing body armor in humid climates adds **5°F** to the WBGT index; wearing CBRN protective ensemble adds **10°F**. MOPP gear and body armor change the category — account for them before you set the work/rest cycle, not after the first casualty.

**Dehydration symptoms.** Early symptoms: loss of coordination, fatigue, inability to concentrate, headache. Thirst will be present but not overpowering — do not wait for overwhelming thirst. As dehydration continues, effects become more pronounced, and dehydration compounds heat injury. Chronic dehydration increases constipation, kidney stones, and other medical problems. The risk of dehydration is just as great in polar deserts as in hot ones.

**Environmental effects on equipment.** Heat, dust, and sand attack everything: engines ingest dust and lose power; lubricants thin and fail; electronics overheat; optics are degraded by dust and heat shimmer; batteries drain faster in heat and fail in cold desert nights. Appendix B is the techniques manual for operating equipment in the desert — the daily cleaning, the filters, the covers, the discipline that keeps machines running when the environment is trying to stop them.

**Uniform and PPE.** Desert troops should remain fully clothed: covering the skin retains sweat on the skin, improving cooling, and prevents direct sun on the skin. The instinct to strip down in the heat is wrong — it increases water loss and sun injury. Flavored electrolyte additives can improve palatability and encourage drinking.

**Navigation and the desert's optical traps.** The manual's figures on day and night refraction are there because the desert lies to the eye. Heat shimmer and mirages displace apparent positions; refraction bends light so that objects appear where they are not, and distances are systematically misjudged in clear desert air — a hill that looks two miles away may be ten. Dead reckoning, compass work, and GPS are the correctives; the eye is not. Night refraction is a different problem with the same lesson: do not trust apparent position at night either.

## 2. Foundations for Desert Operations

**Understanding desert operations.** Desert operations are defined by distance, dispersion, and tempo. The battlefield is wide, forces are spread thin, and the side that can move and sustain itself across that distance wins. The operational framework is the same as anywhere — deep, close, rear, and support — but the distances are larger and the seams between them are wider.

**The multi-domain extended desert battlefield.** The manual's Figure 2-1 makes the point visually: cyberspace, space, and the electromagnetic spectrum are part of the desert battlefield, not an overlay on it. A desert force that loses its networks is blind across distances where blindness is fatal, because there is no terrain to hide the loss.

**Fundamentals of desert operations.** The fundamentals are the doctrine's answer to the environment: mobility (move or die — a static force in the desert is a target), dispersion (spread out so one strike cannot cripple the force), security (the desert's openness makes surprise hard to achieve and hard to prevent — both), sustainment (water, fuel, and ammunition over long lines), and tempo (the side that sets the pace forces the other to react across distances that punish reaction). A desert operation that violates any of these is an operation that is already losing.

## 3. Combat Power in Desert Regions

The chapter walks the warfighting functions through the desert, one by one, and the pattern is consistent: every function is degraded by the environment and must be deliberately compensated.

- **Leadership:** desert leadership is water discipline, heat discipline, and navigation discipline enforced by the leader's own example. A leader who does not drink, rest, and navigate correctly has no authority to enforce any of it.
- **Information and the information warfighting function:** in open desert, information is the terrain — whoever sees further and understands faster wins the engagement before it starts.
- **Command and control:** long distances and poor communications stretch C2; mission command (clear intent, decentralized execution) is not a preference in the desert, it is a necessity.
- **Movement and maneuver:** mobility is the desert's decisive advantage. The force that can move across the desert — and sustain the movement — can choose the fight.
- **Intelligence:** the desert's openness aids collection and defeats concealment; the intelligence problem is sorting the real from the mirage, literally and figuratively.
- **Fires:** long fields of fire and excellent observation favor fires, but heat shimmer, dust, and distance degrade target acquisition and battle damage assessment.
- **Sustainment/logistics:** water, fuel, and ammunition over long, exposed lines of communication. The desert logistics problem is a distance problem, and distance is the enemy's ally.
- **Protection/force protection:** dispersion, camouflage in a landscape with no cover, and protection against the environment itself — heat, dust, and the desert's own hazards.

## 4. Desert Offensive Operations

**Characteristics of the offense in the desert.** Surprise is hard and valuable: the desert's openness means movement is observed, so surprise comes from speed, deception, and attacking where the enemy is not — across ground he considers impassable. Concentration is achieved by movement, not by assembly: forces dispersed for survival converge on the objective, strike, and disperse again. Audacity matters more in the desert than on close terrain, because the distances punish hesitation.

**Types of offensive operations and forms of maneuver.** The standard types (movement to contact, attack, exploitation, pursuit) and forms (envelopment, turning movement, infiltration, penetration, frontal attack) all apply, but the desert favors the envelopment and the turning movement — the wide, fast move around a flank that the open ground permits and the enemy's linear defense cannot cover. The frontal attack in the desert is a last resort: it plays to the defender's long fields of fire.

**Warfighting function considerations.** Each function is planned for the offense: C2 that keeps up with a fast-moving attack, fires that can shift across distance, sustainment that pushes fuel and water forward with the maneuver force, and protection that keeps the attacking force from being fixed by its own logistics tail.

**Consolidate gains.** The offense does not end at the objective. In the desert, consolidation means securing the water and the ground, reorganizing, and preparing for the counterattack that the enemy will launch across the same open ground.

## 5. Desert Defensive Operations

**Characteristics of the defense.** The desert defense is a defense of positions that dominate movement, not a defense of lines: the position that observes and fires across the corridor is the position that matters. Depth is achieved by dispersion and by successive positions, not by a continuous line — there is no continuous line in the desert.

**Types of defensive operations and forms of the defense.** Area defense, mobile defense, and retrograde all apply. The reverse slope defense (Figure 5-1) is the desert classic: the position on the reverse slope is hidden from the enemy's observation and direct fire, and the enemy crests the ridge into a killing zone at close range. It trades the long field of fire for surprise and protection — and in a desert where the attacker can see everything, being unseen is worth more than seeing far.

**Transitions.** The defense is not an end state; plan the transition to the offense, because the desert punishes the force that sits still.

## 6. Desert Stability Operations

Stability operations in the desert are a population problem in a landscape that has almost no population — which means the population is concentrated at the water, the roads, and the towns, and those are the decisive points. The chapter's SWEAT-MSO lines of effort (sewer, water, electricity, academics, trash, medical, safety, other) are the framework for the civil work that makes stability stick. Warfighting function considerations and transitions apply as in the other chapters: stability is not the absence of combat, it is combat's continuation by other means.

## Output Contract

When asked about desert operations, answer from this doctrine: lead with the environment's numbers (WBGT category, water requirement, temperature swing), then the discipline (work/rest, drinking enforcement, navigation by instruments not eyes), then the operation (offense, defense, stability). Quote the WBGT table exactly — a wrong work/rest cycle in Black category is a casualty, not a mistake. Distinguish doctrine (the table, the consumption limits, the classifications) from judgment (which form of maneuver, which defensive method for this ground).

## Operating Rules

1. **Water is a tactical weapon.** Forecast it, enforce drinking, and never let troops conserve themselves into casualties.
2. **Obey the WBGT table, including the gear corrections.** Body armor adds 5°F; CBRN ensemble adds 10°F.
3. **Never exceed 1½ quarts per hour or 12 quarts per day.** Hyponatremia kills too.
4. **Do not trust your eyes in the desert.** Refraction and mirage displace everything; navigate by compass, dead reckoning, and GPS.
5. **Stay fully clothed.** Covering the skin retains sweat and improves cooling.
6. **Mobility, dispersion, security, sustainment, tempo.** Violate any one and the desert does the enemy's work for him.
7. **Maintain the equipment daily.** The desert attacks machines as surely as it attacks men.
