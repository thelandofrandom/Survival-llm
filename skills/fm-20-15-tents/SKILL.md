# FM 20-15 Tents and Tent Pitching

## Purpose

FM 20-15, *Tents and Tent Pitching* (War Department, 24 February 1945, 81 pages, with Change No. 1, 23 December 1947) — the WWII Army manual for every tent in the inventory: what each tent is, how to pitch and strike it, where to put it, and how to keep canvas alive in the field. Published for the information and guidance of all concerned, by order of the Secretary of War, G. C. Marshall. Change 1 (1947, by order of Dwight D. Eisenhower as Chief of Staff) supersedes the pitching paragraph for the shelter tent, new type.

The manual runs ten chapters plus a glossary appendix: individual tents (Ch. 1), wall tents (Ch. 2), general-purpose tents (Ch. 3), tent site (Ch. 4), care of tentage (Ch. 5), heating tents (Ch. 6), lines (Ch. 7), pins (Ch. 8), poles (Ch. 9), and miscellaneous information — flies, sod cloths, ground cloths, floors, and ventilation (Ch. 10).

**Historic — read this first.** The tents themselves are 1940s canvas on wooden poles and pins: shelter halves, wall tents, squad and pyramidal tents, hospital ward tents, command post tents — olive-drab cotton duck treated for fire, water, weather, and mildew resistance. Modern tents are nylon and aluminum, and nobody today pitches a squad tent with a 20-man crew. What survives is the *doctrine*: site selection, drainage and trenching, the rain-shrinkage rule, mildew prevention, wind discipline, heating safety, and the pitching sequence (mark, spread, pin, raise, guy, tighten). The pitching principles transfer to any canvas or modern tent; stock numbers, tent models, and some materials are period detail. Where this manual and a modern tent's instructions disagree on the tent itself, follow the tent's instructions; on site, drainage, and care, FM 20-15 is usually right.

## 1. Individual Tents (Chapter 1, ¶¶1–11)

**1.1. The shelter tent (¶¶1–3).** When shelter halves are authorized, the normal basis of issue is one shelter half per man, with the required pins and poles — two men carry and occupy one complete two-man tent. There are two types: old type and new type. The only difference is that the new type has a triangular piece at both ends, so the tent can be closed completely — superior in cold climates and against wind and rain. A shelter half may also be used as a fly to protect a man while cooking. The half is an individual item of issue, usually carried as part of the field pack.

**1.2. Old type (¶2).** The back is shaped like an inverted V and the front is open. It is made of 33-inch wide, 7.9-ounce cotton duck treated with aluminum acetate, wax, and soap to make it highly water repellent, in two sections that button together. Ridge height 43 inches. Ventilation by opening the closed end. One man can erect it in approximately 7 minutes; two men do it in much less.

**Pitching, formal (¶2b).** Except at formal inspections, the best available ground for concealment and the comfort of the men takes priority over specific alignments and intervals — but the principles of pitching are always the same. Platoons form as prescribed in FM 22-5; each odd-numbered man marks his position with the outside of his left heel near the instep, marking the front tent pole position; odd and even numbers (1 and 2, 3 and 4) pitch together. On the command: each man (if armed) steps obliquely right-front a full pace, lays his rifle on the ground muzzle to the front, barrel to the left, butt near the toe of his right foot, and steps back; all unsling equipment and place packs on the ground in front of them, packs two paces in front of the men's positions; each opens his pack and removes shelter half, poles, and pins; each odd-numbered man places a pin in the ground on his marked spot. Each pair spreads the halves on the ground, buttons to the center, triangle to the rear, the even-numbered man's half on the left — one half right side out and the other inside out, or the button rows will not match (the right side is marked by the "U.S." stamp). Button the halves together. The odd-numbered man adjusts his pole through the front eyelets and holds it upright beside the pin; the even-numbered man pins down the front corner in line with the pins, then drives the front guy pin a tent-pole length ahead of the front pole, loops the guy line over it, runs the other end through the loops of the halves and ties it, keeping the pole vertical when the line is taut. He adjusts the rear pole through the rear eyelets; the odd-numbered man pins down the rear and drives the rear guy pin two and one-half tent-pin lengths from the rear pin of the triangle, then adjusts the guy line. The even-numbered man drives the remaining pins on the left, the odd-numbered man on the right.

**Pitching, double shelter tents (¶2b(3)).** Two old-type shelter tents buttoned together at the square ends, supported by three poles (one center, one at each end). Preferable in cold or inclement weather for the added shelter, and useful in restricted areas for concentration of personnel. Four men button the four halves, ridge first then square ends, so the center eyelets stack in order: lower half of the front tent, lower half of the rear tent, upper half of the front tent, upper half of the rear tent. Two men hold front and rear poles; two pin the corners; one man enters with the center pole and inserts it through the center eyelets. Two guy pins at each end, one tent-pole length from the corner pins and on line with the side pins; guy lines adjusted through both loops at once. Double tents work only with the old type — the new type has built-in double ends.

**Striking (¶2c).** Unbutton enough buttons to grasp the poles, let them fall left or right; when flat, pull the pins, unbutton the halves, and roll packs.

**1.3. New type (¶3, as changed by Change 1, 1947).** Same characteristics as the old type except: an added V-section on the front end, and ventilation by opening one end or both. Pitched the same way, except both halves are spread right side out with buttons to the center, even-numbered man's half on the left; and after the front corner pins are driven, the even-numbered man pins down the front of the tent and drives the front guy pin two and one-half tent-pin lengths from the front pin of the triangle (the distance from the base of the front pole to one of the front tent pins). Striking as in ¶2c.

**1.4. Two-man mountain tent and jungle hammock (¶¶4–11).** The mountain tent is a complete two-man unit with pins and poles, built for cold climates (it has a built-in cloth floor, §5.4). The jungle hammock is the individual shelter for jungle use — off the ground, against flooding and ground insects. Both are pitched on the same principles: level ground, taut lines, guyed poles, ventilation. → `references/individual-tents.md`

## 2. Wall Tents (Chapter 2, ¶¶12–20)

**2.1. The tents.** Tent, fire-resistant, wall, small, O-D; tent, fire-resistant, wall, large, O-D; tent, fire-resistant, storage, O-D. All are olive-drab, fire-resistant treated cotton duck, issued complete with pins, poles, and lines per the parts lists in the manual (each part with its stock number — the manual's parts tables are the authoritative issue lists).

**2.2. Pitching (¶18).** About 20 minutes for four men to pitch the large and small wall tents; 30 minutes for eight men to erect the storage tent. The officer or NCO in charge selects the ground, indicates the direction the tent faces, the line it sits on, and the position of the door pin. Procedure: (1) drive a pin to mark the center of the door; (2) spread the tent, door foot stops over the door pin; (3) draw the front corners taut, align, and pin down; (4) lace the rear door if there is one; (5) draw the rear corners taut in both directions and pin down; (6) drive the four eave line pins on each corner in prolongation of the diagonals, about two paces beyond the corner pins; (7) loosen the front door; (8) loosen the two corner foot stops from the corner pins on one side only; (9) insert the ridge pole between the ventilation strip and the ridge; (10) insert the spindle of the upright poles in the ridge pole and in the grommets; (11) raise the tent and hold it; (12) replace the loosened corner foot stops, secure and tighten the guy lines to hold the poles vertical; (13) drive the wall pins through the foot stops as they hang, then the intermediate eave-line pins in alignment with those already driven.

**2.3. Striking (¶19) and folding (¶20).** Remove all pins except the four corner eave-line pins and the corner wall pins on the down-wind side; unfasten guy lines and hold them while the tent is lowered down wind; remove poles and remaining pins, fasten poles together, collect pins. Folding is a fixed sequence: spread flat, folded at the ridge so the bottoms of the side walls are even, sod cloth folded under, ends forming triangles; fold the bottom of the wall up about one foot; fold the triangular ends in to form a rectangle; fold the top over about 9 inches; fold top to foot twice; throw all eave and guy lines onto the tent except the second eave line from each end; fold the ends in to cover about two-thirds of the second panel; double the left fold over until the bundle is 3 to 5 inches from the right fold; place the right fold on top; tie the bundle with the two exposed eave lines. Folded correctly, the side walls rather than the top are exposed, protecting the top in handling and storage. → `references/wall-and-general-purpose-tents.md`

## 3. General-Purpose Tents (Chapter 3, ¶¶21–60)

The big canvas: squad, M-1942, O-D (6-foot 2-inch door); pyramidal, M-1934, O-D; command post, M-1942, O-D; hospital ward, O-D; assembly, O-D; maintenance shelter; surgical truck, O-D; and latrine screen, O-D — all fire-resistant. Each section gives the tent's purpose, its parts list with stock numbers, and its pitching, striking, and folding procedure — the same grammar as the wall tents, scaled up: more men, more poles, longer ridge lines, and for the hospital ward and command post tents, stovepipe openings and windows for ventilation. The squad tent's side wall comes in sections; the pyramidal tent's ridge and pole arrangement differs from the wall tent's; the assembly tent is sectional, the sections overlapping by approximately 3 feet to give depth. Do not improvise the sequence for a large tent: the manual's order — mark, spread, pin corners, set ridge and uprights, raise, guy, then wall pins — is what keeps a big canvas from tearing or collapsing on the crew. → `references/wall-and-general-purpose-tents.md`

## 4. Tent Site (Chapter 4, ¶¶61–63)

**4.1. Selection (¶61).** Great care in selecting the site; battle needs may force a poor one, but know what a good one is. (a) Protect from wind and storms — do not pitch in an open field or on an exposed ridge; if the wind is cold and one end must stay open, pitch the closed end into the wind. (b) High ground if possible — it will be drier. (c) Tough grass turf if available. (d) In hot weather, a shady area free of underbrush. (e) In woods, avoid a location directly under dead trees or trees with large dead branches. (f) By a river or lake, place the tent far enough back to be above the high-water mark. (g) In mountainous country, avoid canyons and dry creek beds — they "have been known to fill up with rushing torrents in a remarkably short time." (h) Do not camp at the base of a cliff or steep mountainside — avalanches and rockfalls. (i) Level ground, free of projecting roots and embedded rocks, extending about a foot beyond all sides of the walls, where a ditch should be dug to carry off rain water.

**4.2. Pitching in snow (¶62).** Before choosing a site on a snow-covered glacier, prod the surface with an ice or ski pole to find concealed crevasses — you may not find an area without them, but their presence should be known. Pack the snow hard by stamping with skis or snowshoes, or shovel off the top snow to firm snow below. A doorstep dug a foot below the surface makes entry easier and gives a place to brush off snow. Leave space between the tent sides and any protective snow wall to shovel out drift; the wall must not be high if snow is likely to drift. On a slope, form a horizontal platform, packing removed snow around the outer edge to widen it. Consider the prevailing wind.

**4.3. Trenching (¶63).** When in doubt, always dig the trench. The exceptions: very sandy soil that absorbs water as fast as it falls, or a little mound sloping off in all directions. On heavy soil, clay, or flat rock, always dig — those surfaces hold water. Dig all around the tent, just outside the tent pins; do not cut a V-shape — cut straight down, sloping the side away from the tent inward toward the straight side. Cast the dirt away, never against the tent — it will rot the canvas. The ditch seldom needs to be more than 4 or 5 inches deep, and in the shallowest places not over 3 inches; give it enough slope that water flows freely toward the outlet and does not back up. At the lowest point dig an outlet trench connected to the ring ditch to carry water off. If water may flow in from higher ground, dig a diversion ditch before it can reach the tent. → `references/site-care-heating.md`

## 5. Care of Tentage (Chapter 5, ¶¶64–69)

**5.1. General (¶64).** The duck is treated to resist fire, water, weather, and mildew; the pigments give a dark olive-drab shade for protective coloration and make the material practically lightproof. The treatment adds 40 to 50 percent to the basic fabric weight. Rope, thread, and wood are treated with preservatives. Field re-treatment is possible with "Compound, Coloring, Type 1, Color C Green" (stock No. 51-C-1598) or "Compound, Re-treating, Water, Weather, and Mildew Resistant (For Cotton, Duck and Webbing)" (stock No. 51-C-1608) — waterproofing waxes, mildew inhibitors, and coloring pigments in a volatile, inflammable solvent. The life of a well-made tent is in direct proportion to the care given it, in packing, handling, unpacking, unfolding, erecting, taking down, folding, and storing. The main enemies: weather, fire, mildew, and carelessness.

**5.2. Rain (¶65a) — the shrinkage rule.** Rain causes canvas and rope to shrink. Lines are normally drawn taut; as soon as they become water-soaked they may tighten enough to tear the tent apart — "tents have been torn completely in two under such circumstances." Two remedies: (1) keep the lines sufficiently loosened at all times so that when they tighten in wet weather they will not tear the tent — all eave and corner lines should have a free swing of approximately 18 inches at the middle of the line (tighten only for inspection); (2) place blocks under each upright pole, to be removed in rain — removing them automatically loosens all lines.

**5.3. Wind (¶65b).** In a strong wind, immediately tighten all lines, close door flaps, secure walls to foot-stop pins, lace all corner lacings, keep guy ropes tight — so the wind blows around rather than into the tent.

**5.4. Fire (¶66).** Fire-resistant tentage will not burst into flame but will burn and char slowly — "fire-resistant" does not mean it will not burn. Tent fires usually come from carelessness or failure to use the spark arresters issued with tent stoves: use them, clean screen-type arresters regularly, make sure the stove fire is out before removing the arrester; if none are available, improvise from fine-mesh wire screen. Use only extinguishers whose contents are not injurious to fabric — water, or water and a harmless antifreeze; some extinguishers contain acids or solvents that ruin tent materials.

**5.5. Mildew (¶67) — the greatest enemy.** "More good tents have probably been ruined by mildew than by any other single agent. Mildew has been known to ruin the best of tents in 3 days." Canvas is always inoculated with airborne micro-organisms; mildew thrives on contact with soil, dragging across muddy or damp soil, and rolling up while wet — jungles favor it by humidity and temperature. Prevention: (1) never roll up a tent when wet — even damp from dew, it will mildew when stacked away; (2) be positive the seams, edges (bottom edge particularly, where material is double), and sod cloth are dry; (3) do not roll pins and poles up with the tent; (4) under trees, inspect the roof frequently for drippings; keep tentage clean — fungi feed on oil, grease, starch, tree drippings, and sizing in the fabric; (5) to store, hang off the ground in midday sunlight, or dry indoors at normal temperature in a well-ventilated place, suspended off the floor. Storage: stack on dunnage of cured 2-by-4 lumber, at least 4 inches from a hard or wooden floor, 8 inches from an earthen floor; dunnage between courses in damp air; never near ventilators or openings where moisture condenses; never bag field tentage until thoroughly dried and brushed clean; visible mildew → open air, preferably sun; issue oldest stock first; withdraw mildewed tentage, brush, dry thoroughly, and issue at once to the driest installations or segregate it; unsalvageable tentage is salvaged or destroyed. Re-treating compound is applied to dry, brushed-clean tentage by spray or brush, brushed in thoroughly (seams and leak areas especially), dried at least 2 hours until all solvent odor is gone, in the open away from flame, no smoking — the solvents irritate skin like gasoline.

**5.6. Carelessness (¶68) and repair (¶69).** The greatest damage comes from carelessness: forgetting to loosen lines in rain, skipping spark arresters, careless rope adjustment, slipshod pin-driving, dragging tents over rough ground. Follow the pitching, striking, and folding procedures; never drag a tent along the ground or floor; pack pins and poles separately; transport a tent only with a covering (its bag, or the osnaburg/burlap it arrived in — save it for rewrapping); inspect frequently, especially seams, bindings, lines, and strain points, watching for mildew, foreign matter, small rips and holes, splitting seams, loose grommets, and rotting lines — and ask an officer or NCO if unsure how serious a defect is. Repairs: cement patching for minor field repairs (three circular patch sizes — No. 1, 3 in. diameter, for holes/rips up to 1½ in.; No. 2, 4⅜ in., up to 2⅞ in.; No. 3, 6¼ in., up to 4¾ in. — with a minimum ¾-inch overlap on all sides; two coats of cement, first dried to tacky, about 5 minutes, then pressed together with a roller); hand sewing (No. 15 sailmaker's needle, No. 5 ply cotton twine, beeswax, sewing palm — a patch must be stitched on any hole exceeding 4¼ inches, overlapping at least 1 inch on all sides plus ½ inch for turning under); machine sewing in shops for larger damage. → `references/site-care-heating.md`

## 6. Heating, Lines, Pins, Poles (Chapters 6–9, ¶¶70–80)

**6.1. Heating (¶¶70–74).** When using a stove in a tent, take every precaution against fire: spark arresters installed, shields around stovepipe openings, personnel trained in building and maintaining stove fires and in fire regulations. Stovepipe openings are built into most tents with canvas flap covers; when not needed for the stove they serve as additional ventilation. Individual shelters (shelter tent, two-man mountain tent) have nothing issued for heating; expedients when men must stay in them long or dry off: (1) build a brush fire over the area for an hour or two, clear all coals and sparks, then pitch — the ground stays warm for several hours and dry to sleep on; (2) heat 5- or 6-inch stones in a hot fire for 2 or 3 hours, roll or lift them in with forked sticks (a bucket or metal container holds them, or set upside down over them) — they give off heat for several hours; if room is short, bury them in a hole even with or slightly below the surface; (3) the one-burner cooking stove takes the chill off a small tent; (4) solidified alcohol in cans heats a small shelter tent comfortably; (5) a gasoline lantern is an excellent heater, and even candle lanterns take off the chill. Shields and hoods (metal, protecting canvas around stovepipe openings) are painted for camouflage or glare reduction — never paint them on the tent, as paint removes waterproofing and causes leaks; clean with naphtha or gasoline, prime (class 101), dry 12 hours, then lusterless olive-drab enamel (class 204).

**6.2. Lines (¶¶75–77).** Ropes are critical: the stability and safety of the tent may depend on guy lines, eave lines, foot stops, and door fasteners. Deterioration is mechanical (surface wear, internal friction) and chemical (weather, acids). Store in a dry, unheated building or a room with free air circulation, in loose coils off the floor on wooden grating or hung on wooden pegs — never in a small confined space without air circulation; clean before storing; continuous sunlight injures rope. Dry thoroughly in open air after wetting (hung loosely between two trees, not touching the ground) — wet rope stored damp rots ("dry-rot"). Wash dirty rope in clean water and dry thoroughly — grit grinds the fibers. Keep away from chemicals and fumes, especially acids and alkalis; drying oils such as linseed oil and paint also damage rope; keep out of reach of animals — excreta weakens rope.

**6.3. Pins (¶¶78–79) and poles (¶80).** Each pole is stamped with its kind (e.g., "Upright lower section, for 12 ft. 0 in. pole") — check the stamp so each tent has its proper poles. Inspect for cracks, splits, serviceability of the metal joiner, and missing or bent spindles. Upright poles settle into soft ground and loosen the tent — place blocks of wood or flat rocks under the bases. Sink poles generally 2 to 4 inches into the ground. Pins are driven as the pitching procedures direct and collected and stored separately from the canvas.

## 7. Miscellaneous (Chapter 10, ¶¶81–85)

**7.1. Flies (¶81).** An auxiliary canvas roof that sheds rain and cools the tent. Flies may be used with the large and small wall tents, storage tent, squad M-1942 tent, and hospital ward tent — but are no longer issued for the storage, small wall, large wall, and squad tents (see ¶¶15g, 37g for the hospital ward and large wall fly as separate items). Set tightly on the ridge pole, or better, on two ridge poles with an air space between fly and ridge for circulation when the tent will not move for a while. Usually guyed to the same double-notched pins as the tent, close to the roof; or projected as a porch, well away from the ridge. Any fly is a good wind trap — a waterproof tent without a fly is often preferable. A fly may also be erected as a separate unit to protect supplies, equipment, or cooking operations — the large wall tent fly is most often used this way and is issued as a separate item.

**7.2. Sod cloths (¶82).** A strip of canvas about 8 or 9 inches wide sewn along the bottom edge of the walls, sides and ends. When set up, it lies flat on the ground inside the tent, weighted with clean, dry material, to keep out insects, crawling animals, wind, and cold. Banking a tent inside with leaves and earth is a poor substitute — earth and leaves will not stay tight and earth rots canvas.

**7.3. Ground cloths and floors (¶¶83–84).** Ground cloths are generally not issued — trodden ground is easier to sweep and more sanitary than a filthy ground cloth. Exceptions: the two-man mountain tent's built-in floor (cold climates, keeps out wind and drifting snow) and the maintenance shelter's six ground cloths (a floor to lay spare parts and tools on). Wooden floors suit semi-permanent wall and pyramidal tents and make ditching unnecessary — but the floor must be the exact size of the tent: too large pulls the tent out of shape, and a new tent shrinks a few inches after a hard rain, so wet a new tent thoroughly, dry it, and only then measure for the floor.

**7.4. Ventilation (¶85).** Extremely important: a closed tent is less permeable to air than a house with the windows closed. All Army tents have ventilating facilities — built-in ventilators, stovepipe openings usable for ventilation when the stove is out, doors, and on most tents side walls that roll up; the command post tent has four windows. Never depend on air coming in around the bottom of the tent: if the sod cloth is properly weighted, very little air enters there — "like trying to ventilate a house through the cracks in the floor."

## Output Contract

When asked how to pitch, strike, site, or care for a tent: give the sequence first, then the numbers (7 minutes for one man on a shelter tent; 20 minutes for four men on a wall tent; 18-inch free swing on lines; 4–5-inch trench; 2–4-inch pole sink; 3-day mildew ruin), then the cautions — and say which tent the answer belongs to, since shelter, wall, and general-purpose tents pitch differently. Flag the historic equipment plainly: the models and stock numbers are 1945; the site, drainage, and care rules are durable.

## Operating Rules

1. **Site first.** High, dry, sheltered ground beats any tent pitched well on a bad site.
2. **When in doubt, dig the trench.** Four to five inches, straight-cut, dirt cast away from the canvas.
3. **Lines loose in rain.** Eighteen inches of free swing, or the shrinking canvas tears itself apart.
4. **Never roll up wet.** Mildew ruins a good tent in three days; dry seams, edges, and sod cloth before storing.
5. **Wind tight, rain loose.** Tighten everything in wind; loosen lines in rain — opposite treatments, both urgent.
6. **Fire-resistant is not fireproof.** Spark arresters on, stove fire out before the arrester comes off.
