# AFDP 3-50 Personnel Recovery

## Purpose

AFDP 3-50, *Personnel Recovery* (26 September 2025), is the U.S. Air Force's operational-level doctrine for getting isolated personnel home. It defines personnel recovery (PR) — in JP 3-50's words, "the sum of military, diplomatic, and civil efforts to prepare for and execute the recovery and reintegration of isolated personnel (IP)" — and lays out how the Air Force prepares for it, plans it, and executes it across the competition continuum, from peacetime cooperation through armed conflict. Its motto is borrowed from the Code of the Air Rescueman, written by Brigadier General Richard T. Knight in 1946: "That others may live."

The publication's central claim is that PR is not a rescue service bolted onto operations — it is integral to operations and should be considered across the competition continuum. Four reasons: PR sustains the morale, cohesion, and capability of forces; a successful recovery is a force multiplier, returning personnel to fight again; PR preserves critical combat resources; and it denies adversaries the intelligence and propaganda value of captured personnel. The Air Force is the only service specifically directed to conduct PR on a global scale (DoDD 5100.01), and its rescue forces should be ready to launch and recover any isolated person, regardless of Service, any time, any place.

Three groups carry PR, and the doctrine addresses all three: **commanders and staffs** who manage PR programs and missions; **recovery forces** (deliberately trained PR forces plus augmenting assets); and **individual Airmen** trained for potential isolation and recovery. Failure to prepare creates the perception that the United States cannot protect its people — a perception that erodes political and public support and diminishes credibility, influence, and access. → `references/pr-system-and-tasks.md`

## 1. The Personnel Recovery System

**1.1. What the system is.** The PR system is the integrated set of people, organizations, procedures, and capabilities that prepares for and executes recovery and reintegration. The Air Force's PR system has three pillars: deliberately trained PR forces; leaders who manage PR programs and missions; and Airmen trained for potential isolation and recovery. The doctrine focuses on the military option for recovery, but the air component may also be tasked to support diplomatic and civil PR options.

**1.2. Who is at risk.** Both combatants and noncombatants are at risk of isolating events. Terrorists, insurgents, criminal networks, pirates, or other non-state actors can be embedded in urban areas or less well-defined battlefields and seek to exploit isolated personnel. DoDI 2310.05 (Accounting for Missing Persons — Boards of Inquiry) lists the missing-personnel categories that frame the problem: missing in action, interned or detained in a foreign country, captured, beleaguered, and besieged against a person's will — and these categories extend to hostage taking, kidnapping, and abduction. Isolated personnel can range in scale from one person to an entire organized unit. While services identify personnel at higher risk of isolation for additional preparation, **all personnel have the potential to become isolated and should be prepared.**

**1.3. The PR system across the competition continuum.** PR is planned and executed across cooperation, competition below armed conflict, and armed conflict. At the lower end, PR supports military engagement, security cooperation, and deterrence in chief-of-mission environments. As operations escalate into conflict, Title 10 authorities clarify and DoD leads. Planners must understand the continuum to identify limitations, threats, and additional PR capabilities (including partner nations) at each stage.

## 2. Command and Control — JPRC, PRCC, and the Chain

**2.1. Organizations.** The Air Force PR command-and-control structure runs through the **Joint Personnel Recovery Center (JPRC)** at the joint force level and the **personnel recovery coordination cell (PRCC)** at the component level, with wing operations centers below them. The PRCC is the component's PR mission coordinator: when an actual or potential PR incident is reported, the PRCC assumes mission-coordinator duties, initiates PR planning, tasks and coordinates mission requirements with subordinate PR-capable units, and provides SAR incident reports and SAR situation reports to the JPRC.

**2.2. Division of labor.** The JPRC coordinates and tasks PR support when missions involve forces from multiple components (other than non-conventional assisted recovery missions). When the JPRC receives a request for PR support, it initiates action to locate the IP (if unknown), recommends and coordinates tasking of forces. When a component independently initiates a PR mission, it must notify the JPRC through its PRCC so the mission is coordinated and deconflicted; the JPRC then monitors the mission and stands ready to support. Direct communication with the AOC, JPRC, PRCC, and wing operations centers is essential — vital when combat conditions dictate formation of a combat search and rescue task force (CSARTF).

**2.3. Command relationships and authorities.** Chapter 2 covers the PR organizations, their command relationships, and their authorities. The key point: PR command relationships must be established in planning, before the isolating event, so that when the event occurs the only question is execution, not who is in charge. PR forces should also attempt to influence rules of engagement development as early as possible to gain maximum flexibility in recovering IP — ROE and law-of-war considerations are particularly important for the use of force during CSAR (including self-defense) and for the treatment and release of persons captured or detained.

## 3. Preparation — Before the Event

**3.1. Three areas of preparation.** PR preparation takes place in policy and doctrine, education and training, and equipment — focused on the three groups above (commanders and staffs, recovery forces, individuals at risk). Preparation is ongoing in peace and conflict: reading policy and doctrine, participating in PR exercises, completing SERE and Code of Conduct training, and organizing, training, and equipping PR forces (e.g., rescue squadrons) and others who may be called on. Success is far more likely if everyone is organized, trained, equipped, and able to process relevant information and act on it. Because PR events do not lend themselves to extensive prior planning, preparation done long before the event is what shortens the decision and execution processes.

**3.2. Commanders and staffs.** DoD policy requires commanders to link force protection programs and PR as a means of preserving the force. At the highest levels, commanders must be prepared to manage and synchronize PR; lower-level commanders must be prepared to coordinate and execute it.

**3.3. Forces.** Some units are specifically organized, trained, and equipped to conduct PR. Others are not dedicated to PR as a primary mission but have capabilities that can be applied to effect or support recovery, and may be tasked or designated to support or recover personnel. Still others are not primarily organized, trained, or equipped for PR but have a recognized capability to recover personnel. All three categories matter to the planner.

**3.4. Isolated personnel.** Airmen whose duties increase their risk of isolating events receive in-depth PR preparation including SERE school, ISOPREP, and review of evasion plans of action (EPAs). But Agile Combat Employment (ACE) changes the risk picture: Airmen who traditionally remained "inside the wire" at main operating bases must now move between forward operating sites and contingency locations, and isolating events during those movements are more likely — in land or maritime areas that may be more permissive, but where Airmen are still isolated personnel if they must survive, evade, resist, or escape. That level of in-depth preparation is not normally afforded to all Airmen supporting ACE — which is precisely why the doctrine flags it: the population at risk is growing faster than the preparation pipeline.

**3.5. Adaptation.** Lessons learned from past exercises and operational PR events — saved in the Joint Lessons Learned Information System (JLLIS) and validated via PR events, debriefings, exercises, wargames, and other activities — feed continuous improvement of preparation. Historical data are vital. A named example: Red Flag Rescue, the Air Force exercise that prepares USAF, joint, and coalition forces for PR.

## 4. Planning — The PR Appendix and Force Flow

**4.1. Where PR planning lives.** PR planning is part of contingency and crisis planning, using the joint planning process for air (JPPA), with detailed mission analysis, course-of-action development, and wargaming based on the plan's mission goals and tasks. The product is the PR appendix — **Appendix 5 to Annex C** of a JFC's campaign plan or the air component's joint air operations plan (JAOP) — built from a completed PR mission analysis. Planning options may be diplomatic, civil, or military; the bulk of military planning is the military option, though planners may support the other two.

**4.2. What pre-mission planning needs.** The information required for planning and for launch authority includes: location of the IP, authentication, threat, weather, terrain assessment, evaluation of safe passage corridors, and air refueling capabilities. Best practice: co-locate all planning activities dedicated to PR, and keep direct communication with the AOC, JPRC, PRCC, and wing operations centers.

**4.3. Force planning and designations.** The purpose of force planning is to identify the forces needed and phase them into the operational area. Planners identify PR resources, delivery date, and employment location, supported by time-phased force and deployment data (TPFDD). Commanders must ensure the force flow includes sufficient recovery forces commensurate with the risk to force and the risk of isolation. PR forces may be assigned designation coding on the TPFDD — **dedicated, designated, or capable** — so planners can extract PR data and schedule recovery-force deployments to support the plan. A PR designation tells the planner which assets are available and at what level of PR readiness.

**4.4. Other planning considerations.** Consider the capabilities of the host nation, other Services and functional components, and multinational forces. Integrate PR into contingency and crisis planning and update Appendix 5 to Annex C of the JAOP accordingly. Address operational contract support requirements in the JAOP's Annex W. Ensure planners and forces are thoroughly familiar with the law of war and applicable ROE.

## 5. Execution — The Five Tasks

USAF PR is centered on five execution tasks, accomplished once an isolating event has occurred. CSAR is the preferred recovery mechanism and the primary means the air component employs to accomplish the "recover" task; it is the method for PR in denied and hostile environments, where it is often the only feasible means. CSAR is **not** conducted in non-contested operations such as humanitarian assistance, civil SAR, or casualty evacuation. The three CSAR components are command and control, recovery forces, and the IP.

**5.1. Report.** Awareness and notification of the isolating event. Rapid, accurate notification is essential. Threat conditions permitting, IP should attempt contact with friendly forces per the notification procedures in the PR SPINS portion of the ATO. Once an incident is reported, the PRCC assumes mission-coordinator duties, initiates planning, and reports to the JPRC.

**5.2. Locate.** Methods include theater electronic surveillance, reconnaissance, C2 aircraft, space assets, wingman reports, counterintelligence support to force protection, and visual and electronic search by trained PR forces. Even with precise coordinates, PR forces must authenticate IP identity before facilitating support and recovery. An effective authentication system is essential to prevent compromise of critical information and minimize risk to IP and the recovery force — rescue assets are extremely vulnerable during execution and need exact, reliable authentication. Authentication methods include CSAR code words, letters, numbers, visual signals, and IP report data; theater-specific procedures are published in directives, OPLANs, and PR SPINS. IP and rescue forces must take extreme care not to compromise authentication information.

**5.3. Support.** The planned effort to ensure the physical and psychological sustainment of IP. Five objectives: situational awareness, protection, establishing two-way communications, morale-building support, and aerial resupply (including aerial escort to a supply cache or a more secure area). Support may also include suppression of enemy threats to the IP — precluding capture and disrupting the adversary's response to rescue efforts. When possible, combat rescue officers (CROs), pararescuemen, and equipment may be pre-positioned to support IP until recovery. Family members may receive physical and psychological assistance during the event. Support continues during a captivity scenario, including missing-in-action cases, until the PR event is resolved.

**5.4. Recover.** Activities by commanders, staff, recovery forces, and IP to physically recover the IP. The PRCC assesses the situation quickly, determines mission feasibility, and disseminates data to units that may participate. Once execution is feasible, units may be tasked to initiate or continue planning or launch from alert. The JFC or designated PR supported commander issues the execution order; the JFC's PR CONOPS or SPINS direct launch and execution authority. Recovery responses: **immediate** (from ATO-directed ground or airborne alert posture; forces forward-located or loitering to cut flight time and air refueling needs, sometimes embedded in existing airborne missions); **deliberate** (chosen when immediate response is not possible for environmental, political, or threat reasons — may involve creating a recovery task force from single-component, joint, multinational, or multi-agency assets); and **hold** (a mission is never closed due to risk or inability to locate the IP, but it may be placed on hold when the information required to execute does not meet the commander's execution criteria, e.g., location or intelligence).

**5.5. Reintegrate.** Reintroducing the IP into society and ultimately returning the member to service. Reintegration provides medical care and protects the well-being of recovered personnel through decompression, while debriefings gather intelligence and SERE information. It begins when the recovery force relinquishes physical control of the IP to a designated team member or organization in the theater reintegration process. Theater reintegration runs in two phases under combatant commander guidance; Phase III may be a long-term Service responsibility with no specific end date. Non-DoD American citizens recovered by DoD PR teams are reintegrated through a Department of State process unless DoD's specialized capabilities are specifically required, requested, and approved.

## 6. Recovery Forces and the CSAR Task Force

**6.1. Who the recovery forces are.** CSAR recovery forces are personnel who organize, train, and perform PR operations, plus augmentation forces who assist. Combining the proper mix of air and ground PR elements, the Air Force is postured to recover IP in any environment. All aircraft under the air component commander's C2 should be prepared to assist recovery operations.

**6.2. The CSAR task force (CSARTF).** In denied or contested environments, PR efforts are normally combined into a tailored CSARTF — a mechanism that has significantly enhanced CSAR operations. The CSARTF typically has two elements: PR assets and augmenting assets, including designated aircraft, specially trained personnel, and specific positions crucial to the mission. It is designed to protect the IP and the recovery force package before, during, and on egress from small arms, surface-to-air, air-to-air, and air-to-ground threats. Its exact composition varies with the threats en route and near the IP; with proper planning it can defeat or degrade the threat to an acceptable level of risk. Because threats and IP status change, the CSARTF may require numerous adjustments and augmentation during recovery — and the PRCC should be prepared to request augmentation from other Service or functional components through the JPRC.

**6.3. Vertical lift.** Rescue helicopters conduct long-range, low-level, day or night, marginal-weather operations into hostile environments to recover distressed or isolated personnel. Missions are usually flown as multi-ship formations for mutual support, but may be executed with a single ship based on threat and supporting asset availability.

**6.4. Dynamic rescue.** The doctrine also addresses dynamic rescue — recovery operations that must adapt in real time as the situation changes, with forces re-tasked and re-composed during execution rather than following a fixed plan. The CSARTF's ability to adjust is what makes dynamic rescue possible.

## 7. Preparation, Planning, and Execution — The Through-Line

The publication's structure is its argument: preparation (Chapter 3), planning (Chapter 4), execution (Chapter 5). Preparation is what makes the other two fast. Planning is what makes execution coherent. Execution is five tasks, not one event — report, locate, support, recover, reintegrate — and the mission is not over when the helicopter lands with the survivor aboard. Reintegration, including decompression, medical care, debriefing, and the long tail of Phase III, is part of the mission, and the doctrine treats a recovery without reintegration as unfinished business. The same applies to the family: physical and psychological assistance to IP families runs through the event and into recovery.

## Output Contract

When asked about Air Force personnel recovery, answer from this publication's structure: (1) define PR and the three groups who carry it (commanders/staffs, recovery forces, individuals at risk); (2) describe the PR system and the JPRC/PRCC command-and-control chain; (3) walk the preparation–planning–execution sequence; (4) name the five execution tasks — report, locate, support, recover, reintegrate — and what each requires, including authentication before recovery and the immediate/deliberate/hold response options; (5) describe the CSARTF and its two elements; (6) note the ACE complication — the at-risk population now includes Airmen moving between forward sites who may not have received in-depth SERE preparation. Distinguish AFDP 3-50 (Air Force operational doctrine, 2025) from JP 3-50 (joint PR doctrine) and from the 1990s CSAR publications JP 3-50.2/3-50.21, which cover the same mission at the joint level with older organizations and terminology.

## Operating Rules

1. **PR is integral to operations, not an add-on.** Plan it across the competition continuum, from peacetime through armed conflict.
2. **Prepare before the event.** PR events do not lend themselves to extensive prior planning; preparation is what shortens decision and execution.
3. **Authenticate before recovering.** Exact, reliable authentication is essential — rescue assets are extremely vulnerable during execution, and compromised authentication data endangers both the IP and the force.
4. **A mission is never closed for risk or inability to locate.** It may be placed on hold when execution criteria are not met — and resumed when they are.
5. **CSAR is the preferred recovery mechanism in denied and hostile environments** — and it is not used for humanitarian assistance, civil SAR, or casualty evacuation.
6. **Reintegration is part of the mission.** Recovery without decompression, medical care, and debriefing is unfinished.
7. **Mind the ACE gap.** Airmen moving between forward operating sites face isolation risk without the in-depth preparation given to high-risk aircrew — plan for them explicitly.
