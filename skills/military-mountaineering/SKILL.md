# Military Mountaineering

## Purpose

TC 3-97.61 *Military Mountaineering* (U.S. Army, November 2025, 432 pages) is the Army's current doctrine for fighting and moving in mountains. It supersedes the 2012 edition. This skill distills it into field-usable doctrine for a small-unit leader, instructor, or self-reliant mountaineer operating in steep, cold, high terrain: how mountains punish the unprepared, how to move over rock, snow, and ice, how to build rope systems that actually hold, and how to keep people alive when altitude, cold, and avalanches are all working against them.

Core idea: **in mountains, the terrain is the enemy's ally and your logistics chain's enemy.** A mountain unit's combat power is capped by acclimatization, calories, water, and rope skill — not by firepower. The manual's through-line is progression: train the individual first (levels 1–3), then the team, then the unit; never skip a step, because the mountain collects the debt later, usually at night, in a storm, above a cliff.

This skill covers chapters 1–16 and appendices A–C: mountain environment and weather, training levels, environmental injuries and altitude illness, sustainment and bivouac, avalanches, mobility, navigation, knots and rope management, anchors, rope systems, fixed ropes and rappelling, hauling and lowering, stream crossing, glacier travel and crevasse rescue, mountain rescue and evacuation, and rope bridges.

## 1. The Mountain Environment — Read the Ground Before You Fight It

Mountains are formed by folding, faulting, volcanic activity, and erosion, and the manual classifies terrain by slope and technical difficulty, not just elevation. What matters tactically:

- **Observation and fields of fire** are superb from heights — and mutual: ridgelines are exposed, valleys are kill zones, and a position that sees everything can be seen from everywhere.
- **Avenues of approach** in mountains are few and obvious: passes, saddles, ridgelines, and valley floors. Control the pass and you control movement; bypasses are rare and usually worse.
- **Key and decisive terrain** is almost always high ground commanding a pass, a valley junction, or a water source. The manual stresses that in mountains, key terrain is often *the only* terrain that matters.
- **Obstacles** are natural and permanent: cliffs, glaciers, rivers in spate, avalanche paths. They canalize movement more reliably than any minefield.
- **Cover and concealment** are thin above the treeline. Rock offers cover from direct fire but not from plunging fire or observation; snow offers concealment only while it is falling or fresh.
- **Terrain levels (I–III)** grade difficulty: Level I is nontechnical walking on trails and moderate slopes; Level II adds steep, exposed ground requiring hands and basic rope work; Level III is technical rock, snow, and ice requiring trained mountaineers, fixed ropes, and anchors. Plan the mission to the level, not to the map's optimism.

**Weather is the decisive factor.** Mountain weather is local, violent, and fast: temperature drops roughly with altitude, wind accelerates over ridges and through passes, and afternoon thunderstorms build over peaks with little warning. The manual's planning rules:

- Forecast at the *elevation you will occupy*, not the valley floor; expect the summit to be 10–20°F colder and far windier.
- Plan movement to be off exposed ridges and summits before afternoon convection builds; lightning kills mountaineers who linger on high ground.
- Whiteout, fog, and spindrift can erase navigation in minutes — carry compass, altimeter, and GPS, and know how to use all three (see §7).
- Treat every weather forecast as a guess with a 6–12 hour horizon; re-evaluate at every halt.

## 2. Training Levels — Progression Is the Safety System

The manual defines three levels of military mountaineer. Do not assign tasks above a soldier's level.

- **Level 1 — Basic Military Mountaineer.** Individual movement on steep, nontechnical and low-technical terrain: mountain walking, scrambling, basic knots, belaying, rappelling, fixed-rope ascent, stream crossing, snow travel, self-arrest, and avalanche awareness. This is the baseline every member of a mountain unit needs.
- **Level 2 — Advanced Military Mountaineer.** Leads climbs, builds anchors, runs rope systems, rigs fixed ropes and rope bridges, performs hauling and lowering, leads glacier travel and crevasse rescue, and trains Level 1 soldiers. The squad's technical backbone.
- **Level 3 — Master Military Mountaineer.** Plans and supervises unit mountain operations, runs the training program, advises the commander on route selection, risk, and avalanche management, and performs high-angle rescue. The unit's subject-matter expert.

**Unit training progression:** individual skills → buddy/team skills (belay teams, rope teams) → collective tasks (fixed-rope installations, rope bridges, glacier movement, casualty evacuation). Skills degrade fast without rehearsal; the manual treats knot tying, belay, and self-arrest as perishable skills requiring regular re-validation. Candidates for formal mountain training need baseline fitness, no fear-of-heights disqualifier, and demonstrated proficiency at each level before advancing.

## 3. Environmental Injuries and Altitude — The Body Fails First

### Cold injuries

Cold injuries split into freezing (frostbite) and nonfreezing (hypothermia, chilblain, immersion/trench foot). All are preventable; all are worsened by fatigue, dehydration, wet clothing, wind, and tobacco (nicotine constricts blood vessels and raises frostbite risk).

- **Hypothermia** is core temperature falling faster than the body can produce heat. It can occur on moderate days — wet plus wind plus exhaustion is enough. Classified mild above 90°F (32°C) core, severe below. Signs progress from shivering and clumsiness to confusion, slurred speech, loss of shivering, and unconsciousness. The field test in the manual: open the victim's clothing and feel the stomach — if the trunk feels cold, suspect hypothermia even if the limbs feel warm. Prevention is the buddy system (watch each other), dry layers, calories, and rest. Treatment: stop heat loss first — shelter, dry clothing, insulation from the ground, warm sweet drinks if conscious — then evacuate. Never give alcohol.
- **Frostbite** is tissue freezing. Superficial frostbite affects skin only (white to grayish, hard surface, soft tissue beneath); deep frostbite extends into muscle and bone. Early signs: cold, pain, burning, then numbness. Buddies check each other's faces, ears, fingers, and toes. Prevention: proper clothing, no tight boots or gloves, keep moving, no bare metal contact. Treatment: rewarm only when refreezing is impossible (a thawed foot that refreezes is worse than one left frozen), handle gently, no rubbing with snow, evacuate.
- **Immersion foot / trench foot** comes from prolonged wet, cold feet — even above freezing. Keep feet dry, change socks, air feet at halts.

### Altitude illness

Acclimatization cannot be shortened. The schedule governs the mission, not the reverse.

- **Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS):** headache (the most noticeable symptom, sometimes severe), loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, poor sleep, fatigue. Symptoms develop and peak by day two and run a 5–7 day course. Treatment: rest, small frequent liquids and light food, ibuprofen or aspirin, deep breathing, acetazolamide in the first 24 hours for mild-to-moderate cases, and descent of at least 1,000 feet if symptoms persist. AMS itself is nonfatal — but ascending through it invites HAPE or HACE.
- **High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE):** fluid in the lungs, possible above 8,000 feet, can kill healthy soldiers. Warning signs: progressive dry cough with frothy white or pink sputum, breathlessness far worse than companions', rapid pulse (120–160 bpm), crackling lung sounds, cyanosis (blue face, hands, feet), and deterioration that can be sudden. Prevention: gradual ascent — **no more than 1,000–2,000 feet of sleeping-altitude gain per day, with a rest day every 3,000 feet gained.** Treatment is descent: **2,000–3,000 feet minimum, immediately** — a 300-meter (1,000-foot) drop can help, but do not delay a foot descent waiting for air evacuation. Rest, warmth, oxygen if available, litter evacuation.
- **High-Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE):** fluid in the brain, also above 8,000 feet after rapid ascent. Main symptom is severe headache plus any neurological sign: personality change, loss of coordination, inability to concentrate, vomiting, decreased urination, paralysis, stupor, convulsions, coma. HACE and HAPE can strike experienced, well-acclimatized mountaineers without warning. **Immediate descent is mandatory at the first symptom.** Both edemas can be fatal within hours.

**Field rule:** any casualty who is worse than their companions at the same altitude, or who deteriorates after arrival, descends. Do not debate it.

## 4. Sustainment — Calories, Water, Clothing, Load

Mountain operations burn 4,500–6,000 calories per soldier per day. Carbohydrates provide about 4 kcal/g, protein about 4 kcal/g, fat about 9 kcal/g — and fat and protein demand more water and oxygen to digest, so carbohydrate-heavy rations suit high altitude. Dehydration is the quiet killer: cold suppresses thirst, exertion and altitude increase water loss, and melting snow costs fuel and time. Plan water procurement (streams, snow melt, resupply) as a mission task, not an afterthought, and treat all mountain water.

- **Clothing** follows the layered system; the manual details the Extended Cold-Weather Clothing System (ECWCS) with a modular glove system. Rules: no cotton next to skin, vent before you sweat, add layers at halts before you get cold, protect extremities, and keep one dry set in reserve. Wet clothing in wind is a hypothermia machine.
- **Soldier load management:** every pound matters more at altitude. The manual ties load to mobility: plan loads by terrain level, distribute team equipment (ropes, anchors, stoves) across the team, and cache or aerially resupply where possible rather than carrying everything.
- **Bivouac:** site selection is a survival skill — sheltered from wind and avalanche paths, near water and wood if available, with drainage and a flat sleeping area. Shelter options run from poncho lean-tos and 10-man arctic tents to snow caves (excellent insulation, but they demand time, tools, and ventilation to avoid carbon-monoxide and collapse hazards). Lightweight stoves are mission-critical in snow country: they melt water and cook, but they need ventilation, fuel discipline, and a windscreen. On glaciers, bivouac procedures add crevasse awareness — probe the site, rope up, mark the perimeter.

## 5. Avalanches — The Slope Decides

Most avalanche victims trigger the avalanche that kills them. The manual's avalanche chapter is built around three judgments: terrain, snowpack, and people.

- **Slope angle is the master variable.** Most large avalanches occur on slopes between **30° and 45°**. Slopes above 60° usually shed snow too often to build a slab. Slopes under 30° rarely slide — but they can be runout zones for slides from above. Measure slope angle; do not estimate it by eye on a white slope.
- **Avalanche problems:** loose-dry (point-release sluffs, fan-shaped, can trigger slabs, dangerous in terrain traps), storm slab (new snow, lasts hours to days), wind slab (lee and cross-loaded terrain, smooth rounded deposits, sometimes hollow-sounding), persistent slab (weak layer buried for days to months, triggers from light loads, propagates unpredictably — manage with wide safety buffers and conservative terrain choice), deep persistent slab (thick hard slab on a deep weak layer, hard to trigger but catastrophic), wet-loose and wet slab (spring warming, travel when the snow surface is cold and strong).
- **Terrain traps** multiply consequences: cliffs, gullies, creeks, lakes, trees, benches, roads, crevasses, and bergschrunds — anywhere debris piles deep or trauma is likely. A small slide into a terrain trap kills as surely as a big one on open ground.
- **Risk management:** unit leaders own the avalanche decision; every mountaineer owns the observation. Check the forecast, dig and read the snowpack, watch for natural avalanches (if naturals are running, human-triggered slides are likely), travel one at a time through suspect terrain, space out, keep escape routes in mind, and carry beacon, probe, and shovel — and know how to use them as a team.
- **Rescue:** the buried victim's survival clock is measured in minutes. The manual's sequence is signal search → coarse search → fine search → probing → shoveling (strategic shoveling, moving snow downhill and away, not just digging a hole). Group checks of beacons before entering avalanche terrain are mandatory. Practice until the sequence is muscle memory; a real burial is the worst possible first rehearsal.

## 6. Mountain Mobility — Walk, Climb, Cross Snow and Ice

### Individual movement

- **Mountain walking:** short steps, flat-foot placement on loose ground, the **rest step** (lock the rear leg straight briefly with each step so the skeleton, not the muscle, carries the weight — this is what lets a soldier climb all day), pressure breathing (rhythmic deep breathing matched to pace), and rest-step pacing on ascents. Descend with bent knees, short steps, and controlled speed — most mountain injuries happen on the way down.
- **Rock climbing:** balance over strength. Footwork first: edging, smearing, jamming (hand, fist, finger, and foot jams in cracks), stemming in dihedrals and chimneys, mantling onto ledges, liebacking, underclinging. Hands are for balance; feet do the work. Down-climbing is a separate skill — practice it, because retreat is often down, not up. Helmets always; rockfall is a constant hazard, and the climber above is the main source.
- **Snow and ice:** crampons (flat-footing on low-angle snow, front-pointing on steep ice), ice axe as walking aid, anchor, and self-arrest tool, step-cutting when crampons are absent, glissading only on known, safe slopes (never toward a cliff, crevasse, or rock band). **Self-arrest** must be reflexive: axe across the chest, pick into the snow, feet up and away from the slope, practiced until it works from any falling position. Alpine touring skis, snowshoes, and ahkio sleds extend mobility for the unit, each with its own training burden.
- **Ice climbing:** two tools, front-pointing, body position close to the ice, deliberate placements — waterfall ice and alpine ice behave differently, and ice quality changes with temperature through the day.

### Load management for mobility

Plan the load to the pitch: what you carry on a scramble is not what you carry on a vertical pitch. Rope, rack, and hardware go on the climbers; sustainment goes on the support element. The manual's planning rule is to decide load by movement technique (scrambling, climbing, fixed rope) and to stage equipment at the base of technical sections rather than hauling it through them.

## 7. Navigation in Mountains — Trust Instruments, Verify Constantly

Mountain navigation defeats map-and-compass alone: magnetic anomalies near iron-rich rock, featureless snowfields, whiteout, and the foreshortening that makes distances lie.

- **Equipment:** map, compass, GPS, altimeter (barometric — recalibrate at known elevations, since pressure changes masquerade as altitude changes), and a route card with checkpoints, bearings, distances, and times.
- **Techniques:** terrain association, resection, dead reckoning with pace count adjusted for slope (uphill pace is shorter; downhill pace is longer and less regular), and contour interpretation — in mountains, contour spacing *is* the route plan.
- **Route planning:** plan by legs between identifiable checkpoints, with an azimuth and a back-azimuth for every leg, escape routes, and rally points. In technical terrain, route finding becomes a climbing problem: reconnoiter the pitch, identify the line, and rope up before you need the rope.
- **Technical navigation:** roped movement techniques on exposed ground, route finding through cliff bands and glaciers, and the discipline of turning around when the route does not go — the mountain will still be there tomorrow; the unit may not be.

## 8. Knots, Rope, Anchors — The Technical Core

### Rope, webbing, carabiners

Kernmantle rope is the standard: a braided sheath over a core of load-bearing fibers. Know your rope's diameter, length, and condition; a rope's history matters — falls, abrasion, chemical exposure, and UV all retire it. Webbing (tubular and flat) and sewn slings build anchors and extend placements; carabiners are rated on their major axis only — never load a carabiner across its gate or on its minor axis, and keep the gate closed and locked under load.

### Knot doctrine

The manual teaches a family-based system. Key knots and their jobs:

- **Figure-eight family:** figure eight on a bight (primary tie-in and anchor loop), rerouted figure eight (tie-in to harness), two-loop figure eight, directional figure eight, figure-eight bend (joining two ropes), inline figure eight. The figure eight retains roughly **75–80% of rope strength** — the strongest commonly used knot family in the manual.
- **Overhand family:** overhand, water knot (joining webbing — dress it carefully and leave long tails), overhand on a bight.
- **Friction hitches:** Prusik (the workhorse — grips when loaded, slides when unloaded; used for ascending, as a backup, and in hauling systems), Klemheist, autoblock. Prusik cord is smaller-diameter than the main rope by rule; a Prusik that is too thick will not grip.
- **Hitches:** clove hitch (adjustable anchor attachment), girth hitch, Munter hitch (a belay and rappel hitch that works with a single carabiner — no device needed), Munter mule (a releasable tie-off of a Munter), tensionless hitch / high-strength tie-off (wrapping the rope around a tree — retains near-full rope strength).
- **Bends and special knots:** bowline (a fixed loop, but it can work loose — back it up), butterfly (mid-rope loop that loads in any direction), double fisherman's (joining cord for Prusik loops).

**Knot rules that never change:** dress every knot (align the strands neatly), set it (pull it tight), leave adequate tails, and inspect before loading. A sloppy knot is a failed knot that has not been loaded yet. The manual's inspection standard is that any knot you cannot verify by sight and feel gets retied.

### Rope care

Inspect before and after every use: run the rope through your hands feeling for soft spots, flat spots, cuts, and sheath damage. Coil properly (butterfly coil or mountaineer's coil), keep rope out of dirt, chemicals, and vehicle exhaust, dry it before storage, and retire rope that has held a factor-2 fall or shows core damage.

### Anchors

Anchors are built to the standard of **redundancy and equalization**: a single-point anchor is acceptable only when the point is bombproof (a massive tree, a huge boulder, a solid rock tunnel); otherwise build multipoint anchors that share the load and survive the failure of any one point (the SERENE/ERNEST principles in modern terms — solid, equalized, redundant, no extension).

- **Trees:** the strongest natural anchor. Use the tensionless hitch for near-full strength; pad the rope against sharp bark edges; multipoint tree anchors for marginal trees.
- **Rock:** natural (boulders, protrusions, chockstones, tunnels, arches, pinch points) and artificial (pitons, bolts, chocks/nuts, camming devices). Each placement must be tested — tug it, bounce it, and if it moves, it does not count. Pitons are driven, not hammered into submission; bolts are the most reliable artificial anchor but require drilling and are not an on-the-move option; cams work in parallel-sided cracks, chocks in tapering ones.
- **Ice:** ice screws (the standard, placed at an angle to the fall line), V-threads (Abalakov threads — two intersecting holes threaded with cord, surprisingly strong and retrievable), ice pillars and bollards.
- **Earth and snow:** deadman anchors (buried skis, packs, or pickets), picket holdfasts (driven at an angle, often in series), snow bollards (a carved horseshoe trench the rope wraps around), and buried ice axes. Snow anchors are the weakest class — their strength depends entirely on snow consolidation, and they must be load-tested.

## 9. Rope Systems — Belay, Lead, Fixed Rope, Rappel

### Belayed climbing

Top-rope and lead climbing differ in one decisive way: in a top-rope, the anchor is above the climber and a fall is short; in a lead, the climber places protection as they go and a fall can be twice the distance to the last piece. The manual's operating rules:

- **Top rope:** bombproof anchor, belayer anchored or braced, climbing commands standardized and called every time ("On belay?" / "Belay on" / "Climbing" / "Climb on" / "Slack" / "Tension" / "Off belay" / "Belay off" — and their equivalents; the point is that both parties use the same words every time).
- **Lead climbing:** the leader places protection, clips the rope, and manages rope drag; the belayer feeds slack smoothly and catches falls dynamically. The belay device or Munter hitch must be second nature — hesitation in a fall is the accident.
- **Body belays** (hip, shoulder) are taught as field-expedient techniques when no device exists; they work, but they demand a braced stance and a gloved hand, and they are a backup, not a preference.
- **Team of three:** caterpillar, end roping, parallel, and fixed strands — methods for moving three climbers on one rope system without leaving anyone unprotected.

### Fixed ropes and rappelling

Fixed ropes let a whole unit move over ground only a climber could cross. Simple fixed ropes for moderate slopes; fixed ropes with intermediate anchors for long or broken pitches; vertical fixed ropes for cliffs. Ascend with Prusiks or mechanical ascenders; descend by rappel.

- **Rappelling fundamentals:** anchor, rappel device or Munter hitch, backup Prusik below the device, gloves, helmet, and a checked system before committing weight. The manual teaches multiple rappel techniques (seated, body, Australian, with and without device) and the transitions — ascending to descending and back — because real operations require changing direction mid-rope.
- **Safety:** the most dangerous moment is the transition onto and off the rope. Check the anchor, check the device, check the backup, then commit. A rappel that begins with an unchecked anchor is a fall with extra steps.

## 10. Hauling, Lowering, Stream Crossing, Glaciers, Rescue, Rope Bridges

### Hauling and lowering

Mechanical advantage systems move casualties and heavy loads up cliffs. Know the math: a 1:1 system moves the load one meter per meter pulled; a 3:1 (Z-drag) moves it one meter per three pulled, at one-third the force (minus friction); a 5:1 and compound systems trade speed for force. Components: pulleys, Prusiks as progress captures, anchor, and haul line. Reduce friction (pulleys at every bend) or the theoretical advantage evaporates. Lowering is the mirror image: create friction (Munter hitch, figure-eight device, brake bars) and a hands-free backup (a friction hitch on the brake strand) so a slipped grip does not become a dropped load. Passing a knot through a hauling or lowering system is a rehearsed skill, not an improvisation.

### Stream crossing

Reconnoiter first: depth, current, bottom, and the downstream hazard (what happens if someone falls in). Individual crossings use a staff and a braced stance facing upstream; team crossings link arms or use a rope; belayed crossings use a fixed line with the crosser clipped in. Swimming is the last resort. Safety rules: never cross above a waterfall, strainer, or rapid you cannot survive; loosen pack straps so the pack can be shed; cross in the morning when glacial streams run lowest; and treat a mountain stream in spate as an obstacle to go around, not a challenge to beat.

### Glacier travel and crevasse rescue

Glaciers hide crevasses under snow bridges that can collapse under a boot. Travel roped, spaced so one person is never on the same snow bridge as the next, probing suspect ground, crossing crevasses perpendicular to their run, and moving in the cold of morning when bridges are strongest. On a crevasse fall: the team arrests the fall, builds an anchor, and transfers the load — then either the victim Prusiks out, or the team hauls them out with a mechanical advantage system. The manual's crevasse-rescue sequence (arrest → anchor → transfer → communicate → extract) is a drill to rehearse on flat ground before it is needed on ice.

### Mountain rescue and evacuation

Rescue is planned in two modes: deliberate (time to plan, equipment on hand) and improvised (what you have, right now). General steps: secure the scene, assess the casualty, stabilize, choose the movement technique, and move — with prolonged field care if evacuation is delayed. Casualty movement without litters (fireman's carry, pack-strap drag, two-person carries) is slow and exhausting; litters, even improvised ones, are almost always worth building. Low-angle rescue uses simple lowers and drags; high-angle rescue is a full rope system with lowering and hauling. The manual is blunt: a rescue that creates a second casualty has failed.

### Rope bridges

One-rope, two-rope, and three-rope bridges cross gaps too wide or too dangerous to wade or climb. Construction: anchor both sides (bombproof, redundant), tension the bridge rope, and build in redundancy so a single failure does not drop the crosser. Crossing methods depend on the bridge type — commando crawl, sitting, or clipped traverse — and retrieval matters: plan how the last person and the rope get across before the first person starts.

## Output Contract

When asked about a mountain task, answer with the manual's progression: (1) name the terrain level and the governing hazard (altitude, cold, avalanche, fall, water); (2) give the procedure as a numbered sequence with the numbers that matter (altitudes, angles, calories, distances); (3) state the safety check or abort criterion; (4) cite the chapter. Never recommend a technique above the user's stated training level without flagging the gap and the training needed. If the user asks for a knot, anchor, or rope system, name the knot/anchor, its use, its strength retention, and its failure mode.

## Operating Rules

1. **The mountain sets the schedule.** Acclimatization, weather, and snowpack are not negotiable; a plan that fights them is a casualty report waiting to be written.
2. **Progression before performance.** Level 1 skills before Level 2 tasks; rehearsed drills before real rope work.
3. **Redundancy in everything that holds a life.** Two anchors, two points, a backup Prusik, a checked knot.
4. **Descend on doubt.** AMS, HAPE, HACE, bad weather, suspect snow — the descent is the treatment, the abort, and the rescue all at once.
5. **The buddy system is a system, not a slogan.** Check each other for cold injury, altitude symptoms, and knot errors.
6. **Numbers over impressions.** Slope angle measured, altitude gained per day counted, calories and water planned — never eyeballed.
