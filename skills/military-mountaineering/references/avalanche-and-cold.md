## Avalanche problems table

| Problem | What it is | Duration / trigger | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose-dry (sluff) | Dry unconsolidated snow, point release, fan-shaped | Minutes; surface layers | Dangerous in terrain traps; can trigger slabs |
| Storm slab | Soft cohesive layer of new snow | Hours to days; stabilizes quickly | Wait it out or avoid fresh loading |
| Wind slab | Wind-deposited cohesive layer, lee/cross-loaded, smooth, sometimes hollow-sounding | Days; wind events | Stick to sheltered or wind-scoured ground |
| Persistent slab | Slab over a buried persistent weak layer | Days to months; light loads trigger it | Wide safety buffers; conservative terrain choice |
| Deep persistent slab | Thick hard slab over a deep weak layer | Months to a season; hard to trigger, catastrophic | Avoid the terrain entirely when present |
| Wet-loose / wet slab | Wet unconsolidated snow or slab | Spring warming; daily cycle | Travel when the snow surface is cold and strong (early morning) |

## Slope angle — the master variable

- **30°–45°:** most large avalanches occur here.
- **Above 60°:** snow usually sluffs off before a slab can build.
- **Below 30°:** rarely slides, but can be a runout zone for slides from above.
- Measure slope angle with an inclinometer or poles; never estimate by eye on a uniform white slope.

## Terrain traps (consequences multipliers)

Cliffs, gullies, creeks, lakes, trees, benches, roads, crevasses, bergschrunds — anywhere debris piles deep or trauma is likely. A small slide into a terrain trap kills as surely as a large one on open ground. Route planning must identify and avoid traps, not just avalanche start zones.

## Avalanche search and rescue sequence

1. **Group check** before entering avalanche terrain: every beacon transmitting, every beacon receiving, probes and shovels carried and accessible.
2. **Signal search:** follow the flux line of the victim's beacon signal; move fast along the fall line until a signal is acquired.
3. **Coarse search:** bracket the signal along the fall line to find the strongest reading.
4. **Fine search:** grid the last meters at low height above the snow until the minimum distance reading.
5. **Probing:** probe in a spiral/concentric pattern at close spacing; a strike is a soft, spongy resistance distinct from rock or ice.
6. **Shoveling:** strategic shoveling — start downhill of the probe strike and move snow away in a trench, not straight down a hole. Speed matters: survival odds fall steeply after ~15 minutes of burial.
7. **First aid and evacuation:** treat for asphyxia, hypothermia, and trauma; the buried victim is a casualty the moment they are uncovered.

## Cold-injury table

| Injury | Mechanism | Signs | Field treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypothermia (mild, core >90°F / 32°C) | Heat loss outpaces production; wet + wind + exhaustion | Shivering, clumsiness, confusion | Stop heat loss: shelter, dry layers, insulation from ground, warm sweet drinks if conscious |
| Hypothermia (severe, core <90°F) | Continued exposure | Loss of shivering, slurred speech, unconsciousness | Handle gently, insulate, evacuate urgently; never give alcohol |
| Frostbite, superficial | Skin freezes | White to grayish, hard surface, soft tissue beneath | Rewarm only if refreezing is impossible; no rubbing with snow |
| Frostbite, deep | Freezing extends to muscle/bone | Hard, pale, numb, loss of sensation | Same; gentle handling; evacuate |
| Immersion/trench foot | Prolonged wet, cold feet above freezing | Numbness, swelling, pain on rewarming | Dry feet, change socks, air feet at halts |

## Altitude-illness table

| Illness | Altitude | Key signs | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| AMS | Any, worsens with altitude | Headache (most noticeable), nausea, appetite loss, vomiting, poor sleep; peaks day 2, runs 5–7 days | Rest, fluids, light food, ibuprofen/aspirin, acetazolamide first 24 h; descend ≥1,000 ft if persistent |
| HAPE | Over 8,000 ft | Frothy white/pink sputum, breathlessness worse than companions', pulse 120–160, crackling lungs, cyanosis | **Descend 2,000–3,000 ft immediately**; rest, warmth, oxygen; litter evac |
| HACE | Over 8,000 ft | Severe headache + personality change, loss of coordination, confusion, vomiting, stupor, convulsions, coma | **Immediate descent mandatory**; can be fatal within hours |

## Acclimatization rules

- **No more than 1,000–2,000 ft of sleeping-altitude gain per day.**
- **One rest day (no altitude gain, no heavy exertion) per 3,000 ft gained.**
- Acclimatization cannot be shortened; a schedule that ignores it produces casualties, not speed.
- Anyone worse than their companions at the same altitude descends — no debate.

## Cold-injury prevention checklist

- [ ] Buddy system: check faces, ears, fingers, toes at every halt
- [ ] No cotton next to skin; vent before sweating; add layers at halts
- [ ] Keep one dry clothing set in reserve
- [ ] Eat and drink on schedule — fatigue and dehydration drive hypothermia
- [ ] No tobacco (vasoconstriction raises frostbite risk); no alcohol
- [ ] Loosen tight boots/gloves; keep feet dry, change socks
- [ ] Never touch bare metal with bare skin in extreme cold
