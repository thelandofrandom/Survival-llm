# Navigation and Bivouac — Reference (TC 3-97.61, Chapters 4 and 7)

Distilled from TC 3-97.61 *Military Mountaineering* (Army, Nov 2025). Paraphrased; short quotes only.

## Chapter outline

- Ch. 7 §I — Military land navigation in nontechnical mountainous terrain: equipment, techniques, route planning.
- Ch. 7 §II — Navigation in technical mountainous terrain: reconnaissance, roped movement, route finding.
- Ch. 4 §II–IV — Cold-weather sustainment, clothing, small-group sustainment, load management, water, aerial resupply, bivouac.

## Navigation equipment checklist

- [ ] Map (correct scale, protected from weather) and protractor
- [ ] Compass (and a spare; check for magnetic interference near iron-rich rock)
- [ ] GPS with spare batteries — a tool, not a plan
- [ ] Barometric altimeter — recalibrate at every known elevation; pressure changes masquerade as altitude changes
- [ ] Route card (see below) and notebook/pencil
- [ ] Watch for timing legs

## Navigation techniques

| Technique | When to use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Terrain association | Features visible | In mountains, contour spacing *is* the route plan; ridgelines and drainages are handrails |
| Resection | Need to fix position | Two or three bearings to known features; in featureless snow, resection fails — use dead reckoning |
| Dead reckoning | Whiteout, fog, snowfields | Pace count adjusted for slope; compass bearing held rigorously; drift compounds fast |
| Altimeter navigation | Contour ambiguity | Follow a contour line at a known elevation to a checkpoint; recalibrate often |
| Back-azimuth | Every leg | Always record the return bearing before leaving a checkpoint |

## Pace-count adjustments

- Uphill pace is shorter; downhill pace is longer and less regular.
- On steep ground, count steps in hundreds and re-measure at every checkpoint rather than trusting a flat-ground pace count.
- In deep snow or scree, pace count is unreliable — use time and altimeter instead.

## Route card format

For each leg, record: leg number, from → to checkpoint, grid coordinates, magnetic azimuth, back-azimuth, distance, expected time, elevation change, terrain notes, escape route, and rally point. The route card is the plan the team navigates by when visibility dies; write it before moving, not during.

## Route planning rules

1. Plan by legs between identifiable checkpoints — never plan a single long leg across featureless ground.
2. Plan for the elevation you will occupy: weather, temperature, and wind at altitude, not in the valley.
3. Be off exposed ridges and summits before afternoon convection builds (lightning).
4. Identify escape routes and rally points for every leg.
5. In technical terrain, route finding is a climbing problem: reconnoiter the pitch, identify the line, rope up *before* you need the rope.
6. The turn-around decision is made before departure: define the condition (time, weather, snow, fatigue) that triggers it, and honor it.

## Sustainment numbers

- **4,500–6,000 calories per soldier per day** in mountain operations.
- Carbohydrates ~4 kcal/g, protein ~4 kcal/g, fat ~9 kcal/g. Fat and protein need more water and oxygen to digest — carbohydrate-heavy rations suit altitude.
- Water: cold suppresses thirst while exertion and altitude raise loss. Plan water procurement (streams, snow melt, resupply) as a mission task. Treat all mountain water.
- Melting snow costs fuel and time — budget both.

## Clothing rules (ECWCS layered system)

- No cotton next to skin; moisture-wicking base layer, insulating mid layer, wind/waterproof shell.
- Vent before you sweat; add layers at halts *before* you get cold.
- Modular glove system: thin liners for dexterity, insulated gloves for warmth, shells for wind/wet.
- Protect extremities first — head, hands, feet lose heat fastest.
- Keep one dry set in reserve at all times.

## Bivouac site selection checklist

- [ ] Sheltered from prevailing wind and spindrift
- [ ] Not in an avalanche path, runout zone, or below a cornice
- [ ] Not in a dry wash or flood-prone gully
- [ ] Near water and fuel (wood) if available
- [ ] Flat, drained ground for sleeping; clear of rockfall lines
- [ ] On glaciers: probe the site for crevasses, rope up, mark the perimeter
- [ ] Concealment from observation if tactically required

## Shelter options

| Shelter | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Poncho lean-to | Hasty halt, mild conditions | Fast, minimal warmth |
| 10-man arctic tent (floorless) | Unit bivouac in snow | Stove-compatible with ventilation |
| Snow cave | Storm survival, deep snow | Excellent insulation; needs time, tools, ventilation (CO and collapse risk) |
| Snow trench / wind wall | Emergency overnight | Quick, better than exposed ground |

## Lightweight stove rules

- Stoves are mission-critical in snow country: they melt water and cook.
- Always ventilate — carbon monoxide kills in enclosed snow shelters.
- Use a windscreen; budget fuel for snow melting, not just cooking.
- Never sleep with a stove running in a closed shelter.
