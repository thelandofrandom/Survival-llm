## Dead reckoning

Navigate by compass bearing and pace count: shoot an azimuth to the destination, walk it, count paces to know when you should arrive. Works in any visibility and any terrain; the only method that works in featureless ground (desert, flat forest, fog). Weakness: cumulative error — small mistakes in bearing or pace add up, so check position at every opportunity.

## Terrain association

Match the ground you see to the map: identify the terrain features around you (hills, valleys, streams, roads, buildings) and your position relative to them. Faster and more accurate than dead reckoning in distinctive terrain; useless in featureless terrain.

- **Handrail:** a linear feature you follow (river, ridge, road).
- **Attack point:** an obvious feature near the destination from which you navigate the final leg.
- **Catching feature:** a feature beyond the destination that tells you you have gone too far.

## Resection, intersection, and related methods

- **Resection:** find your own position by shooting azimuths to two or more known features, converting to back azimuths, and plotting them — where the lines cross is where you are. Needs two widely separated features for accuracy.
- **Intersection:** locate a distant unknown point by shooting azimuths to it from two known positions.
- **Modified resection:** one known feature plus a known direction or distance (e.g., a single azimuth plus a road you know you are on).
- **Polar coordinates:** locate a point by azimuth and distance from a known position.

## Combining methods

Use dead reckoning and terrain association together: dead reckoning keeps you on a bearing, terrain association confirms where you are. When they disagree, stop — the disagreement is information, and walking on is how people get lost.

## Elevation and relief

Ridges, valleys, saddles, depressions, draws, and spurs each have a signature contour shape. A saddle is the low point between two hills and the high point between two valleys — the natural crossing point. A draw is a shallow valley that may hold water and cover. Know the ground from the map before your feet find it the hard way.

## Terrain association (field skill)

Orient the map (align it with the terrain, using the compass or by matching features), identify five major terrain features, and confirm your position before moving. Training is progressive: obvious terrain, then subtle terrain, then night and bad weather.

## Mounted land navigation

The driver drives; the navigator navigates — never combine the jobs. Use the odometer for distance, the compass for direction, the map for position; confirm at every stop. Vehicle navigation is faster but coarser than foot navigation, and the error per kilometer is larger.

## Navigation by terrain type

| Terrain | Key points |
|---|---|
| Desert | Few features — dead reckoning and resection dominate; mirages and heat distort distance |
| Jungle | Limited visibility — short legs, frequent checks; pace count unreliable in dense vegetation |
| Mountains | Contours are distinctive, but weather closes in fast |
| Arctic | Whiteout destroys terrain association — dead reckoning and GPS only |
| Urban | Streets are handrails, but street names and maps may be wrong or outdated |
