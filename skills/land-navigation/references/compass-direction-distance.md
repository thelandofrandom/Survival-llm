# Compass, Direction, Distance, and Pace Count

Source: FM 3-25.26, Chapters 5 (Scale and Distance) and 6 (Direction).

## Chapter outline

- Ch. 5: Scale and distance — measuring map distance, pace count
- Ch. 6: Direction — azimuths, the lensatic compass, declination diagram, converting azimuths, field-expedient direction finding

## The lensatic compass

Standard military compass: floating dial, sighting wire, lens for reading the dial, bezel ring set to the desired azimuth. Hold level, let the dial settle, read the azimuth under the index line. Luminous marks allow walking a bearing at night. Keep the compass away from metal objects, vehicles, power lines, and weapons — all deflect the needle.

## Azimuths and back azimuths

An azimuth is a direction measured clockwise from north, 0°–360°. A **back azimuth** is the opposite direction: add 180° if the azimuth is under 180°, subtract 180° if it is over 180°. The back azimuth is how you return along a route and how you check your work — if the back azimuth from your destination does not point at your start, something is wrong.

## Three norths

- **True north:** the North Pole.
- **Grid north:** the map's grid lines.
- **Magnetic north:** where the compass points.

The declination diagram shows all three and the angles between them. Convert with the diagram's rule, not from memory — the sign of the correction varies by location and by sheet.

## Field-expedient direction finding

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Shadow stick | Mark a stick's shadow tip; wait 15–20 min; mark the new tip. Line between marks runs east–west; first mark is west. |
| Watch method (N. hemisphere) | Point the hour hand at the sun; halve the angle between the hour hand and 12 o'clock — that line points south. (Use 1 o'clock for daylight saving time.) |
| Polaris | The North Star sits almost directly above true north (northern hemisphere). |
| Southern Cross | Use in the southern hemisphere to find south. |
| Unreliable | Moss on trees — does not reliably grow on the north side. Do not trust it. |

## Measuring distance on the map

Use the graphic scale bar, a ruler, or the protractor's scale. On a 1:50,000 map, measure in centimeters and multiply by 500 for meters. For curved routes, lay a strip of paper or string along the route, then straighten it against the scale bar. Straight-line distance is always shorter than the distance you will walk — add for slope, vegetation, and detours.

## The pace count

- A **pace is one natural step, about 30 inches long**.
- Calibrate: walk a measured 100-meter course on flat terrain, count paces, repeat for a reliable average. The manual's method: walk a **600-meter course**, count the paces, divide by six for your 100-meter pace count.
- Record it on the map or in a notebook; mark every 100 meters with a knot in a string or a tick in the notebook.
- **What changes it:** slope, brush, fatigue, load, darkness. Rule of thumb: if it normally takes you **120 paces to walk 100 meters** on flat ground, allow more on difficult ground and recheck whenever the terrain changes. A pace count is personal — calibrate your own.

## Estimating distance by eye

Apparent size of known objects (a person ~1.7 m tall; a house ~8 m wide), clarity of detail at known ranges, and time × speed (about 4 km/h on flat ground, less on slopes or in brush). Combine methods — a single estimate is a guess; two that agree are a measurement.
