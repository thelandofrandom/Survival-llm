# Survival

## Purpose

ATP 3-50.21, *Survival* (U.S. Army, September 2018) — the Army's doctrine for staying alive, healthy, and free after an isolating event, until recovery by friendly forces. This skill is for anyone who may have to survive cut off from support: a soldier separated from their unit, a pilot downed in hostile or remote terrain, or a civilian facing a genuine backcountry or disaster survival situation. The end goal is always the same: return to friendly control with honor.

The manual's core idea is that survival is a system, not a bag of tricks. It rests on four proficiencies — **protection, sustenance, survival medicine, and navigation** — applied in priority order through the **survival pattern**, and it insists that the decisive factor is mental: "Having survival skills is important; having the will to survive is essential." Army forces are typically equipped and prepared to survive up to **96 hours** of isolation, subject to recovery-force capability, threat, and environment — but the skills here are built to stretch well beyond that.

Survival begins when a person executes their isolated-soldier guidance (ISG) or evasion plan of action (EPA). Everything in this skill is organized around what to do first, what kills fastest, and what can be improvised from almost nothing.

## 1. The Psychology of Survival — Will First

**1.1. Stress is a condition, not a disease.** Stress is the body's reaction to pressure — fight, flight, or freeze. It releases stored fuel, raises breathing rate, and tenses muscles. In moderate doses it sharpens performance; in excess it becomes distress and causes panic, forgetfulness, carelessness, angry outbursts, withdrawal, and thoughts of death. The survivor's job is to manage stress, not eliminate it.

**1.2. The SURVIVAL acronym (Figure 1-1).** The manual's memory tool for sound decisions under pressure:

- **S — Size up the situation.** Surroundings, physical condition, equipment. Think before acting.
- **U — Undue haste makes waste.** Panic-driven rushing wastes energy and creates mistakes.
- **R — Remember where you are.** Orient yourself; know your location relative to safety, water, and danger.
- **V — Vanquish fear and panic.** Fear is normal. Acknowledge it, then act on training.
- **I — Improvise.** Everything is a potential tool. Ordinary objects have second uses.
- **V — Value living.** The will to live is a decision. People who give up psychologically die long before their bodies fail.
- **A — Act like the natives.** Local people have already solved the problems of this environment — watch what they eat, wear, and avoid.
- **L — Learn basic skills.** Skills practiced in training become confidence in isolation.

**1.3. Common survival stressors and reactions.** Fear, anxiety, anger, frustration, guilt, depression, and loneliness are all normal reactions. Expect them; do not mistake them for failure. The countermeasures are routine and purpose: set small achievable goals, keep a schedule, stay busy with survival tasks, and maintain hope through a realistic plan for recovery. A survivor who is working a plan is far harder to break than one who is waiting passively.

**1.4. The survival pattern.** Priorities are set by what kills first, in this environment, right now. In all situations survival medicine has top priority; after that the order shifts with the environment. In cold, fire comes first (warmth), then shelter, then water, then food. In heat, water and shade come first. The pattern is: **survival medicine → protection (fire, shelter, clothing) → sustenance (water, food) → navigation**. Reassess constantly — the priority that was right at noon may be wrong at midnight. → `references/survival-pattern.md`

## 2. Survival Medicine — Remote, Improvised Care

**2.1. How survival medicine differs from normal medicine.** Care is remote and improvised, with limited resources, limited labor, and delayed evacuation. The four fundamentals are **prevention, recognition, mitigation, and treatment**. Prevention dominates: stay hydrated, stay warm and dry, keep feet sound, eat what you can, and avoid injury — because in isolation a small problem becomes a fatal one.

**2.2. Lifesaving steps (Chapter 2).** In order: open the airway and restore breathing (jaw-thrust method for a suspected neck injury; head-tilt/chin-lift otherwise), stop severe bleeding with direct pressure, pressure dressings, and pressure points, then treat for shock. A casualty in shock is kept warm, laid flat with legs elevated (unless head, chest, or abdominal injury forbids it), and given nothing to eat or drink if evacuation and surgery may follow. Splint fractures as found — do not straighten a deformed limb in the field. Treat heat and cold injuries early: cool a heat casualty in shade with water and airflow; warm a cold casualty gradually with insulation, warm liquids, and body heat — never rub frostbitten tissue and never thaw a frozen limb if it may refreeze.

**2.3. The body's pressure points.** When direct pressure fails, compress the artery upstream of the wound: brachial (upper arm, inside), femoral (groin), and others shown in Figure 2-3. A tourniquet is a last resort for extremity bleeding that cannot be controlled — wide band, at least 1.5 inches, with a windlass stick, tightened until bleeding stops, marked with the time applied, and never loosened in the field.

**2.4. Medicinal plants and natural remedies.** The manual catalogs plants with survival-medicine properties — for example, willow bark (salicin, a pain and fever reliever, chewed or brewed as tea), garlic and onion (antiseptic), and various astringents for diarrhea. Rules: use only plants you can positively identify, test a small amount first, and never use an unknown plant as medicine. Diarrhea and dysentery are among the most dangerous field illnesses because they accelerate dehydration; treat with rest, fluids, and astringent teas, and boil all water.

**2.5. Poisonous plants.** The danger signs: milky sap, almond scent, spines or fine hairs, umbrella-shaped flower clusters, beans or bulbs, and seeds inside pods. Avoid all mushrooms entirely — identification is too unreliable and the penalty is too high. Never eat a plant you cannot positively identify, and never test an unknown plant by eating a full portion. → `references/survival-medicine.md`

## 3. Water — The First Sustenance Priority

**3.1. How much you need.** At 68°F with limited activity, a person needs **2 to 3 quarts of water per day** to maintain efficiency. Daily losses run about 1.4 quarts in urine, 1.0 quart in sweat, and 0.2 quarts in fecal matter. In high heat, sweat loss can reach **3.5 quarts per hour**. A burn victim may lose up to **5 quarts per day**; vomiting and diarrhea increase losses further. Thirst is a late and unreliable indicator — by the time you feel very thirsty you are already dehydrated, and the thirst response weakens in cold weather, which is why cold-weather dehydration is common.

**3.2. Treat all surface water as contaminated.** Assume every stream, pond, and puddle carries human and animal waste, with the possible exception of very high mountain streams or springs in uninhabited areas. Never drink urine or alcohol — both accelerate dehydration. Never drink stagnant water from natural depressions; it is heavily contaminated.

**3.3. Purification — the numbers that matter.**

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Boiling (safest) | Rolling boil **1 minute at sea level; 3 minutes above 8,000 feet** |
| Iodine, 2% tincture | **5 drops per quart** (10 if water is cloudy or cold); wait 30 minutes |
| Povidone iodine, 10% military grade | **2 drops per quart**; civilian 2% solution needs 10 drops |
| Bleach | Per label/tablet directions; iodine and bleach do not reliably kill Cryptosporidium — boiling does |
| Filtration + purification | Filter sediment first (cloth, sand), then purify; clear water purifies far more reliably |

**3.4. Finding and collecting water.** Indicators: animal trails converging downhill, birds circling water at dawn and dusk, green vegetation in dry country, and insects (bees and mosquitoes rarely range far from water). Methods: rain catchers and bamboo catchers, transpiration bags tied over leafy branches, solar stills (a hole, a container, clear plastic sheeting, a stone weight — yields are small, so build several), dew collection with cloth at dawn, water from green bamboo sections, vines (cut high first, then low, and let it drip — avoid milky or bitter sap), banana stumps, and condensation traps. Always carry a container; an improvised one can be made from plastic bags or any non-porous material. → `references/water-food.md`

## 4. Food — Energy to Keep Moving

**4.1. Food considerations.** In a short survival episode (under a few days) water matters far more than food — a healthy person can go weeks without food but only days without water. Once water is secured, food restores energy, morale, and the ability to think clearly. The manual's priorities: plants first (lowest energy cost and risk), then traps and fishing (passive, working while you do other tasks), then active hunting (highest energy cost). Preserve what you catch — smoke, dry, or salt meat; cook everything thoroughly.

**4.2. The Universal Edibility Test.** When a plant cannot be positively identified, test it — one plant at a time, over **24 hours**, so any reaction can be traced to that plant:

1. Inspect: reject plants with milky sap, almond smell, spines, umbrella flower clusters, beans, bulbs, or seeds in pods. Avoid all mushrooms.
2. Smell: crush a small part; reject if it smells of almonds or peaches (cyanide compounds).
3. Skin test: rub on the inside of the elbow or wrist; wait 15 minutes; reject if it itches, burns, or reddens.
4. Lip test: touch to the lips; wait 15 minutes.
5. Tongue test: hold a small piece on the tongue; wait 15 minutes; do not swallow.
6. Chew and spit: chew a small piece, spit it out; wait 15 minutes.
7. Swallow a small amount; wait 8 hours; eat nothing else.
8. If no symptoms after 8 hours, eat a larger portion; wait another 8 hours before declaring it safe.

**4.3. Plants worth knowing.** The manual lists the most common globally available edible plants (cattail, dandelion, plantain, wild onion and garlic, clover, acorns — leach acorns in water to remove bitter tannins before eating — and others), with preparation notes. General rules: cook leaves to reduce bitterness and toxins; eat roots and tubers cooked; never eat large quantities of any new plant at once.

**4.4. Traps, snares, and fishing.** Passive food gathering beats active hunting on energy. Snares: simple loop, Apache foot snare, drag noose, twitch-up, treadle spring. Deadfalls: figure-4 and Paiute. Set snares on game trails, at burrow entrances, and near water; check them at dawn and dusk; camouflage with local materials and avoid leaving human scent (handle with gloves or smoke). Fish with improvised hooks (bone, thorn, bent wire), gill nets, bottle traps, and weirs; fish are most active at dawn and dusk. Butchering: bleed, skin, gut, and cook promptly; save fat; boil bones for broth; never eat raw freshwater fish or meat.

**4.5. Insects and other protein.** Grasshoppers, crickets, grubs, and ants are safe when cooked and are rich in protein — remove legs, wings, and stingers first. Avoid brightly colored insects, hairy caterpillars, and anything that stings or bites. → `references/water-food.md`

## 5. Fire — Warmth, Water, Food, Signal

**5.1. Fire basics.** Fire needs three things — heat, fuel, and oxygen — and fails if any one is missing. Build in a sheltered spot, clear a circle down to mineral soil, and keep a fuel pile ready before you light anything. The fire lay goes tinder → kindling → fuel: tinder is the finest, driest material (shredded bark, dry grass, char cloth, bird down); kindling is pencil-thick sticks; fuel is wrist-thick and larger. The tepee lay lights fastest; the log-cabin and pyramid lays burn longer and steadier; a star fire conserves fuel by feeding sticks in from the ends.

**5.2. Starting fire without matches.** Methods in order of reliability: lighter or matches (carry them — this is not the time for pride), fire steel / ferrocerium rod with char cloth or fine tinder, magnifying lens on a sunny day, battery and steel wool, flint and steel, then friction methods — fire plow, hand drill, and bow drill (the most reliable friction method; softwood hearth board, hardwood spindle, a bow, and a bearing block). Friction fire demands dry wood, correct technique, and patience; practice it before you need it. Keep a coal alive: carry embers in a bundle of dry tinder inside a container, or bank the fire under ash overnight.

**5.3. Fire as tool.** Fire purifies water, cooks food, dries clothing, warms the shelter, keeps animals away, and signals rescuers (three fires in a triangle is the international distress signal; smoke by day, flame by night; add green boughs or rubber for thick smoke). Never build a fire you cannot control, and never leave a fire burning in a shelter while you sleep unless it is built for it with ventilation. → `references/fire-shelter-navigation-knots.md`

## 6. Shelter and Clothing — Protection from the Elements

**6.1. Shelter site selection.** High, dry ground; near water and firewood but not in a flood channel or dry wash; protected from wind; away from dead standing trees (widow-makers) and insect nests. In the desert, go below ground — a below-ground shelter can be 30°F cooler than the surface. In snow country, a tree-pit or snow cave uses snow itself as insulation.

**6.2. Shelter types.** Naturally occurring first: caves, rock overhangs, hollow trees. Then improvised: poncho lean-to (quick, one-person), A-frame, debris hut (a ridgepole piled with leaves and debris at least 2 feet thick — warm, slow to build, worth it in cold), raised platform shelter (wet jungle), tree-pit snow shelter, and field-expedient lean-to with a fire reflector wall that bounces heat back at you. A shelter need only be big enough to lie in — extra volume is extra air to heat. Insulate yourself from the ground first; the ground steals more heat than the air.

**6.3. Clothing.** Dress in layers, keep dry, and protect the head — a large share of body heat is lost through an uncovered head. In cold, wear loose layers that trap air; in heat, wear loose, light-colored clothing that covers skin and a hat against sun. Dry wet clothing by fire whenever possible; wet clothing loses most of its insulating value and accelerates hypothermia. Repair and improvise: stuff dry grass, leaves, or newspaper between layers for insulation; make footgear from bark, hide, or cloth wrapped and tied.

**6.4. Cold and heat injuries.** Hypothermia kills quietly — shivering, then confusion, clumsiness, slurred speech, then shivering stops (a late, dangerous sign). Rewarm gradually with insulation, warm sweet liquids, and body heat; never give alcohol. Heat injuries progress from cramps to exhaustion to heatstroke — a medical emergency with hot, dry skin and altered mental state; cool the person immediately with water, shade, and airflow. → `references/fire-shelter-navigation-knots.md`

## 7. Movement and Navigation — Getting to Recovery

**7.1. Stay or move?** Staying put is usually right when you are near a known route or crash site, when rescuers know where to look, when you are injured, or when moving would burn more than it gains. Move when water, food, or shelter cannot be found where you are, when the location is dangerous, or when you know a definite route to safety. Before moving, leave a clear record of your direction and intent — a note, an arrow of stones, a marked trail — and mark your trail as you go.

**7.2. Field-expedient direction finding.** Shadow-stick method: place a stick upright, mark the tip of its shadow, wait 15–20 minutes, mark the new tip — the line between marks runs roughly east–west (first mark is west). Watch method: point the hour hand at the sun; the angle between the hour hand and 12 o'clock, halved, points south (northern hemisphere). At night, Polaris (the North Star) sits almost directly above true north; in the southern hemisphere, use the Southern Cross. Moss does not reliably grow on the north side of trees — do not trust it.

**7.3. Navigation methods.** Dead reckoning (compass bearing plus pace count), terrain association (matching the ground to the map), and celestial navigation. Measure distance by pacing — know your personal pace count over 100 meters on flat ground, and adjust for slope, brush, and load. Estimate distance to landmarks by the apparent-size method. Cross water only where the current is slowest, at a bend or a wide shallow section; cross at an angle downstream; never cross a fast river alone if it can be avoided, and never tie yourself to a rope across a river.

**7.4. Signaling for recovery.** Three of anything is distress: three fires, three whistle blasts, three gunshots, three flashes. A signal mirror can be seen for miles on a sunny day — sweep the horizon, then flash the aircraft when it is in view. Ground-to-air signals: large geometric symbols (see the SERE skill for the full symbol set), at least 3 feet wide and 18 feet long, with high contrast against the background. → `references/fire-shelter-navigation-knots.md`

## 8. Survival Equipment and Knots

**8.1. The kit.** The manual's survival equipment chapter stresses maintaining what you have and improvising the rest: a cutting tool, a fire starter, a container for water, cordage, a signaling device, a first-aid kit, and navigation aids. A knife is the single most valuable survival tool. Field-expedient weapons and tools can be made from wood, stone, and bone, but a real blade is worth its weight many times over.

**8.2. Cordage and lashing.** Cordage can be made from plant fibers (nettle, milkweed, dogbane), inner bark, sinew, or rawhide — twist fibers into two-ply cord, then braid for strength. Lashings (square, diagonal, shear, tripod) let you build shelters, litters, and traps from poles.

**8.3. Essential knots (Appendix A).** Square knot (joining two ropes of equal size — never for lives or loads), bowline (a fixed loop that will not slip or jam — the rescue knot), figure-eight (stopper knot), clove hitch (quick attachment to a post), sheet bend (joining ropes of different sizes), prusik (a sliding friction knot for climbing and rescue), and the Speir knot. Learn these before you need them: a knot you cannot tie cold, wet, and tired is a knot you do not know. → `references/fire-shelter-navigation-knots.md`

## Output Contract

When asked a survival question, answer in this order: (1) what kills first in this situation and what to do about it right now, (2) the procedure with the numbers that matter (times, temperatures, quantities), (3) what to improvise if the proper tool is missing, (4) what NOT to do — the common fatal mistakes. Lead with the survival pattern priority for the environment in question. Cite the chapter (e.g., "ATP 3-50.21, Ch. 3 — Water") for any procedure or number. If the question is really a medical emergency, answer with the survival-medicine steps here and point to the `first-aid` and `tccc-casualty-response` skills for the full treatment. If it is really a navigation question, point to `land-navigation`.

## Operating Rules

1. **Medicine first, always.** Survival medicine has top priority in every environment.
2. **Water before food.** A person dies of thirst in days, of hunger in weeks. Never trade water for food effort.
3. **Purify everything.** Assume all surface water is contaminated; boiling is the most reliable method.
4. **Stay or move is a decision, not a feeling.** Decide on evidence — rescuers' knowledge, injury, resources — and leave a record of your choice.
5. **Three is distress.** Three fires, three blasts, three flashes — use it, and recognize it.
6. **Improvise, but practice first.** Friction fire, snares, and knots fail under stress if they have never been done in calm.
7. **Never eat what you cannot identify.** The Universal Edibility Test is a 24-hour process for a reason; never shortcut it, and never test mushrooms.
8. **The will to survive is a skill.** Routine, small goals, and a plan are the treatment for panic and despair.
