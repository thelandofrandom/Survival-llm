# Fire, Shelter, and Clothing

Source: ATP 3-50.21, Chapters 5 (Fire) and 6 (Shelter and Clothing).

## Chapter outlines

- Ch. 5: Fire basics; building a fire; fire-starting techniques
- Ch. 6: Primary shelter; shelter considerations; construction methods; clothing

## Fire — the triangle

Fire needs **heat, fuel, and oxygen**. Remove any one and it fails. Build on cleared ground down to mineral soil, sheltered from wind, with a fuel pile gathered before lighting.

## Fire lays

| Lay | Use |
|---|---|
| Tepee | Fastest to light; good for starting |
| Log cabin | Steady, longer burn; good for cooking |
| Pyramid / upside-down fire | Long burn, less tending |
| Star fire | Fuel conservation — feed sticks in from the ends |
| Lean-to | Windy conditions; reflects heat one direction |

Build tinder → kindling → fuel: tinder is the finest, driest material (shredded bark, dry grass, char cloth, bird down); kindling is pencil-thick; fuel is wrist-thick and larger.

## Ignition methods (most to least reliable)

1. Lighter or matches — carry them; this is not the time for pride.
2. Ferrocerium rod / fire steel with char cloth or fine tinder.
3. Magnifying lens on a sunny day.
4. Battery and fine steel wool.
5. Flint and steel with char cloth.
6. Friction: bow drill (most reliable friction method), hand drill, fire plow. Requires dry softwood hearth board, hardwood spindle, bow, bearing block — and practice.

Keep a coal alive: carry embers in a bundle of dry tinder, or bank the fire under ash overnight.

## Fire as a tool

Purifies water, cooks food, dries clothing, warms the shelter, deters animals, signals rescuers. **Three fires in a triangle is the international distress signal** — smoke by day (add green boughs or rubber for thick smoke), flame by night. Never build a fire you cannot control; never sleep with an open fire in an unventilated shelter.

## Shelter — site selection

High, dry ground; near water and firewood but not in a flood channel or dry wash; protected from wind; away from dead standing trees (widow-makers) and insect nests. Desert: go below ground — a below-ground shelter can be ~30°F cooler than the surface. Snow country: use snow itself as insulation (tree-pit, snow cave).

## Shelter types

| Shelter | Notes |
|---|---|
| Natural (cave, overhang, hollow tree) | Use first if available; check for animals and rockfall |
| Poncho lean-to | Fastest improvised shelter; one person |
| A-frame | Two-person; good in woodland |
| Debris hut | Ridgepole piled with leaves/debris ≥2 ft thick; warm, slow to build |
| Raised platform | Wet jungle; keeps you off ground water and insects |
| Tree-pit snow shelter | Dig under a snow-laden evergreen; snow insulates |
| Lean-to + fire reflector | Reflector wall bounces fire heat back at you |
| Below-ground desert shelter | Coolest option in extreme heat |

A shelter need only be big enough to lie in — extra volume is extra air to heat. **Insulate yourself from the ground first**; the ground steals more heat than the air.

## Clothing

- Layers trap air; loose layers insulate better than one tight layer.
- Keep dry: wet clothing loses most of its insulating value.
- Protect the head — a large share of body heat is lost through an uncovered head.
- Heat: loose, light-colored clothing covering skin; hat against sun.
- Improvise insulation: dry grass, leaves, or newspaper stuffed between layers; footgear from bark, hide, or wrapped cloth.

## Cold and heat injury quick reference

| Injury | Key signs | Field action |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothermia | Shivering → confusion, clumsiness, slurred speech → shivering stops | Rewarm gradually; insulation, warm sweet liquids, body heat; no alcohol |
| Frostbite | Pale, waxy, numb skin | Rewarm in warm water only if refreezing is impossible; never rub |
| Heat cramps | Muscle cramps, sweating | Rest in shade; sip salted water |
| Heat exhaustion | Heavy sweating, weakness, nausea | Shade, cooling, fluids |
| Heatstroke | Hot dry skin, confusion, unconsciousness | Cool immediately — water, shade, airflow; evacuate |
# Movement, Navigation, and Signaling

Source: ATP 3-50.21, Chapter 7 (Movement and Navigation) and Chapter 8 (Survival Equipment), plus AFH 10-644 Ch. 21 for ground-to-air signal standards.

## Chapter outline

- Ch. 7: Decision to stay or move; water crossings; cardinal direction; navigation methods; measuring distance; field-expedient distance estimation
- Ch. 8: Maintaining equipment; field-expedient weapons; cordage and lashing

## Stay or move

Stay when rescuers know where to look, when you are near a route or crash site, when injured, or when moving costs more than it gains. Move when the location is dangerous, when water/food/shelter cannot be found locally, or when you know a definite route to safety. Before moving, leave a record of your direction and intent and mark your trail.

## Finding direction without a compass

| Method | Procedure |
|---|---|
| Shadow stick | Upright stick; mark shadow tip; wait 15–20 min; mark new tip. Line between marks runs east–west; first mark is west. A line perpendicular through the stick points north–south. |
| Watch method (N. hemisphere) | Point hour hand at the sun; halve the angle between hour hand and 12 o'clock — that line points south. (Use 1 o'clock for daylight saving time.) |
| Polaris | North Star sits almost directly above true north in the northern hemisphere. |
| Southern Cross | Use in the southern hemisphere to find south. |
| Unreliable | Moss on trees — does not reliably grow on the north side. Do not trust it. |

## Navigation methods

- **Dead reckoning:** compass bearing + pace count. Know your personal pace count over 100 meters on flat ground; adjust for slope, brush, and load.
- **Terrain association:** match the ground you see to the map; use handrails (rivers, ridges, roads), attack points, and catching features.
- **Celestial:** sun by day, stars by night (see table above).
- **Distance estimation:** apparent size of known objects; pacing; time × speed.

## Water crossings

Cross where the current is slowest — at a bend, a wide shallow section, or a braided channel. Cross at an angle downstream. Unbuckle your pack (so you can shed it), use a stick as a third leg, and cross in a group linked arm-in-arm if possible. Never tie yourself to a rope across a river — a snagged rope drowns you.

## Signaling — the numbers

- **Three of anything is distress:** three fires in a triangle, three whistle blasts, three gunshots, three flashes.
- **Signal mirror:** can be seen for miles on a sunny day. Sweep the horizon, then flash the aircraft when it is in view.
- **Ground-to-air signals:** at least **3 feet wide and 18 feet long**, straight lines and square corners, high contrast against the background, in a large open area visible from all directions. Standard symbols: V = require assistance; X = require medical assistance; N = no / negative; Y = yes / affirmative; arrow = proceeding in this direction.
- **Smoke by day, flame by night.** Add green boughs for white smoke; rubber or oil for black smoke.

## Survival equipment — the kit

The single most valuable item is a cutting tool (a knife). After that: fire starter, water container, cordage, signaling device, first-aid kit, navigation aids. Maintain what you have; improvise the rest. Field-expedient weapons and tools can be made from wood, stone, and bone, but a real blade is worth its weight many times over.
# Knots, Cordage, and Equipment

Source: ATP 3-50.21, Chapter 8 (Survival Equipment) and Appendix A (Survival Knots and Rope).

## Chapter outline

- Ch. 8: Maintaining equipment; field-expedient weapons; cordage and lashing
- Appendix A: Elements of ropes and knots; knot types and uses

## Cordage

Make cordage from plant fibers (nettle, milkweed, dogbane), inner bark, sinew, or rawhide: separate fibers, twist into two-ply cord, braid plies for strength. Test every length before trusting it with a load.

## Lashings

| Lashing | Use |
|---|---|
| Square lashing | Join two poles at right angles |
| Diagonal lashing | Join two poles at an angle; prevents racking |
| Shear lashing | Join two poles side by side (A-frame legs) |
| Tripod lashing | Three poles for a tripod (cooking, shelter, signal) |
| Round lashing | Bind poles parallel |

## Essential knots

| Knot | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Square (reef) knot | Join two ropes of equal size | Never for lives or critical loads; capsizes under unequal load |
| Bowline | Fixed loop that will not slip or jam | The rescue knot; "the king of knots" |
| Bowline on a bight | Two loops in the middle of a rope | Rescue harness, hauling |
| Figure-eight | Stopper knot; prevents rope end running through | Easy to inspect |
| Clove hitch | Quick attachment to a post or pole | Can slip if load shifts; back it up |
| Sheet bend | Join ropes of different sizes | Double sheet bend for very different sizes |
| Prusik | Sliding friction knot | Climbing, rescue, tensioning; grips under load, slides when unloaded |
| Speir knot | Joining cordage | Shown in Appendix A, Figure A-11 |

## Rule

A knot you cannot tie cold, wet, tired, and in the dark is a knot you do not know. Practice the bowline, figure-eight, clove hitch, and sheet bend until they are automatic.

## Equipment maintenance

- Keep the blade sharp and dry; a dull knife is more dangerous than a sharp one.
- Keep fire-starting gear dry — carry tinder in a sealed container.
- Inspect cordage before every use; retire frayed or UV-damaged rope.
- Keep the water container clean; never store untreated water in it longer than needed.
- Know your kit blindfolded: in darkness or panic, you will find gear by feel and memory, not by sight.
