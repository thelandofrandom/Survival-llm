# Survival, Evasion, and Recovery

## Purpose

MCRP 3-02H *Survival, Evasion, and Recovery* (Marine Corps, June 1999 — a multiservice publication also issued as FM 21-76-1, NWP 3-50.3, and AFTTP(I) 3-2.26) is the pocket manual for the isolated service member: the aviator down behind enemy lines, the soldier cut off from his unit, anyone who must survive, evade capture, and get recovered. It is written to be carried — the manual itself recommends inclusion in the aviator's survival vest — and it is organized for use under stress: a quick-reference checklist up front, then nine short chapters. Its core idea is in its first line: **decide to survive.** Survival is a decision made before the crisis, and everything after it is procedure.

Key grounding facts: the SURVIVAL acronym (Size up, Use senses, Remember where you are, Vanquish fear, Improvise, Value living, Act like the natives, Live by your wits); water must be purified by iodine tablets, bleach, or boiling for 5 minutes; latrines go 200 feet from water; and recovery depends on the evader following the theater recovery plan — site selection, authentication, and signaling — rather than improvising contact with friendly forces.

## 1. Decide to Survive — The SURVIVAL Acronym

**S — Size up the situation, surroundings, physical condition, equipment.** Before acting, assess: what happened, where you are, what condition you are in, what you have. Panic action wastes the one resource you cannot replace — time.

**U — Use all your senses.** Sight, hearing, smell, touch. The enemy is detected by senses before he is seen; water is found by sound and smell before it is seen; danger is smelled (smoke, fuel, cooking) before it is heard.

**R — Remember where you are.** Keep track of your location from the moment of the incident. Note direction of travel, landmarks, and the last known position — navigation after the fact is guesswork.

**V — Vanquish fear and panic.** Fear is the first enemy. The manual's prescription is action: focus thoughts on the task at hand, work the checklist, and let procedure replace panic.

**I — Improvise and improve.** Use what you have; improve your situation a little at a time — shelter, water, fire, food, in that order of urgency.

**V — Value living.** The will to live is a decision, not a mood. The manual is explicit: the evader who decides to survive, survives; the one who gives up, does not.

**A — Act like the natives.** In evasion, blend with the local population's patterns — movement, dress, timing, behavior. Standing out is being caught.

**L — Live by your wits.** Knowledge, adaptability, and judgment are the survival kit that cannot be lost, confiscated, or broken.

## 2. Immediate Actions and the Hole-Up Site (Chapter I)

**2.1 Immediate actions.** The quick-reference checklist, executed in order:
1. Assess the immediate situation. **Think before you act.**
2. Take action to protect yourself from nuclear, biological, or chemical hazards (Chapter IX).
3. Seek a concealed site.
4. Assess medical condition; treat as necessary (Chapter V).
5. Sanitize your uniform of potentially compromising information.
6. Sanitize the area; hide equipment you are leaving.
7. Apply personal camouflage.
8. Move away from the concealed site, zigzag pattern recommended.
9. Use terrain to advantage — communication and concealment.
10. Find a hole-up site.

**2.2 The hole-up site.** At the hole-up site: reassess the situation; treat injuries, then inventory equipment; review the plan of action and establish priorities (Chapter VI); determine your current location; improve camouflage; focus thoughts on the task at hand; execute the plan — and stay flexible.

**2.3 Concealment.** Select a place of concealment providing: adequate concealment from ground and air; safe distance from enemy positions and lines of communication (LOC); listening and observation points; multiple avenues of escape; protection from the environment; and possible communications/signaling opportunities. Stay alert, maintain security, and drink water. Concealment is active — it is maintained, not found once and forgotten.

**2.4 Movement.** Travel slowly and deliberately. Do not leave evidence of travel; use noise and light discipline. Stay away from LOC. Stop, look, listen, and smell — then act. Move from one concealed area to another. Use evasion movement techniques: move at night or in bad weather when possible, use covered routes, and never move in a straight line toward your destination.

## 3. Navigation (Chapter II)

**3.1 The requirement.** The evader must know where he is, where he is going, and how to get there without a map or compass if necessary. Navigation is a survival skill, not a convenience: the difference between evasion and wandering is a direction.

**3.2 Methods.** Compass and map when available; sun and shadow when they are not. The stick-shadow method: place a stick upright, mark the tip of its shadow, wait 15 minutes, mark the new tip — the line between the marks runs roughly east–west (first mark is west). Stars: the North Star for latitude and direction in the northern hemisphere; the Southern Cross in the southern. Terrain association — reading the ground against the map — is the most reliable method when a map exists. Pace count and time-distance estimation keep the evader honest about how far he has actually traveled.

**3.3 Navigation discipline.** Move on a deliberate azimuth; check it often; record distance traveled; note landmarks passed. Never trust a single indicator — confirm direction by two methods when you can.

## 4. Radio Communications and Signaling (Chapter III)

**4.1 The rule.** Communicate as directed in applicable plans and orders — particularly when considering transmitting in the blind. Be prepared to use communications and signaling devices on short notice. **Use of communications and signaling devices may compromise your position.** Every transmission is a trade: information for exposure. The theater plan decides the trade, not the evader's impatience.

**4.2 Radio.** The survival radio is used per the evasion plan of action and the theater recovery plan. Authentication procedures — proving you are who you claim to be — are prearranged; an evader who cannot authenticate will not be recovered by radio, and an enemy who can imitate authentication is the reason the procedures exist.

**4.3 Visual signals.** The signal mirror: use as directed by recovery forces; if no radio, use only with confirmed friendly forces; cover when not in use. Use the mirror to sweep the horizon. Smoke: black smoke (burning rubber/oil) for contrast against light backgrounds, white smoke (green vegetation) against dark backgrounds; use smoke signals as directed. Panel signals and ground-to-air signals (the standard international ground signals) mark positions and convey simple messages to aircraft. Audio signals — voice, whistle, weapons fire — are used when visual signals will not work, and sparingly: sound carries, and the enemy hears it too.

**4.4 Signaling checklist.** Before signaling: confirm the intended recipient is friendly; select the signal the recovery force expects; be ready to authenticate; have an escape route if the signal draws the wrong attention.

## 5. Recovery (Chapter IV)

**5.1 The recovery site.** Select sites in accordance with the criteria in theater recovery plans. Ensure the site is free of hazards; secure personal gear; select the best area for communications and signaling devices; observe the site for proximity to enemy activity and LOC. Follow recovery force instructions — exactly, immediately, and without improvisation. The recovery force's instructions are the plan; the evader's job is to be where the plan says, when it says, doing what it says.

**5.2 Helicopter recovery.** The manual covers helicopter pickup procedures: the evader's actions on the approach of the aircraft, the use of the hoist or ladder, and the discipline of staying in position until directed. Never approach a helicopter from the tail; never move until signaled; secure all loose equipment.

**5.3 Authentication.** Recovery depends on authentication — the evader proving identity to the recovery force, and the recovery force proving itself to the evader. Prearranged codes, questions, and signals are the mechanism. An evader who breaks authentication discipline can be recovered by the enemy instead.

## 6. Medical (Chapter V)

**6.1 Self-aid.** The evader is his own medic. Assess, treat, and keep moving: control bleeding with direct pressure and dressings; immobilize fractures with improvised splints; treat shock by keeping the patient warm, flat, and still; and manage pain so the patient can move. The chapter is first aid, not definitive care — the goal is to keep the evader alive and mobile until recovery.

**6.2 Field hygiene and illness prevention.** Purify all water (see Chapter VII). Wash hands before preparing food or water. Clean eating utensils after each meal. Locate latrines 200 feet from water and away from shelter. Treat blisters and foot problems early — a foot injury ends evasion faster than hunger. Prevent with improvised goggles in bright snow or sand; protect lips and mouth from cold injury.

**6.3 The medical priority.** In evasion, medical care serves movement. A treatment that keeps the evader moving is worth more than a perfect treatment that keeps him in place. Know the difference between an injury that can be managed on the move and one that requires a hole-up — and make the call early.

## 7. Personal Protection (Chapter VI)

**7.1 Shelter.** Shelter is protection from the environment first and concealment second. The evader builds or finds shelter that keeps him dry and out of the wind — a lean-to, a debris hut, a snow cave, or a natural rock overhang, depending on the environment. A shelter that can be seen from the air is a shelter that should not be built. Improve shelter a little at a time; a dry evader with a poor shelter outlasts a wet evader with a good plan.

**7.2 Clothing.** Dress for the environment and the activity: layers that can be added or removed, avoiding overdressing and overheating (sweat is the enemy in cold weather — wet clothing loses insulation). Take special care of feet: change socks regularly, dry them when possible, and treat hot spots before they become blisters.

**7.3 Fire.** Fire is warmth, water purification, cooking, and morale — and a signal to the enemy. Build small fires, shielded from observation, using dry wood that burns with little smoke; extinguish completely and scatter the ashes. A fire that can be seen or smelled from a distance is a liability, not an asset.

**7.4 Priorities.** The manual's personal-protection priorities: consider food and water requirements; take special care of feet; pack equipment to prevent loss, damage, pack imbalance, and personal-safety hazards; go around obstacles, not over or through them; maintain a realistic pace and take rest stops when needed.

## 8. Water (Chapter VII)

**8.1 Requirements.** Water is the first survival priority — a man can live weeks without food and days without water, and in heat the margin shrinks to hours. The manual's water chapter covers requirements, procurement, and preparation/storage.

**8.2 Procurement.** Water sources in order of preference: rain, streams and springs, lakes, dew, and — when nothing else exists — improvised sources. The solar still: a pit covered with clear plastic, a container in the center, a stone depressing the plastic above the container — sunlight evaporates ground moisture and condenses it into the container. The vegetation bag: a clear plastic bag tied over leafy vegetation, collecting transpired water. Green bamboo holds water in its sections. Dry-area techniques are slow and yield little — they are a supplement, not a supply.

**8.3 Preparation and storage.** **Purify all water obtained from natural sources by using iodine tablets, bleach, or boiling for 5 minutes.** Boiling is the most reliable method. Filter first when the water is cloudy — sediment shields organisms from chemical treatment. Store purified water in clean, covered containers; never store it where it can be contaminated by latrines or waste. The latrine rule again: 200 feet from water.

## 9. Food (Chapter VIII)

**9.1 The priority rule.** Food is the lowest survival priority — water, shelter, and evasion come first. A healthy man can go days without food and still move. The manual's food chapter exists so the evader can extend his endurance, not so he can eat well.

**9.2 Procurement techniques.** Snares work while unattended — the simple loop snare, built with material that will not break under the strain of holding an animal, using a figure-8 (locking) loop if wire is used; snares are set on runs and trails, where animals actually travel. Fishing: improvised hooks, lines, and traps. Trapping and hunting are energy-expensive and noisy — snares and fishing are the evader's methods because they work while he rests and hides.

**9.3 Plant foods.** Use the evasion chart to identify edible plants — the chart is the authority, not memory. If you cannot positively identify an edible plant and choose to try an unknown plant, the manual's edibility test applies: selection criteria first (enough of the plant to test; a plant that is not obviously poisonous by smell or appearance), then the staged test — touch to skin, touch to lip, small taste, small swallow, wait — with hours between stages and no other new food during the test. A plant that fails any stage is not eaten. **Never eat a plant you cannot positively identify when any alternative exists.**

**9.4 Rules for avoiding illness.** Purify all water; locate latrines 200 feet from water and away from shelter; wash hands before preparing food or water; clean all eating utensils after each meal. Food poisoning in evasion is not an inconvenience — it is a mission-ending event.

## 10. Induced Conditions (Chapter IX)

**10.1 NBC hazards.** The final chapter covers nuclear, biological, and chemical hazards: recognition, protection, and first aid. The evader's NBC rule is avoidance — detect, avoid, and decontaminate if exposed — because an evader without protective equipment cannot fight through a contaminated area. Take action to protect yourself from NBC hazards as one of the immediate actions (Chapter I), before seeking concealment.

**10.2 The induced-conditions mindset.** Heat, cold, altitude, and NBC are conditions the environment or the enemy imposes on the evader. The response is the same in each case: recognize the condition, protect against it with what you have, and adjust the plan — slower movement, more water, better shelter, a different route — rather than pushing through it and becoming a casualty.

## Output Contract

When asked about survival, evasion, or recovery, answer from this manual's sequence: decide to survive (SURVIVAL), immediate actions and the hole-up site, navigation, communications and signaling, recovery, then the sustaining skills (medical, protection, water, food, induced conditions). Give the numbers the manual gives — 5 minutes' boiling, 200 feet for latrines, the SURVIVAL letters in order — and say when a technique (such as a specific solar-still yield) is a supplement rather than a supply. Never present modern SERE doctrine (JP 3-50) as if it were in this 1999 manual; if the question needs current doctrine, say this manual is the 1999 multiservice baseline and bridge explicitly. For any question about contacting friendly forces, lead with authentication and the theater recovery plan — an evader who improvises contact is an evader who gets captured.

## Operating Rules

1. **Decide to survive — first, and out loud.** Everything else is procedure; the decision is the foundation.
2. **Think before you act.** The first immediate action is assessment, not movement.
3. **Purify all water.** Iodine, bleach, or 5 minutes' boiling — no exceptions for natural sources.
4. **Latrines 200 feet from water.** Sanitation is survival; diarrhea ends evasion.
5. **Communications may compromise position.** Signal only as the plan directs, and authenticate.
6. **Food is the lowest priority.** Water, shelter, and evasion come first; snares work while you rest.
7. **Never eat an unidentified plant when any alternative exists.** The edibility test is a last resort, not a menu.
8. **Follow recovery force instructions exactly.** The plan is theirs; your job is to be where it says, when it says.
