# JP 3-50.2 Doctrine for Joint Combat Search and Rescue

## Purpose

JP 3-50.2, *Doctrine for Joint Combat Search and Rescue* (26 January 1996), is the joint doctrine that tells a joint force who is responsible for getting isolated personnel back, how the rescue organization is built, and how a CSAR incident runs from the first distress call to the final debriefing. It is the "who and how it is organized" companion to JP 3-50.21, which supplies the tactics, techniques, and procedures. Together the two publications cover the whole CSAR problem: this one assigns responsibility and sets the system; the other executes inside it.

The manual's governing idea is simple and is stated as a commander's obligation: the joint force commander (JFC) has primary authority and responsibility for CSAR in support of U.S. forces within the area of responsibility or joint operations area — including civilian personnel such as Civil Reserve Air Fleet crew members and deployed technical representatives. No component does CSAR alone if it can be helped; every component supports the others' CSAR operations to the fullest extent practicable, and adjacent JFCs owe each other mutual support. The system exists so that a downed aircrew, a cut-off patrol, or an isolated individual is never one service's problem alone.

Three definitions carry the whole doctrine. **Combat search and rescue (CSAR)** is the search for and recovery of isolated personnel in a combat environment, by dedicated or capable forces, under threat. **Isolated personnel** are military or civilian personnel separated from their unit or organization in an environment requiring them to survive, evade, resist, or escape while awaiting recovery. The **Joint Search and Rescue Center (JSRC)** is the joint facility that plans, coordinates, and executes joint SAR and CSAR within the joint force's assigned area; the **Rescue Coordination Center (RCC)** is its component-level counterpart. → `references/organization-and-responsibilities.md`

## 1. Who Is Responsible — The Chain of Obligation

**1.1. Services and USSOCOM.** Each Service and U.S. Special Operations Command is responsible for performing CSAR in support of its own operations, consistent with its assigned functions — but each must take into account the availability and capability of the other Services' CSAR-capable forces, including the U.S. Coast Guard. Self-sufficiency is the starting point, not the end state; the doctrine assumes cross-component help.

**1.2. The joint force commander.** The JFC owns CSAR for the joint force. The JFC normally delegates the recovery mission to the joint force component commanders, but retains the duty to establish a JSRC to monitor recovery efforts, plan, coordinate, and execute joint SAR and CSAR, and integrate CSAR with other evasion, escape, and recovery operations. When planning, the JFC must weigh host-nation policies, laws, regulations, and capabilities. The JFC normally exercises control of all forces committed to a joint CSAR operation through a designated component commander or through the JSRC.

**1.3. Component commanders.** Component commanders plan and conduct CSAR in support of their own operations while executing the JFC's campaign and operation plans, taking into account the forces of other components — including the Coast Guard if assigned. They establish an RCC (or functional equivalent, such as the Navy's Rescue Coordination Team) to coordinate all component CSAR activity, and they provide mutual CSAR support to other components to the greatest extent possible. If a component commander does not establish an RCC, those duties fall to another staff organization, normally the operations section, with a trained and designated CSAR representative — the component search and rescue controller — acting in the commander's name.

**1.4. Unit commanders.** Unit commanders must be prepared, from inherent capabilities, to conduct CSAR in support of their own operations and to provide mutual CSAR support to other units. CSAR planning is part of all operation planning, not an afterthought. Requirements beyond a unit's capability go to the component RCC — additional CSAR-capable forces, planning information, evasion aids. Unit commanders also ensure their people are familiar with joint CSAR doctrine, joint force CSAR SOPs, and any area-specific TTP, and that unit and personal evasion plans of action (EPAs) and isolated personnel reports (ISOPREPs) are current.

**1.5. Isolated personnel.** Isolated personnel have responsibilities of their own, and the doctrine treats them as active participants, not passive victims: they confirm distress and authenticate themselves, follow their EPA, use evasion aids and signaling as trained, and cooperate with recovery forces. Their best contribution is preparation done before the event — a current ISOPREP, a rehearsed EPA, and knowledge of authentication data and procedures. → `references/isolated-personnel.md`

## 2. The JSRC and the RCCs — The Rescue Organization

**2.1. The JSRC.** The JSRC is a primary SAR facility, suitably staffed by supervisory personnel and equipped for planning, coordinating, and executing joint SAR and CSAR operations within the joint force's area. It is operated jointly by personnel from two or more Service or functional components, or it may have a multinational staff (a multinational SAR center). It should be staffed equitably by trained personnel drawn from each joint force component, including Coast Guard participation where practical. The JFC tasks one of the component commanders to designate their component RCC to function also as the JSRC; that component must possess the necessary forces and capabilities — command, control, communications, computers, and intelligence (C4I), and surveillance — to plan and execute joint CSAR expeditiously, and the JFC gives it the authority to do so. The designated component commander designates the JSRC Director, who has overall responsibility for operating the center. In a limited operation with no significant component involvement, the JFC may instead establish the JSRC as part of the JFC's own staff. Standing JFCs (subordinate unified commands, standing JTFs) should maintain a standing JSRC; contingency JTF commanders should establish one in the earliest stages of forming the JTF.

**2.2. JSRC duties in peacetime.** Develop joint force CSAR SOPs and communications plans; establish reporting requirements for the JSRC and component RCCs; help develop CSAR appendixes to Annex C (Operations) of OPLANs, CONPLANs, and OPORDs, linked to casualty affairs, medical, repatriation, and mortuary affairs appendixes; coordinate and deconflict component evasion and recovery plans and review them for supportability; develop an integrated personnel recovery concept of operations; conduct on-the-job and informal training for JSRC personnel and RCC augmentation personnel; organize and conduct CSAR mission training exercises; coordinate peacetime SAR as realistic training; and develop a plan to transition from peacetime to combat operations, including augmentation personnel, additional communications, and dedicated intelligence support requirements.

**2.3. JSRC duties in combat.** Develop a joint force CSAR threat decision matrix tailored to the current threat analysis; develop and disseminate special instructions (SPINS) in air tasking orders specifying the primary theater CSAR and recovery guidance, concepts, and procedures for all high-risk combatants; alert appropriate components to the location of isolated personnel; coordinate with national, theater, host-nation, and component intelligence resources to gather information on the location and status of isolated personnel and the threat to their recovery; coordinate with the psychological operations officer on ways to influence the local population favorably regarding CSAR; coordinate and deconflict mutual CSAR support by joint force components and multinational forces; monitor all CSAR incidents prosecuted by component RCCs; and maintain a database and file on each isolated person until recovery is complete — then forward all files and the database to the Joint Services Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape (SERE) Agency. The files should not be destroyed.

**2.4. The component RCC.** The RCC coordinates all component CSAR activities, including coordination with the JSRC and other component RCCs. Its typical duties: notify and coordinate with the JSRC when conducting unilateral CSAR missions; coordinate CSAR support provided to or received from other components; coordinate augmentation personnel to the JSRC; develop specific component RCC procedures; extract planning factors from the JSRC's theater evasion and recovery concept of operations and pass them to subordinate units for evasion-plan development; coordinate and develop unit and personal EPAs; maintain staff cognizance for preparing and maintaining ISOPREPs; develop and review CSAR and evasion-and-recovery appendixes to Annex C for component supporting plans; forward pertinent data on isolated personnel, their status, and location to the JSRC on request; and maintain a file on each isolated person until recovery is complete. The RCC is the vital link between isolated personnel and joint CSAR support available within or adjacent to the joint force.

## 3. The Incident Sequence — From Distress to Debriefing

**3.1. Distress indicators.** A CSAR incident begins with one of these: a Mayday call; nonreturn from a mission; overdue contact; receipt of an emergency beacon transmission; sighting of an aircraft or vessel going down; a report of personnel isolated by enemy activity; or receipt of ground emergency codes used by survivors of downed aircraft. The doctrine stresses that the indicator may be picked up by noncomponent assets — an AWACS, for example — and relayed directly to the JSRC, causing multiple reports of the same incident. Personnel follow the reporting procedures anyway; duplicate reports are expected and harmless, missed reports are not.

**3.2. The sequence of events.** The typical sequence runs: (a) the unit requesting CSAR support notifies its component RCC, which notifies the JSRC; (b) the component RCC assumes duties as CSAR mission coordinator initially, reports the incident to the JSRC, initiates CSAR planning, receives an intelligence briefing to determine area threat, designates an ISOPREP control point and obtains ISOPREP data and the EPA from units, tasks subordinate CSAR-capable forces, coordinates with the JSRC and the requesting unit, and requests additional recovery forces through the JSRC if component resources are inadequate; (c) the JSRC coordinates JFC tasking of other component RCCs when a component cannot execute or needs support, coordinates use of nondedicated CSAR resources, coordinates SOF use with the J-3 and the special operations component, coordinates development of a CSAR task force (CSARTF) with component CSAR controllers, coordinates with intelligence and the special operations component to alert evasion-and-recovery nets where established, alerts all forces in the area to report any evidence of isolated personnel, and determines whether current operations will provide temporary air superiority near the isolated personnel; (d) the JSRC assigns a CSAR mission coordinator and provides all available data; (e) the mission coordinator confirms the distress call and authentication data and assists in planning; (f) isolated personnel confirm distress and authenticate; (g) recovery forces evaluate the probability of success and execute; (h) intelligence personnel debrief recovered personnel.

**3.3. When the JSRC takes over as mission coordinator.** Component RCCs are normally the first line of response. The JSRC assumes the mission-coordinator role when RCCs are not established; when the JSRC receives the initial notification; when the event is complex enough to require tasking several component commanders; when the mission is beyond conventional CSAR forces and requires a SOF response; when the responding RCC requests additional assistance and asks the JSRC to assume coordination; or when current operations or nonavailability of CSAR-capable resources preclude the component commander from initiating or continuing a response.

**3.4. Speed matters.** Chances of successful rescue are improved by acting quickly and using all immediately available resources. When immediate CSAR is not possible, the unit commander expeditiously requests CSAR using the most secure radio system available, followed by message traffic. The doctrine's bias throughout is toward launching the assessment and the planning in parallel with notification, not in sequence after it.

## 4. Incident Reporting — What to Send, and How

**4.1. The distressed individual or crew.** When bailout, crash landing, or ditching appears imminent, or when conditions are severely deteriorating, pilots and aircrew should attempt radio contact — calling on the frequency of last contact or an established common frequency — and transmit: call sign, nature of distress, type of aircraft or unit, position, course, speed, altitude, number of personnel, and the pilot's or unit commander's intentions. Transmit "in the blind" only when contact with known friendly forces is impossible by any other means. Attempt to maintain the transmission long enough to permit a direction-finder plot of position — while recognizing that in some instances enemy direction-finding may preclude continuous transmission. If time permits, turn IFF to the emergency position; aircrews may elect not to, to avoid highlighting the aircraft to enemy forces.

**4.2. The unit.** When notified of a subordinate element's distress, the unit commander decides whether immediate on-scene support is appropriate and may designate an on-scene commander (OSC) for escort, communications, or other immediate needs. Unit commanders also report their present capability to conduct CSAR and the status of any ongoing CSAR in support of their own operations. The information for a request for active CSAR support is set out in Figure I-2 of the manual.

**4.3. The RCC and the JSRC.** The component RCC forwards CSAR incident information to the JSRC by the quickest and most secure means, normally radio or telephone, followed up with the applicable U.S. message text formats (USMTFs). The JSRC receives reports on all CSAR incidents involving joint force resources, forwards them to higher authority as directed, and provides follow-up reports to higher authority, adjacent JSRCs, and component RCCs. Selected USMTFs for CSAR are in Appendix K. → `references/incident-reporting-and-search.md`

## 5. Search Methods — Finding the Isolated Person

**5.1. Electronic search.** Search radars and direction-finding or homing equipment conduct electronic searches: surface vessel radars, forward-looking airborne radars, and side-looking airborne radars. Effectiveness is limited initially by the sending capability of the distressed person's equipment (emergency locator beacon) and the receiving capability of the searchers (homing adaptor), and subsequently by enemy activity.

**5.2. Visual search.** Visual and electronic searches are conducted using the search-area designation methods below, tailored to each incident by environment (maritime, coastal, inland), enemy activity, weather, time of day, and available CSAR-capable resources. Maritime searches normally combine surface and air search and rescue units; detailed maritime planning factors — aerospace drift, maritime drift, water temperature — are in JP 3-50 (National Search and Rescue Manual, Volume I) and JP 3-50.1 (Volume II, Planning Handbook).

**5.3. Search-area designation methods.** The manual gives six, and the choice depends on environment and what is known:

| Method | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary (maritime) | Square or rectangle oriented east-west or north-south, stated as two latitudes and two longitudes | D-7: 26N to 27N, 64W to 65W |
| Corner point (maritime) | Area (except circular) described by stating in sequence the latitude and longitude of each corner | E-7: 23°15'N 74°35'W to 23°10'N 73°25'W to 22°20'N 73°25'W to 22°25'N 74°25'W to origin |
| Center point | Area defined by a center point and radius | Used when the location is approximate and a radius search is practical |
| Track line | Area along a known track or route | Used when the isolated person was last known along a route |
| Boundary (inland) | Area bounded by prominent geographic features, stated in sequence | A-1: Highway 15 south, Lake Merhaven west, Runslip River north, Bravado mountain range east |
| Corner point (inland) | Area described by stating in sequence the geographic features of each corner | A-6: junction of Highways 15 and 26, Red River bridge, north end of Whitney Canal, summit of Eagle Mountain |

The inland boundary method is the one most often used for inland CSAR incidents, because terrain features are what survivors and searchers can both actually see and describe.

## 6. Recovery Methods — Getting Them Out

**6.1. The recovery menu.** Joint CSAR recovery methods include helicopter recovery (the workhorse — including hoist, rope, and landing pickups), fixed-wing recovery, ground-force recovery by conventional forces, special operations recovery, and naval vessel recovery. The method is chosen by threat, terrain, the isolated person's condition and location, and what forces are available; JP 3-50.21 covers the execution-level detail of each.

**6.2. The airborne mission commander (AMC).** In a CSAR task force, the AMC controls the airborne effort, coordinates the rescue combat air patrol (RESCAP), forward air controllers, and the recovery aircraft, and keeps the JSRC/RCC informed. The manual's Figure II-2 lists AMC duties; the TTP volume carries the AMC checklist. The on-scene commander runs the immediate recovery scene. The distinction matters: the AMC manages the air picture and the task force, the OSC manages the ground truth at the pickup point.

**6.3. Authentication before recovery.** Recovery is not normally initiated until the isolated person has been authenticated. The ISOPREP (DD Form 1833) is the primary means of authentication — it carries the individual's identifying data, including photograph and fingerprints where available. An original may be required by the recovering unit, and the capability to make quality copies at the deployment location may not exist, so originals matter. Authentication data must be protected: if the enemy learns the answers, the rescue force can be lured into a trap. → `references/isolated-personnel.md`

## 7. Command, Control, Coordination, and Communications

**7.1. Command relationships.** The manual lays out the typical relationships in Figures III-1 through III-3: JFC to Service components, JFC to functional components, and the variant in which the commander of special operations forces has been assigned joint force CSAR responsibilities. Control of forces committed to a joint CSAR operation is exercised by the JFC through a designated component commander or through the JSRC. The key point for planners is that CSAR command relationships are established before the incident, in the OPLAN and the CSAR appendix — not improvised at the distress call.

**7.2. Mission type orders.** CSAR is normally tasked by mission type orders: the commander states the mission and the intent, and the executing commander decides the how. This matters because CSAR is time-critical and threat-driven; over-prescriptive orders from above slow the response and strip the on-scene commander of the flexibility the situation demands.

**7.3. Coordination.** CSAR coordination runs through the JSRC and the RCCs, using established CSAR communications channels maintained and monitored in the RCC. Coordination covers: tasking other components when one cannot execute; use of nondedicated CSAR resources; SOF employment; CSARTF development; evasion-and-recovery net activation; and mutual support with adjacent JFCs and multinational forces, which may require temporary exchange of liaison personnel.

**7.4. Communications and interoperability.** The manual specifies typical voice circuits in a joint CSAR task force (Figure III-4) and interoperability requirements (Appendix G) covering the communications, procedures, and equipment compatibility that let Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Air Force, Coast Guard, and SOF elements work the same incident. Interoperability is treated as a planning requirement, not a hope: if the helicopter cannot talk to the fighter providing cover and the JSRC cannot talk to either, the task force does not exist in any useful sense. Pre-mission communications checks and common procedures (USMTFs, SPINS, brevity) are the mechanisms.

## 8. Planning — Before the Distress Call

**8.1. Concurrent operational planning.** CSAR planning is concurrent with operational planning. The JSRC and RCCs build CSAR appendixes to Annex C of OPLANs, CONPLANs, and OPORDs, and the CSAR appendix must be linked to casualty affairs, medical, repatriation, and mortuary affairs. Planning factors extracted from the theater evasion-and-recovery concept of operations flow down to units so they can write supportable evasion plans.

**8.2. Precautionary CSAR.** Where missions put personnel at high risk of isolation — deep strikes, overwater transits, operations near hostile territory — the JFC establishes precautionary CSAR: forces postured, alerted, and positioned before the mission flies, so the response begins in minutes rather than hours. Precautionary CSAR is the doctrinal answer to the fact that the first hour after isolation is usually the decisive one.

**8.3. Threat analysis.** The JSRC develops a CSAR threat decision matrix tailored to the current threat analysis. Planners assess the threat to aircraft and the threat to ground forces separately — they drive different force requirements. Threat, climate and weather, and astronomical conditions (Figure IV-1) all shape the plan: darkness, moonlight, cloud cover, and seasonal weather change both the survivor's prospects and the search's effectiveness.

**8.4. Decision making and the planning checklist.** Chapter IV closes with a joint CSAR planning checklist (Figure IV-2) covering the decision factors a commander weighs: threat, weather, available forces, the isolated person's status and location, authentication, and the probability of success. The doctrine is explicit that a CSAR mission is a risk decision, not an automatic launch: recovery forces evaluate the probability of success and the commander decides. A rescue that becomes a second loss helps no one.

## 9. Intelligence and Support Requirements

**9.1. Intelligence.** Chapter V requires current intelligence on the threat, the objective area, and the isolated person's situation: order of battle, air defense, enemy search and capture capabilities, terrain, and population attitudes. Sources include the theater joint intelligence center / joint intelligence support element, national capabilities, component intelligence, and automated data processing intelligence systems. The JSRC must be able to get intelligence fast — the threat picture at the moment of the incident, not last week's estimate. National intelligence capabilities (including space-based systems) can be tasked through the JSRC to refine the isolated person's location.

**9.2. Support.** Chapter VI covers the people and the facilities: JSRC and RCC staffing (trained, experienced personnel, equitably drawn from components), augmentation requirements, and facility and operating-space requirements — the JSRC needs communications, intelligence access, plotting and display capability, and space for a 24-hour operation. A CSAR center that cannot operate around the clock is a peacetime convenience, not a combat capability.

**9.3. Service capabilities.** Appendixes A through F catalog the CSAR capabilities of the Army, Navy, Marine Corps, Air Force, Coast Guard, and the special operations component — what each brings (aircraft, personnel, equipment, limitations) so planners can build a joint task force from real capabilities rather than assumptions. Appendix G covers interoperability requirements; Appendix H gives the Evasion Plan of Action format; Appendix J gives instructions for the ISOPREP (DD Form 1833); Appendix K gives selected U.S. message text formats for CSAR.

## 10. The Isolated Person's Kit — EPA and ISOPREP

**10.1. Evasion Plan of Action.** The EPA is the individual's (and unit's) pre-planned course of action if isolated: intended routes, rally points, safe areas (SAFEs), signaling methods, and actions on contact with friendly or hostile forces. EPAs should be mission-specific for aircrews, developed and updated as requirements dictate, and readily available to the JSRC through the RCC when needed. A good EPA is the difference between a survivor who moves with purpose toward recovery and one who wanders.

**10.2. ISOPREP.** The ISOPREP (DD Form 1833, Appendix J) is the personal data record used to authenticate an isolated person and to plan their recovery: identity, physical description, photograph, fingerprints, and authentication data (code words, personal details only the individual would know). Component commanders must ensure ISOPREPs — including fingerprinting — are properly prepared and maintained for all personnel whose duties may place them in danger of isolation. The ISOPREP is the primary means of authentication, and its data must be safeguarded as carefully as any classified material, because compromised authentication data turns a rescue into an ambush.

## Output Contract

When asked about joint CSAR doctrine, answer from this publication's structure: (1) name who owns the problem at each echelon — JFC, component commander, unit commander, isolated person — and what each owes; (2) describe the JSRC/RCC organization and who staffs and directs it; (3) walk the incident sequence from distress indicator through notification, mission coordination, execution, and debriefing; (4) give the reporting flow and the information a distress call must carry; (5) name the search method and area-designation method that fits the environment; (6) state the recovery method and the authentication requirement before recovery; (7) cite the planning factors — threat matrix, weather, astronomy, precautionary CSAR, go/no-go judgment. Distinguish doctrine (this publication) from TTP (JP 3-50.21) and from the evasion-and-recovery doctrine in JP 3-50.3. Do not invent modern organizations or procedures: this is 1996 doctrine, and where current practice differs (e.g., the PR system in JP 3-50 and AFDP 3-50), say so.

## Operating Rules

1. **The JFC owns CSAR.** Delegation to components does not remove the JFC's responsibility, and the JSRC is the JFC's instrument for exercising it.
2. **Report fast, report twice.** Duplicate reports of one incident are expected; a missing report is not. Notify the RCC, which notifies the JSRC, by the quickest secure means, then follow up with message traffic.
3. **Authenticate before recovering.** No recovery launches on an unauthenticated voice. Protect ISOPREP data as if lives depend on it — they do.
4. **Plan CSAR concurrently with operations, not after.** Precautionary CSAR is postured before high-risk missions fly.
5. **CSAR is a risk decision.** Recovery forces evaluate probability of success; commanders decide. A second loss is not a rescue.
6. **Interoperability is a planning requirement.** Common communications, procedures, and SPINS are built before the incident, not discovered during it.
7. **Keep the file.** Maintain a database and file on each isolated person until recovery is complete, then forward it to the Joint Services SERE Agency — never destroy it.
