# FM 3-25.150 Combatives (2002)

## Purpose

FM 3-25.150, Combatives, U.S. Army, 18 January 2002, 265 pages — the bridge edition of Army combatives. It supersedes FM 21-150 (30 September 1992) and introduces the ground-fighting system that the current TC 3-25.150 (2017) later refined: dominant body positions, body-positioning moves, finishing moves (chokes and arm bars), and drills built around position before submission. At the same time it keeps much of the 1992 manual's older material — rifle-bayonet fighting, field-expedient weapons, standing defense against chokes and knives, and group tactics — that the 2017 edition trimmed or reorganized.

**Currency warning — read this first.** The current standard is TC 3-25.150, Combatives (March 2017); use the `combatives` skill for the current system. This 2002 skill is for what differs and what endures: the **first full statement of the dominant-position system**, the **basic/advanced ground-fighting progression** in its original form, **standing defense** (chokes, bear hugs, knife, bayonet), and **group tactics** (lethal and restrictive force scenarios, including one-against-two). Where the 2002 and 2017 manuals disagree on a technique's details, the 2017 manual governs modern training.

The manual opens with Patton: "Very few people have ever been killed with the bayonet or saber, but the fear of having their guts explored with cold steel in the hands of battle-maddened men has won many a fight." Its definition is unchanged from 1992: hand-to-hand combat is an engagement between two or more persons in an empty-handed struggle or with handheld weapons such as knives, sticks, and rifles with bayonets — and it matters because projectile weapons may be lost, broken, or fail to fire, and because friendly and enemy forces intermingled make firearms and grenades impractical.

## 1. Introduction — definition, purposes, principles, safety

**Purposes of combative training (1-2).** Battlefield scenarios may require silent elimination of the enemy; unarmed combat and expedient-weapons training should not be limited to forward units. Any soldier may face an unarmed confrontation, and the training also builds strength, flexibility, balance, cardiorespiratory fitness, courage, confidence, self-discipline, and esprit de corps.

**Basic principles (1-3).** The 1992 principles carry over — physical balance (feet shoulder-width, knees flexed, low center of gravity; exploit the opponent's balance), mental balance (fear and anger must not overcome concentration), position (move the whole body off the line of attack, usually at 45 degrees), and timing (too soon and he anticipates; too late and he strikes) — but the 2002 manual reframes them around the ground fight: the principles now serve the goal of gaining a dominant body position, not merely landing a strike.

**Safety (1-4).** Safety precautions are a chapter-1 requirement, not an afterthought: controlled progression, protective equipment, and trained supervision before any live training against resistance. → `references/introduction-and-training.md`

## 2. Training — trainers, unit programs, areas, teaching

**Train the trainer (Section I).** Instructors must be physically fit and highly proficient in demonstration and practical application. Safety precautions are the trainer's first responsibility; no technique is taught live until it has been taught by the numbers.

**Unit training (Section II).** Basic or one-station unit training establishes the foundation; the unit sustainment training program keeps it alive. The program is designed to run anywhere with little preparation — the manual's recurring insistence that combatives needs a partner and ground, not a facility.

**Training areas (Section III).** Training formations for mass instruction; a matted room for ground fighting; pit construction for throws and falls; and the bayonet assault course, where previously learned rifle-bayonet techniques are graded under near-combat demands.

**Teaching techniques (Section IV).** Warm-ups and stretches (prescribed, with named exercises), crawl-walk-run progression, demonstrations, execution by the numbers, execution at combat speed, drills, and training pads and other protective equipment. The sequence matters: a technique drilled to automaticity by the numbers can be executed at combat speed; a technique rushed to combat speed is just flailing with a partner. → `references/introduction-and-training.md`

## 3. Basic ground fighting — dominant position is the game

**Dominant body positions (Section I).** The hierarchy, in order of control: **back mount** (behind the opponent, the most dominant position — both hooks in, control of the upper body); **front mount** (astride the face-up opponent's chest or abdomen); **guard** (legs locked around the opponent, controlling him from the bottom — defensive, but a platform for sweeps and submissions); **side control** (perpendicular across the opponent's torso). The hierarchy is the whole game: every positioning move climbs it, every escape descends it, and a finish attempted from a bad position is a gift to the opponent.

**Basic techniques (Section II).** **Body positioning moves (3-5):** the movements between positions — achieving the mount from side control, passing the guard, escaping the mount and the back mount, and the arm trap and roll. Each is taught as a movement, not a static hold, because a fight is won in the transitions. **Finishing moves (3-6):** the techniques that end the fight — rear naked choke; cross-collar choke from mount and guard; bent arm bar from mount and side control; straight arm bar from mount and guard; guillotine choke. Position first, then finish: the choke or arm bar is applied only after the position is secured. **Drills (3-7)** ingrain the positioning moves until they are automatic. **Defense against headlocks (3-8)** closes the chapter — the headlock is the untrained fighter's favorite hold, and the defense (posture, hand fighting, and the escape to a dominant position) is a basic skill, not an advanced one. → `references/basic-ground-fighting.md`

## 4. Advanced ground fighting — attacks from every position

**Advanced attacks (Section I).** **Advanced body positions (4-1)** refine the base positions; **pass the guard (4-2)** is the key advanced skill — the guard is the bottom fighter's fortress, and passing it is the price of the mount. **Attacks from the mount (4-3), from the back mount (4-4), and from the guard (4-5)** each get their own treatment: the mount offers strikes and arm bars; the back mount offers the rear naked choke and its counters; the guard offers sweeps, arm bars, and chokes from the bottom. **Knee mount (4-6)** — one knee in the abdomen, the other foot posted — is the transitional position between side control and mount, mobile enough to strike and stable enough to control. **Leg attacks (4-7)** — ankle locks and knee bars — are the advanced finisher family: powerful, fast, and dangerous to train, so they are taught late and drilled with control.

**Strikes (Section II).** Ground fighting is not strike-free: **passing the guard under strikes (4-8)**, **striking from side control (4-9)**, and **defending against strikes in the guard (4-10)**. The lesson is that strikes and grappling are one system — strikes open the guard pass, the guard pass creates the mount, the mount creates the finish. A fighter who treats striking and grappling as separate arts loses to one who combines them. → `references/advanced-ground-fighting.md`

## 5. Takedowns and throws — getting the fight to the ground

**Breakfalls (5-1)** come first: a soldier who cannot fall safely cannot be thrown in training, and the throw is only useful if the thrower stays on his feet and in control. **Closing the distance and achieving the clinch (5-2)** is the bridge from striking range to grappling range — the manual treats the clinch as the place fights are most often decided, because whoever controls the clinch controls the takedown, and the takedown decides who is on top.

**Throw and takedown techniques (5-3):** hip throws, shoulder throws, leg trips and sweeps, taught as whole-body movements driven by hips and legs. **Defending against headlocks (5-4)** reappears here in the takedown context — the headlock is often the entry to a throw, and defending it is defending the takedown. **Takedowns from against a wall (5-5)** cover the urban reality: walls remove the space to sprawl or circle, so the techniques change. **Double leg attack (5-6)** and **single leg attacks (5-7)** are the wrestling core — level change, penetration step, drive through, finish to a dominant position. **Attacks from the rear (5-8)** close the chapter: the rear is the safest place to attack from and the hardest to defend, which is why the back mount sits at the top of the hierarchy. → `references/takedowns-strikes-weapons.md`

## 6. Strikes — the striking range, kept short

**Arm strikes (6-1):** punches, elbow strikes, and hand strikes (knife-hand, heel-palm, hammer-fist), with the manual's standing preference for the body's natural weapons over the fragile fist. **Punching combinations (6-2):** strikes are combined — one opens the target, the next hits it. **Kicks (6-3):** low kicks to knee, shin, and groin; high kicks are discouraged for the same reason as in 1992 — balance sacrificed for a visible target. **Transition between ranges (6-4):** the fighter must move deliberately between striking, clinching, and grappling ranges, and the transition is trained as its own skill — the 1992 manual's "bridging the gap" idea, now expressed in the four-range language (projectile, striking, clinching, grappling) that the 2017 manual would make central. → `references/takedowns-strikes-weapons.md`

## 7. Handheld weapons — bayonet, knife, and expedients

**Angles of attack (7-1).** Weapon attacks are taught as numbered angles — the common language for attack, defense, and counter, drilled by the numbers before they are drilled live.

**Rifle with fixed bayonet (7-2):** thrust, slash, butt-stroke, and parry as instinctive patterns; the bayonet fighter's goal is to close, control, and finish — the same position logic as the ground fight, applied standing. **Bayonet/knife (7-3)** and the **knife-against-rifle sequence (7-4)** cover the knife as a primary weapon and the desperate case of a knife against a longer weapon. **Advanced weapons techniques and training (7-5)** integrate weapons work at combat speed against resistance.

**Field-expedient weapons (Section II).** **Entrenching tool (7-6), three-foot stick (7-7), six-foot pole (7-8).** The 2002 manual drops the 1992 edition's three-foot rope but keeps the principle: the tool extends reach and multiplies force, and the angles of attack and vital targets still apply. A soldier who understands angles and targets can fight with whatever is in his hand. → `references/takedowns-strikes-weapons.md`

## 8. Standing defense — when you are the one attacked

**Unarmed opponent (Section I).** **Defense against chokes (8-1):** tuck the chin, protect the neck, turn into the choke, attack the choking arm's leverage, break the base — the counter starts the instant the choke is felt. **Defense against bear hugs (8-2):** free the arms, drop weight, attack balance, and turn the hug into a takedown or escape.

**Armed opponent (Section II).** **Defense against an armed opponent (8-3)** states the general rule: gain a weapon or create distance first; engagement is the last resort. **Defense against a knife (8-4):** control the weapon arm, never the blade; move off the line of attack; attack the arm's structure. **Unarmed defense against a rifle with fixed bayonet (8-5):** get inside the weapon's effective arc, control the rifle, strike vital targets. The manual is blunt throughout: unarmed against a weapon is a desperate position, and the techniques are damage control, not parity. → `references/standing-defense-and-group-tactics.md`

## 9. Group tactics — more than one fighter on a side

**Lethal force scenarios (Section I).** **Range (9-1), control (9-2), finishing (9-3):** in a lethal group fight, the same logic scales — control range, control the opponent's body, finish, and move to the next threat. Do not get tied to one opponent while another closes.

**Restrictive force scenarios (Section II).** **Two against one (9-4), three against two (9-5), parity (9-6), one against two (9-7), two against three (9-8).** The numbers change the tactics: with the advantage, split and isolate; at parity, pair off and finish fast; outnumbered, keep moving, use one opponent as a shield against the other, and break contact the moment a finish is not available. The one-against-two section is the most practically valuable: the lone fighter's goal is to make it two one-against-one fights in sequence, never one one-against-two fight. → `references/standing-defense-and-group-tactics.md`

## 10. How the three combatives editions differ — a field guide

The three Army combatives manuals form a lineage, and knowing which one you are reading matters more than knowing any single technique:

- **FM 21-150 (1992)** is range-based: close, medium, long. Its distinctive material is the vital-targets striking system, sentry removal, pugil training, and the bayonet assault course. It has no dominant-position system and no guard, mount, or submission hierarchy — ground fighting appears as throws, chokes, and grappling, not as a positional game.
- **FM 3-25.150 (2002, this manual)** is the bridge: it introduces the dominant-position ground system (back mount, mount, guard, side control; positioning moves; chokes and arm bars) and keeps the 1992 material — rifle-bayonet, field-expedient weapons, standing defense, group tactics — alongside it. Its competition appendix is the origin of the formal program, but it is a sketch next to the 2017 version.
- **TC 3-25.150 (2017, current)** rebuilds the system around fight strategy by range (projectile, striking, clinching, grappling) and the dominant-position hierarchy, with a full competition program, intermediate and advanced ground fighting as separate progressions, and detailed training-program guidance. It trims or reorganizes much of the 2002 manual's weapons and standing-defense material.

Practical rule: if you are building a modern training program, build it on the 2017 manual and use this skill as the historical source for the standing-defense and group-tactics chapters and for the original statement of the ground system. If you are studying how the Army's thinking changed — from striking ranges to positional grappling — read the 1992 and 2002 manuals side by side: the 2002 manual is where the ground game arrives, and the 2017 manual is where it matures.

## Appendices — situational training and competitions

**Appendix A — Situational training.** Scenario-based training that puts techniques into mission context: sentry scenarios, detainee handling, restrictive-force situations. The bridge between the drill field and the fight.

**Appendix B — Competitions.** The competition program — rules, scoring, and refereeing — as a training tool: competition is the principal motivational device and the closest safe approximation to a fully resistant opponent. The 2017 manual would expand this into a formal program; the 2002 appendix is its origin. → `references/standing-defense-and-group-tactics.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a combatives technique from this manual, answer in its terms: name the dominant position (or the range, for striking/weapons), the positioning move that gets there, and the finish — position first, then finish. When asked how it differs from 1992, say: the 2002 manual replaces the range framework with the dominant-position ground system while keeping bayonet, expedient weapons, standing defense, and group tactics. When asked how it differs from 2017, say: same system, earlier and less refined form — the 2017 TC 3-25.150 is current, with a fuller competition program and reorganized advanced material. Cite 2017 as current for training programs.

## Operating Rules

1. **Position first, then finish.** A choke or arm bar from a bad position is a gift.
2. **The hierarchy is the game.** Back mount > mount > side control/knee mount > guard; every move climbs or defends it.
3. **Strikes and grappling are one system.** Strikes open the pass; the pass creates the mount; the mount creates the finish.
4. **Control the weapon arm, never the blade.** Unarmed against a weapon is damage control, not parity.
5. **Outnumbered, sequence the fight.** Make it two one-on-ones, never one one-on-two.
6. **Current doctrine governs.** TC 3-25.150 (2017) is the standard; use this skill for the original dominant-position system, standing defense, and group tactics.
