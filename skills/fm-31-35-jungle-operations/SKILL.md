# FM 31-35 Jungle Operations

## Purpose

FM 31-35, *Jungle Operations* (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 26 September 1969, 132 pages) — the Vietnam-era successor to FM 31-30, written at the height of the war in Southeast Asia. It supersedes FM 31-30 of 23 September 1965. Where FM 31-30 was a training manual as much as an operations manual, FM 31-35 is the tighter, operations-first doctrine: six chapters covering the jungle, combat and combat support, combat service support, jungle operations, and special training requirements — plus the first treatment in this lineage of riverine operations and stability operations.

**Supersession note — read this first.** FM 31-35 was itself superseded by **FM 90-5, *Jungle Operations* (16 August 1982)**. For current doctrine, the governing reference is **MCTP 12-10C, *Jungle Operations* (2020)**, already a skill in this vault; where the older manuals disagree with MCTP 12-10C, follow the newer publication. What remains durable in FM 31-35 is its regional treatment of the world's jungles, its operations framework (centralized planning, decentralized execution), its riverine and stability-operations chapters, and its training-priority lists — which read as a checklist for any unit deploying to the jungle. What is dated is its organization and equipment (1969-era weapons, radios, and aviation), its CBR material, and its medical specifics, which should be checked against current guidance.

**Sibling note — the four jungle manuals in this vault.** FM 31-30 (1965) is the foundation and the training manual: the fullest treatment of heat acclimation, the psychology of jungle fear, and the recommended training program. FM 31-35 (1969) is the mid-war tightening: it drops much of FM 31-30's training bulk, adds regional chapters on American, African, and Oriental jungles, and adds riverine operations and stability operations that FM 31-30 never had. FM 90-5 (1982) is the post-war rewrite, "How to Fight," with a 25th Infantry Division voice, a survival chapter, and far more small-unit tactical detail. FM 31-36 (TEST) (1968), despite its vault label, is not a jungle manual at all — it is *Night Operations*, and its skill is filed under that title. Read FM 31-35 when you want the 1969 doctrine in its most compact form.

## 1. The Jungle — Three Regions

**1.1. The jungle environment.** Chapter 2 covers the jungle generally, then treats three regions separately — a structure none of the sibling manuals has. **American jungles** (Central and South America, including the Amazon basin, where the rainy lowlands greatly affect operations), **Africa**, and **Oriental jungles** (Southeast Asia and the Pacific). Each region gets its own climate, vegetation, terrain, and disease picture, because the manual's point is that "the jungle" is not one place: the problems of the Amazon are not the problems of the Mekong Delta.

**1.2. Disease is regional, and malaria is the killer.** Across all three regions, the mosquito is the chief carrier of malaria, "the most widespread and deadly disease" of the jungle; malaria is particularly severe in the northwestern corner of Burma, and is prevalent throughout Southeast Asia. Yellow fever, dengue (breakbone fever), dysentery, and other insect-borne diseases recur in the regional chapters, along with the observation that syphilis and other diseases are very common in many districts with little done to check their spread. The medical lesson is the same one FM 31-30 taught: disease, not the enemy, is the standing casualty producer, and prevention — nets, repellents, anti-malaria medicine, field sanitation — is a command responsibility.

**1.4. Movement and distance.** Chapter 2, Section VII treats movement and distance as a planning problem of its own: in the jungle, distance is measured in time and effort, not miles, and a movement estimate that ignores vegetation, slope, and the state of the trails will be wrong by hours. The manual's rule is the same one FM 31-30 stated — plan by time, allow for the enemy to reinforce while you are still moving, and never promise an arrival time the jungle will not keep. → `references/jungle-regions-and-terrain.md`

## 2. Jungle Operations — The Framework

**2.1. Apply standard principles, modified by the environment.** "Standard and current combat principles must be applied to a jungle environment with emphasis placed on the modification to those principles that are necessitated by the environment." Jungle operations are characterized by restricted maneuver, slow tempo of operations, close combat under conditions of extremely limited visibility, and difficulty in providing logistical support. Special operations — patrolling, operations from forward positions, ambush, and other forms of close combat — are common.

**2.2. Centralized planning, decentralized execution.** Because of the difficulties of command, control, and communications, jungle operations require centralized planning and decentralized execution. Commanders must use mission-type orders that give maximum latitude to subordinates, and the mission must be understood by all personnel. Movement from friendly positions to an objective may take longer than estimated, and the enemy may take advantage of this to reinforce with a unit initially considered too far away — so commanders must not conclude that the enemy cannot attack through supposedly impenetrable areas or reinforce in time to affect the mission.

**2.3. Observation and fields of fire.** In dense jungle, observation and fields of fire are limited by undergrowth. In rain forests, although observation into the forest from outside or above may be nonexistent, fair observation and good fields of fire for a reasonable distance at ground level are possible where the canopy blocks sunlight and prevents undergrowth. Where old forests have burned or been destroyed, secondary growth seriously limits both. In open areas, jungle grass sometimes grows several feet over a man's head. Dense foliage affords excellent concealment from ground and air observation; cover is provided by surface irregularities such as ravines and gullies; extensive ground-level roots can make digging very difficult, requiring positions above ground level.

**2.5. The mission must be understood.** The manual returns repeatedly to one point: because control is so difficult in the jungle, the mission must be understood by all personnel, not just by the commander. Mission-type orders give subordinates latitude, but latitude without understanding is just drift — and in the jungle, drift gets units lost, ambushed, or out of supporting distance. The test of a jungle order is whether the smallest unit that must execute it understands what it is to do and why.

## 3. Combat and Combat Support

**3.1. The combined-arms picture.** Chapter 3 runs through every arm in jungle conditions: infantry (the decisive arm — the manual is explicit that the fight is won by dismounted infantry), armor (limited by terrain, used against definitely located targets), artillery (fires adjusted by sound where visibility prevents observation), air defense and air defense artillery, naval gunfire and tactical air support, Army aviation, CBR operations, engineers, communications, intelligence, and military police. The recurring theme is adaptation: every arm works, but none works the way it works in open terrain, and fire support is hard to control — timing, communications, and observation all degrade in the jungle.

**3.2. Communications.** Jungle communications get a full section of their own — the first in this lineage — because the canopy and the terrain defeat line-of-sight radios and wire is hard to lay and maintain. The manual's answer is redundancy: multiple means, relay stations, and strict communications discipline, with the expectation that units will fight for periods with no contact with higher headquarters.

**3.3. Intelligence and the enemy.** The commander needs timely, accurate information on enemy location, strength, disposition, composition, and activities, and in the jungle most of it comes from patrolling. The manual warns against mirror-imaging: the enemy in the jungle is not a lesser version of a conventional force, and his ability to move through supposedly impenetrable terrain, to live off the land, and to choose the time and place of contact is the central intelligence problem. → `references/combat-and-combat-support.md`

**3.4. Engineers, CBR, and the rest of the fight.** The engineers' jungle work is route and landing-zone construction, obstacle reduction, and water supply — the manual gives them a full section because in the jungle the engineers decide whether anyone else can move at all. CBR operations get a section of their own, covering the employment and defense problems that jungle vegetation, humidity, and limited observation create for chemical, biological, and radiological weapons. The military police section covers traffic control on the few usable routes, prisoner handling, and security of headquarters and installations — unglamorous work that the jungle makes harder, because every route is a chokepoint and every installation is surrounded by concealment. → `references/combat-and-combat-support.md`

## 4. Combat Service Support

**4.1. Supply, maintenance, medical, transportation.** Chapter 4 covers supply, maintenance, medical service, transportation, and personnel management. The jungle's logistics problem is the same one FM 31-30 stated — anticipate requirements well in advance, reconnoiter supply routes and water points continuously, develop alternates — but FM 31-35 adds the headquarters problem: headquarters should be sited in an inconspicuous place, with provision for weapons, ammunition, and enemy contact, and a reduced headquarters may be necessary where the jungle will not support a full one. Waterways and air resupply are treated as primary means, not expedients, and the wet season — which makes rivers navigable — can be operationally advantageous for supply and evacuation.

**4.2. Medical.** The medical section is shorter than FM 31-30's but carries the same message: field sanitation, personal hygiene, and disease prevention are the medical service's main jungle work, and evacuation planning must account for the jungle's distances in time, not miles.

## 5. Riverine Operations and Stability Operations

**5.1. Riverine operations.** Chapter 5, Section VII is new in this manual. Jungle riverine operations exploit the rivers that are often the only lines of communication through the jungle — the middle sector (central valley) and the riverine environment generally, with its own tactical considerations for movement, fire support, and base siting. The manual treats rivers as both highway and obstacle: they carry supply and troops, and they channelize movement into predictable routes that the enemy can watch and ambush.

**5.2. Stability operations.** Section VIII treats stability operations — the population-centered work of village security, civic action, and counterinsurgency that FM 31-30 touched only in its village-clearing paragraph. The manual's framing is that jungle operations in 1969 were inseparable from the political fight for the population, and that influencing the population — offering assistance, especially medical assistance — is a combat task, not an afterthought. → `references/jungle-operations-framework.md`

## 6. Special Training Requirements

**6.1. Training priorities — first priority.** Chapter 6 lists the subjects a unit must be proficient in before jungle operations: (1) patrolling; (2) day and night land navigation; (3) target detection and engagement; (4) ambush and counterambush; (5) perimeter defense in jungle terrain; (6) immediate action drills; (7) village search and seizure operations.

**6.2. Training priorities — second priority.** Subjects to be integrated into all individual and unit jungle training: jungle living; field sanitation and personal hygiene; supply and evacuation techniques; boat drill and use of jungle waterways; guerrilla weapons, mines, and traps; airmobile movements; tracking techniques; terrain orientation; individual maintenance of weapons, clothing, and equipment.

**6.3. Jungle living and acclimatization.** Actual jungle conditions are the ideal training situation; where jungle terrain is not available, dense woods, river beds, swamps, marshes, or thickets can be used, and night fighting techniques simulate the reduced ranges, restricted movement, and control difficulties of the jungle. Commanders must give troops **a minimum of 7 days after arrival in the tropics** to become acclimated to heat and humidity. Acclimation is automatic, begins the first day, and depends on the temperature change between climates; the body remains acclimated for one to two weeks after departure. The foreboding appearance of the jungle, oppressive humidity and heat, unfamiliar noises, and loneliness intensify fear of the unknown — the soldier must be conditioned to realize "that the jungle is neutral and can be made to work for him against the enemy."

**6.4. Heat disorders.** Heat exhaustion is caused by excessive loss of water and salt; during normal operations soldiers should drink **6 to 15 canteens of water a day**. Basic rules: drink plenty of water; become acclimated; use extra salt with food and water; eat the heavy meal during the cool of the day. Full treatment of heat exhaustion and heat stroke is referred to FM 21-10.

**6.5. Individual clothing and equipment.** The combat uniform (fatigues) is adequate as issued; jungle fatigues are best, but other fatigues must fit loosely, jacket worn outside the trousers, and clothing should not be excessively faded from the original olive green, since worn or threadbare clothing will not protect against insect bites, brambles, and direct sunlight as well as new cloth. Troops should avoid wearing underwear, as it may irritate the skin and reduce ventilation. The lightweight jungle boot with cleated sole is preferred; boots should be taken off at every opportunity to dry feet and boots and apply foot powder. A utility hat is recommended where stealth is required, because of the noise the steel helmet makes in dense vegetation. Gloves protect against thorns, brambles, insect bites, and snake bites, and against blisters from prolonged machete use and burns when rappelling rapidly — but should not be worn when not needed, since they soften the skin. The machete is "an indispensable aid to the jungle fighter" for movement through thick jungle and for construction, and an effective weapon as well as a cutting tool. Each soldier should carry a bayonet; a hunting knife or pocket knife can be used in addition. → `references/training-jungle-living-and-tracking.md`

## Output Contract

When answering a jungle question from FM 31-35, give the procedure first, then the numbers that make it work (7-day minimum acclimation, 6–15 canteens of water a day), then the cautions. State the priority order explicitly: acclimation and heat discipline first, then movement and control, then the fighting technique. Name the region when the advice is regional — American, African, and Oriental jungles differ — and say which manual it comes from when a sibling manual differs. Where current doctrine (MCTP 12-10C, 2020) differs from FM 31-35, say so and follow the newer manual. Cite the chapter the procedure comes from; quote the manual sparingly and briefly.

## Operating Rules

1. **The jungle is neutral.** It can be made to work for you against the enemy — once fear is trained out.
2. **Seven days minimum.** No unit is committed to hard jungle work before a minimum of 7 days' acclimation in the tropics.
3. **Centralized planning, decentralized execution.** Mission-type orders, maximum latitude to subordinates, the mission understood by all.
4. **Patrol to know.** In the jungle, patrolling is the primary intelligence source — never assume the enemy cannot move through "impenetrable" terrain.
5. **Rivers are highways and traps.** Use waterways for supply and movement, and expect the enemy to watch them.
6. **Current doctrine wins.** MCTP 12-10C (2020) governs jungle operations today; FM 31-35 is the 1969 doctrine, not the authority.
