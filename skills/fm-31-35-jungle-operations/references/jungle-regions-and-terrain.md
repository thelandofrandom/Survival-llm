# Jungle Regions and Terrain — FM 31-35

## Three Regions

Chapter 2 treats the world's jungles in three regions — a structure none of the sibling manuals has:

- **American jungles** (Central and South America, including the Amazon basin, where the rainy lowlands greatly affect operations).
- **Africa.**
- **Oriental jungles** (Southeast Asia and the Pacific).

Each region gets its own climate, vegetation, terrain, and disease picture. "The jungle" is not one place: the problems of the Amazon are not the problems of the Mekong Delta.

## Disease

- The mosquito is the chief carrier of malaria, "the most widespread and deadly disease" of the jungle; malaria is particularly severe in the northwestern corner of Burma and prevalent throughout Southeast Asia.
- Yellow fever, dengue (breakbone fever), dysentery, and other insect-borne diseases recur across the regions.
- Syphilis and other diseases are very common in many districts, with little done to check their spread.
- Prevention — nets, repellents, anti-malaria medicine, field sanitation — is a command responsibility. Disease, not the enemy, is the standing casualty producer.

## Military Aspects of the Terrain

Jungle operations are characterized by restricted maneuver, slow tempo, close combat under extremely limited visibility, and difficulty in providing logistical support.

**Key terrain** in jungle areas includes trails, roads, bridges, streambeds, and communications centers. High ground is selected as key terrain but for varying reasons — a hill may be the only feature affording a suitable area for defense; a river through otherwise impenetrable forest may be a key terrain feature; a helicopter landing zone can fall into the category.

**Observation and fields of fire:** in dense jungle, limited by undergrowth. In rain forests, observation into the forest from outside or above may be nonexistent, yet fair observation and good fields of fire at ground level are possible where the canopy blocks sunlight and prevents undergrowth. Where old forests have burned or been destroyed, secondary growth seriously limits both. In open areas, jungle grass sometimes grows several feet over a man's head.

**Cover and concealment:** dense foliage affords excellent concealment from ground and air observation; cover comes from surface irregularities such as ravines and gullies. Extensive ground-level roots can make digging very difficult, requiring positions above ground level.

**Obstacles:** in older forests there is generally no obstacle to foot movement other than incidental swampy areas, occasional fallen trees, and vines; secondary growth, swamps, and rivers are the serious obstacles.
