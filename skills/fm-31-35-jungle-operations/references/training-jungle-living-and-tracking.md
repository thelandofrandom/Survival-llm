# Training, Jungle Living, and Tracking — FM 31-35

## Training Priorities — First Priority

Subjects a unit must be proficient in before jungle operations:

1. Patrolling.
2. Day and night land navigation.
3. Target detection and engagement.
4. Ambush and counterambush.
5. Perimeter defense in jungle terrain.
6. Immediate action drills.
7. Village search and seizure operations.

## Training Priorities — Second Priority

Subjects integrated into all individual and unit jungle training: jungle living; field sanitation and personal hygiene; supply and evacuation techniques; boat drill and use of jungle waterways; guerrilla weapons, mines, and traps; airmobile movements; tracking techniques; terrain orientation; individual maintenance of weapons, clothing, and equipment.

## Jungle Living and Acclimatization

- Actual jungle conditions are the ideal training situation; where jungle terrain is unavailable, dense woods, river beds, swamps, marshes, or thickets can be used, and night fighting techniques simulate the reduced ranges, restricted movement, and control difficulties of the jungle.
- Commanders must give troops **a minimum of 7 days after arrival in the tropics** to become acclimated to heat and humidity. Acclimation is automatic, begins the first day, and depends on the temperature change between climates; the body remains acclimated for one to two weeks after departure.
- The foreboding appearance of the jungle, oppressive humidity and heat, unfamiliar noises, and loneliness intensify fear of the unknown — the soldier must be conditioned to realize "that the jungle is neutral and can be made to work for him against the enemy."
- Training should demand individual self-reliance, teamwork, skill, and determination, with control decentralized and reliance placed on small-unit leaders and the individual soldier.

## Heat Disorders

- Heat exhaustion is caused by excessive loss of water and salt; during normal operations soldiers should drink **6 to 15 canteens of water a day**.
- Basic rules: drink plenty of water; become acclimated; use extra salt with food and water; eat the heavy meal during the cool of the day.
- Full treatment of heat exhaustion and heat stroke is referred to FM 21-10.

## Individual Clothing and Equipment

- **Combat uniform (fatigues):** adequate as issued; jungle fatigues best, but other fatigues must fit loosely, jacket worn outside the trousers. Clothing should not be excessively faded from the original olive green — worn or threadbare clothing will not protect against insect bites, brambles, and direct sunlight as well as new cloth. Avoid wearing underwear: it may irritate the skin and reduce ventilation.
- **Footwear:** lightweight jungle boot with cleated sole preferred; boots taken off at every opportunity to dry feet and boots and apply foot powder.
- **Headgear:** utility hat recommended where stealth is required, because of the noise the steel helmet makes in dense vegetation.
- **Gloves:** protect against thorns, brambles, insect bites, snake bites, blisters from prolonged machete use, and burns when rappelling rapidly — but should not be worn when not needed, since they soften the skin.
- **Machete:** "an indispensable aid to the jungle fighter" for movement through thick jungle and for construction; an effective weapon as well as a cutting tool.
- **Bayonet:** each soldier should carry one; a hunting knife or pocket knife can be used in addition.
- **Suspenders** issued with the combat pack should be worn when the belt is worn; the combat pack should not be worn unless the mission dictates.

## Tracking

Tracking techniques are a second-priority training subject. As in FM 31-30, the signs are footprints, the direction of grass, leaves, or sticks kicked up by feet, unnatural formations of vines and dead grass, and sap or fluid exuding from cuts — read against what the enemy is likely to be wearing and doing. FM 31-35 treats tracking as a skill to be trained, not a talent to be assumed.
