# Infantry Rifle Platoon and Squad

## Purpose

ATP 3-21.8 *Infantry Rifle Platoon and Squad* (Department of the Army, January 2024, incorporating Change 1, June 2025) is the current Army techniques publication for how the infantry rifle platoon and squad fight — the smallest maneuver units that actually close with and destroy the enemy. It is for platoon leaders, platoon sergeants, squad leaders, and team leaders, and for anyone who needs to understand how a rifle platoon is organized, how it plans, how it moves, how it attacks and defends, how it patrols, and how it sustains itself. Its core idea: the platoon wins by disciplined small-unit procedures — troop leading procedures, battle drills, formations, and control measures — executed the same way every time, so that under fire nobody has to invent the next step.

Key grounding facts: the rifle platoon is three rifle squads plus a weapons squad plus a headquarters element; the rifle squad is nine Soldiers (a squad leader and two four-man fire teams); the fire team is a team leader, a rifleman, a grenadier (M320), and an automatic rifleman (M249); the weapons squad brings M240 machine guns and Javelin anti-tank missiles. The planning engine is TLP — eight steps from receiving the mission to supervising and refining — and the mission variables are METT-TC (I). The order format is the five-paragraph OPORD: Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment, Command and Signal.

## 1. Organization (Chapter 1)

**1.1 The rifle platoon.** The infantry rifle platoon is the basic maneuver element of the infantry company. It consists of a platoon headquarters (platoon leader, platoon sergeant, radiotelephone operator, forward observer, and medic), three rifle squads, and a weapons squad. The platoon leader is the primary planner: he receives the warning order, operation order, and fragmentary order from the company and develops the platoon plan. The platoon sergeant is the platoon's second-in-command and its sustainment and discipline engine — he assists with the OPORD, determines Soldier load and load echeloning, prepares rehearsals, and runs priorities of work. The forward observer and RTO extend the platoon's reach to fires and communications.

**1.2 The rifle squad — nine Soldiers.** The rifle squad is the smallest element that can maneuver independently. It is a squad leader and two fire teams of four:
- **Fire team:** team leader, rifleman, grenadier (M320 grenade launcher), automatic rifleman (M249 light machine gun). The team leader controls his team's fires and movement; the automatic rifleman is the team's base of fire; the grenadier adds high-explosive reach; the rifleman is the flexible man — security, ammunition bearer, and second set of eyes.
- **Squad leader:** commands the squad, positions himself where he can best control it (usually between the two fire teams), and fights the squad as two mutually supporting teams.

**1.3 The weapons squad.** The weapons squad gives the platoon its heavy punch: M240B/M240L medium machine guns (bipod and tripod mounted) and Javelin close-combat missile systems. The platoon leader employs the weapons squad as a whole (a base of fire for the rifle squads) or attaches its elements to rifle squads. Machine-gun employment is a deliberate decision — guns are positioned for interlocking fires, grazing fire across the engagement area, and a final protective line.

**1.4 Duties and responsibilities.** Chapter 1 spells out every position's duties, from platoon leader to rifleman, and the habitual attachments the platoon may receive (engineers, mortars, snipers, medics, forward observers). The point is that no one guesses their job: the platoon leader maneuvers squads and synchronizes efforts; the platoon sergeant sustains and disciplines; squad leaders fight squads; team leaders fight fire teams; every Soldier knows his battle position, his sector of fire, and his buddy.

**1.5 Command and control.** The platoon leader looks ahead to the next "move" of the platoon while the platoon sergeant manages the current one. Control is exercised through orders, hand-and-arm signals, radio, and — when those fail — by example and position. The platoon leader positions himself where he can see and influence the decisive point, not where he is safest.

## 2. Planning and Preparing (Chapter 2)

**2.1 The operations process and TLP.** Small-unit leaders plan with troop leading procedures — eight steps, executed in parallel with higher headquarters' planning (parallel planning) so the platoon is not waiting for the company order to start moving:

1. **Receive the mission** — from the company WARNORD, OPORD, or FRAGORD. Analyze it: task, purpose, and the higher commander's intent (two levels up).
2. **Issue a warning order** — in the five-paragraph OPORD format, with minimal information, to alert the platoon and start parallel planning.
3. **Make a tentative plan** — the bulk of the work: mission analysis using METT-TC (Mission, Enemy, Terrain and weather, Troops and support available, Time available, Civil considerations, plus Informational considerations integrated throughout), course-of-action development, and analysis.
4. **Initiate movement** — start the platoon moving (or preparing to move) while planning continues; time is the scarcest resource.
5. **Conduct reconnaissance** — leader's reconnaissance of the objective, routes, and positions; confirm or change the tentative plan.
6. **Complete the plan** — finalize the course of action, control measures, and coordination.
7. **Issue the operation order** — the five-paragraph OPORD, delivered so every leader can repeat it back.
8. **Supervise and refine** — rehearsals, inspections, and adjustments until execution.

**2.2 METT-TC (I).** The mission variables: Mission; Enemy (composition, disposition, capabilities, most likely and most dangerous courses of action); Terrain and weather (OAKOC — Observation and fields of fire, Avenues of approach, Key terrain, Obstacles, Cover and concealment); Troops and support available; Time available; Civil considerations; and Informational considerations integrated into the other variables. The leader who skips METT-TC analysis is planning on hope.

**2.3 The five-paragraph OPORD.** Situation (enemy, friendly, attachments/detachments, civil considerations); Mission (task and purpose, in one sentence); Execution (commander's intent, concept of the operation, scheme of maneuver, tasks to subordinate units, coordinating instructions); Sustainment (logistics, medical, personnel); Command and Signal (command, control, communications). The WARNORD follows the same format with minimal information. A FRAGORD changes the OPORD without rewriting it.

**2.4 Rehearsals.** The plan is rehearsed — full dress, reduced force, or at minimum a rock drill or map rehearsal — before execution. The rehearsal is where the plan breaks in a way that can still be fixed. The platoon sergeant assists with preparation for rehearsals and priorities of work.

## 3. Movement and Maneuver (Chapter 3)

**3.1 Route selection and navigation.** The leader selects routes by METT-TC: covered and concealed where possible, avoiding likely enemy observation and obstacles, with rally points and checkpoints. Navigation is by map, compass, and terrain association — the leader sketches legs, records azimuths and distances, and briefs the route so any man can lead it.

**3.2 Movement formations.** The squad and platoon move in formations that balance control, security, and firepower:
- **Column/file:** the most controlled formation; used in restricted terrain, at night, and when moving through dense vegetation. Fire teams in column behind each other.
- **Line:** maximum firepower forward; used when contact is imminent and the enemy is to the front.
- **Wedge:** the fire team's default — two men forward, two back, giving firepower forward and to the flanks. The squad's default is column with fire teams in wedge.
- **Echelon:** firepower weighted to one flank; used when the threat is from that side.
- **Vee:** firepower forward and to both flanks (platoon level).

**3.3 Movement techniques.** Three techniques, chosen by the likelihood of contact:
- **Traveling:** all elements move continuously; used when contact is not likely. Fastest, least secure.
- **Traveling overwatch:** the lead element moves continuously while the trail element moves as needed to maintain overwatch — pauses to observe, then bounds forward. Used when contact is possible.
- **Bounding overwatch:** one element is always stationary, overwatching, while the other bounds forward — successive bounds (same element leads each time) or alternate bounds (elements leapfrog). Used when contact is expected. Slowest, most secure.
- **Movement during limited visibility:** tighter intervals, more frequent halts, navigation by compass and pace count, noise and light discipline absolute.

**3.4 Maneuver.** Maneuver is movement relative to the enemy to gain a positional advantage. The platoon maneuvers with a base-of-fire element (fixes the enemy with fire) and a bounding/maneuver element (moves to the flank or rear to destroy him). The platoon as the reserve is held back to exploit success or reinforce failure. The leader's rule: fire without maneuver is suppression without decision; maneuver without fire is a target.

## 4. Offense (Chapter 4)

**4.1 Characteristics of the offense.** The offense is the decisive form of combat: surprise, concentration, tempo, and audacity. The platoon attacks to destroy the enemy, seize terrain, or both. The offense is conducted as a movement to contact or an attack.

**4.2 Forms of maneuver.** Five forms, chosen by METT-TC:
- **Envelopment:** the main effort attacks the enemy's flank or rear, avoiding his strength. The preferred form when the flank is accessible.
- **Turning movement:** a deep envelopment that forces the enemy to abandon his position or fight at a disadvantage — it turns him out of his defenses rather than assaulting them.
- **Infiltration:** small elements move through gaps in the enemy's position to attack from the rear. Slow, stealthy, high payoff.
- **Penetration:** a concentrated attack through a narrow front to rupture the enemy's defense and roll it up from inside.
- **Frontal attack:** a direct attack against the enemy's front. The least preferred — it attacks his strength — and used only when no other form is feasible or when fixing the enemy for another element.

**4.3 Movement to contact.** The platoon moves to gain contact with the enemy on favorable terms. The sequence: organize for contact (lead element, main body, reserve), establish control measures, move with the appropriate movement technique, and on contact, execute the battle drill — return fire, seek cover, report, maneuver. The platoon leader's example sequence in the manual walks through the full operation: planning, movement, contact, development of the situation, and the decision to attack or bypass.

**4.4 The attack.** Hasty attack (limited planning, exploiting a fleeting opportunity) versus deliberate attack (full planning, rehearsals, and preparation). The attack is organized with an assault element, a support (base-of-fire) element, and a breach element when obstacles must be crossed. Control measures — phase lines, objectives, boundaries, assault positions, and the line of departure — keep the platoon oriented. The sequence: move to the assault position, establish the base of fire, breach, assault through the objective, consolidate, and reorganize. Operations during limited visibility use the same sequence with tighter control and more rehearsal.

**4.5 Common offensive planning considerations.** Command and control (who controls what, and what happens when the leader falls); movement and maneuver; intelligence (what the enemy is doing now); fires (mortars, artillery, close air support — planned and on call); sustainment (ammunition, water, casualty evacuation); protection (security, air defense, CBRN). The leader plans all six, not just the maneuver.

## 5. Defense (Chapter 5)

**5.1 Characteristics of the defense.** The defense is fought to defeat an attack, gain time, economize forces, or retain key terrain. Its characteristics: preparation, security, disruption, massing of effects, and flexibility. A good defense is not a wall — it is a killing system.

**5.2 Area defense.** The platoon defends a position, organized with primary, alternate, and supplementary positions; sectors of fire that interlock; and a final protective line (FPL) — the line of machine-gun fire that is the last barrier before the position is overrun. Position selection weighs fields of fire, cover and concealment, obstacles, and avenues of approach. The area-defense sequence in the manual walks through occupation, preparation, and the fight: engage at maximum effective range, shift fires as the enemy closes, and fight the close battle with grenades and bayonets if necessary.

**5.3 Retrograde.** Three forms: **delay** (trade space for time, inflicting maximum damage while falling back); **withdrawal** (a planned disengagement, usually at night); and **retirement** (a move away from the enemy when not in contact). Retrograde is the hardest operation — it looks like retreat, and it requires the most discipline.

**5.4 The engagement area.** The platoon leader develops the engagement area in seven steps: identify likely enemy avenues of approach; determine the enemy's scheme of maneuver; determine where to kill the enemy; emplace weapons systems; plan and integrate obstacles; plan and integrate indirect fires; rehearse. The engagement area is where fires, obstacles, and maneuver converge — the enemy is canalized into it, fixed in it, and destroyed in it.

**5.5 Fighting positions.** Characteristics: cover (protection from fire) and concealment (protection from observation); sectors of fire; mutual support; overhead cover where time allows. Hasty positions (foxholes dug in minutes, using natural cover) versus deliberate positions (dug in, revetted, with overhead cover and grenade sumps). Machine-gun positions get firing platforms and are sited for grazing fire; Javelin positions are sited for long-range engagement with clear backblast areas.

## 6. Tactical Enabling Operations (Chapter 6)

- **Breaching:** fundamentals (suppress, obscure, secure, reduce, assault — SOSRA), breaching organization (support, breach, assault elements), and the breach area. A breach without suppression is a massacre.
- **Relief in place:** one unit replaces another in position, with variations (same positions or new positions), planned so the enemy never sees the seam.
- **Passage of lines:** forward (attacking through a friendly unit) and rearward (withdrawing through a friendly unit), with coordination measures to prevent fratricide.
- **Linkup:** two friendly forces joining, with recognition signals and coordination to prevent fratricide — the most dangerous moment is the first contact.
- **Tactical deception:** making the enemy see what you want him to see, so he prepares for the wrong attack.
- **Security operations:** screen, guard, and cover, with fundamentals (early warning, reaction time, and the security force's own survival).
- **Troop movement:** administrative, tactical, and approach-march movement, by foot, vehicle, or air.

## 7. Patrols and Patrolling (Chapter 7)

**7.1 Overview.** A patrol is a detachment sent out to perform a specific mission. Patrols are combat patrols (raid, ambush, security) or reconnaissance patrols (area, route, zone). Common considerations: the leader's reconnaissance of the route and objective; patrol-base activities (the patrol base is a secure position used to plan, prepare, and launch the patrol, and to rest and reorganize afterward); and the patrol report and debrief on return.

**7.2 Planning and preparation.** Initial coordination (with higher headquarters and adjacent units); essential elements and supporting tasks (the patrol's information requirements — what the patrol must learn or do); pre- and post-departure preparations (rehearsals, inspections, communications checks, and the plan for what happens if the patrol does not return).

**7.3 Combat patrols — raid and ambush.** The raid: a surprise attack on a fixed objective, with a defined withdrawal — get in, destroy the target, get out. The ambush: a surprise attack from concealed positions on a moving or temporarily halted enemy. Ambush types: point ambush (a single kill zone), area ambush (multiple related ambushes), linear ambush (a long kill zone along a route), L-shaped and V-shaped ambushes (kill zones that trap the enemy in interlocking fire). The ambush is organized with an assault element, a support element, and a security element; the kill zone is the heart of it — the place where the enemy is caught, and where every weapon's sector of fire overlaps. The manual's sequence: occupy the ambush site, initiate on the leader's signal, assault through the kill zone, collect intelligence and equipment, withdraw. Security patrols protect the main body by operating forward of it.

**7.4 Reconnaissance patrols.** Area reconnaissance (learn about a specific area or objective), route reconnaissance (learn about a route — its trafficability, obstacles, and enemy presence), and zone reconnaissance (learn about a larger area). Actions on the objective: the patrol observes from a concealed position, records what it sees (SALT reports), and withdraws without being detected — a reconnaissance patrol that fights has usually failed.

**7.5 Post-patrol activities.** Account for weapons and equipment; debrief every member (the patrol leader's memory is not enough); submit the patrol report. The report is the patrol's product — the intelligence that justifies the risk.

## 8. Sustainment and TCCC (Chapter 8)

**8.1 Sustaining the platoon.** The platoon sergeant owns sustainment: supply and field services (classes of supply — ammunition, water, food, fuel, medical, repair parts), distribution and resupply operations (logistics packages, resupply by air or ground), and maintenance (weapons, optics, vehicles, and communications — PMCS as a daily discipline, not a pre-inspection scramble). Plan, prepare, execute, and assess — sustainment is a cycle, not an afterthought.

**8.2 Tactical Combat Casualty Care.** Chapter 8's TCCC section is the platoon's casualty-care doctrine, and it is current (2024) — unlike the 1982 medical handbook, this section can be followed. Three phases:
- **Care under fire:** return fire, move the casualty to cover, stop life-threatening bleeding with a tourniquet. Do not expose yourself or the casualty to further fire to provide care.
- **Tactical field care:** the MARCH sequence — Massive hemorrhage, Airway, Respirations, Circulation, Hypothermia/Head injury — plus pain control, antibiotics, and splinting. Document on the DD Form 1380 TCCC Card.
- **Tactical evacuation care:** continue care during evacuation; the casualty is not "done" until handed to medical personnel.
The platoon's medics and combat lifesavers are the bridge between the rifleman's tourniquet and the aid station. Full TCCC detail belongs to ATP 4-02.11; this manual gives the platoon's employment of it.

## Output Contract

When asked about infantry small-unit tactics, answer from this manual's structure: organization first (9-man squad, 3 rifle squads + weapons squad), then planning (TLP 8 steps, METT-TC (I), five-paragraph OPORD), then movement (formations and the three techniques), then the mission (offense forms of maneuver, defense and the engagement area, patrol types). Give the numbers the manual gives — squad and platoon composition, TLP steps, movement-technique selection criteria — and say when a detail (such as a specific weapons-system range) belongs to a different publication. Never present the 1982 SF Medical Handbook's casualty care as current when this manual's TCCC section and ATP 4-02.11 exist.

## Operating Rules

1. **Procedures are the product.** TLP, battle drills, and control measures are executed the same way every time — that is what makes them work under fire.
2. **The squad is nine Soldiers; the fire team is four.** Never answer an organization question with a different structure.
3. **Movement technique follows contact likelihood.** Traveling, traveling overwatch, bounding overwatch — pick by the threat, not by speed preference.
4. **Fire without maneuver is suppression without decision; maneuver without fire is a target.** Always pair them.
5. **The engagement area is built, not found.** Seven steps, deliberate, rehearsed.
6. **A reconnaissance patrol that fights has usually failed.** Its product is the report.
7. **Sustainment is a cycle.** Plan, prepare, execute, assess — and the platoon sergeant owns it.
