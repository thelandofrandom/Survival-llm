# Environment and Health — ATP 3-90.99 (2021), Chapter 1

## The desert environment

- **Deserts are arid, barren regions; lack of precipitation is the defining characteristic.** Greatest extremes of temperature and weather volatility. Key terrain is largely determined by water or by terrain that restricts movement.
- **Mountain deserts:** scattered ranges, barren hills/mountains, flat basins; avenues of approach often limited to dry riverbeds (wadis) or valleys; vehicle travel severely restricted.
- **Rocky plateau deserts:** extensive flat areas, solid/broken rock at or near surface — good trafficability, poor cover, excellent observation.
- **Polar deserts:** Antarctic and Arctic are the largest deserts on earth, ~5.5 million square miles each. Icecaps (Greenland, Antarctica); glaciers are rivers of ice and snow; overflow ice when a layer ruptures and water flows up.
- **Sandy/dune deserts:** extensive flat sand/gravel areas; steep dune slopes restrict vehicles; lack of foliage and open terrain favor electronic/sensor operations.
- **Weather:** air stability varies with extreme day–night fluctuation; high winds in certain seasons reduce visibility (dust/sandstorms); dust degrades optics, engines, lungs.
- **Terrain/mobility:** ranges from nearly flat/high trafficability to impassable; canalizes operations (poor cross-country mobility, few hardened roads); roads/trails rare; salt marshes normally impassable (recon to assess); ruins, oil wells, pipelines, refineries, irrigation works are key terrain and hazards.
- **Navigation/observation:** lack of terrain features makes land navigation extremely difficult (dead reckoning + GPS discipline); observation and fields of fire generally excellent, but flat ground contains dead spaces; UAS, radar, night vision, infrared extend observation — for both sides.

## Effects on personnel — heat, water, acclimatization

- **Heat:** non-acclimatized troops need more water; heavy work at 80°F WBGT loses large volumes per hour through sweating; in very high temperatures and low humidity, sweating may not be noticeable (evaporates immediately). Encourage drinking more than troops think necessary, especially during acclimatization.
- **Table 1-1 (Work/rest and water consumption guide):** work/rest times and fluid replacement by WBGT category and work level (easy/moderate/hard); sustains performance/hydration for at least 4 hours. Body armor in humid climates adds 5°F to WBGT; CBRN/MOPP ensemble adds 10°F.
- **Water planning:** ~2 gallons (8 liters) per Soldier/Marine per day; 1 gallon (4 liters) per meal for the mess kit; 0.5 gallon (2 liters) per meal for food preparation; 1 quart per hour of hard work (including rest) in the heat of the day; 1.5 cups (0.8 liter) per hour of hard work in cooler parts of the day.
- **Salt:** non-acclimatized troops need additional salt during acclimatization. Lack of salt → heat cramps; too little salt with insufficient water → heat exhaustion; heat exhaustion can collapse the cooling mechanism → heatstroke. Dehydration nullifies the benefits of acclimatization and fitness.
- **Heat injuries:** cramps, exhaustion (treatable, return to duty), heatstroke (medical emergency). Risk factors: previous heat exhaustion, medication use.
- **Acclimatization:** ~2 weeks with progressive heat exposure and exertion; significant in 4–5 days; full in 7–14 days with 2–3 hours per day of exercise in the heat. Acclimatization does not reduce — and may increase — water requirements. Minimize physical activity in the first days of heat exposure.

## Equipment, uniform, health

- **Equipment:** heat, sand, dust, temperature swings degrade weapons, optics, vehicles, communications (see Appendix B). Maintenance time in the desert is combat power, not overhead.
- **Uniform/PPE:** sun protection, ventilation, dust protection traded against the heat burden of body armor (+5°F WBGT) and MOPP (+10°F WBGT) — leaders account for both in work/rest planning.
- **Health:** sunburn and eye injury from glare, dust-related respiratory problems, diarrheal disease from water-discipline failures — all interacting with fatigue. Fatigue is the most dangerous leadership degrader; leaders often regard themselves as least vulnerable to it.
