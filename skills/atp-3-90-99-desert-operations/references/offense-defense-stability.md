# Offense, Defense, Stability — ATP 3-90.99 (2021), Chapters 4–6

## Desert offensive operations (Chapter 4)

- **Characteristics:** surprise, concentration, audacity, tempo — amplified by desert openness, constrained by desert logistics. The offense is a race between maneuver and sustainment: the force that can move, fight, and be resupplied at distance wins; the force that outruns water and fuel stops, and a stopped force in the desert is a target.
- **Types and forms of maneuver:** movement to contact, attack, exploitation, pursuit; envelopment, turning movement, infiltration, penetration, frontal attack. Envelopments exploit open flanks deserts provide; penetrations reckon with canalized approaches; pursuits are limited by fuel and water, not the enemy's will to run.
- **Warfighting function considerations:** C2 across distance, intelligence on a hard-to-find enemy in vast space, fires at long range with observation problems, sustainment as the pacing function, protection of lengthening lines of communication.
- **Consolidate gains:** the offense does not end at the objective — consolidation (security, reorganization, resupply, preparation for the next operation) is where desert offensives are most often lost, because the force arrives exhausted, low on water, and dispersed.

## Desert defensive operations (Chapter 5)

- **Characteristics:** the desert is used as a weapon — canalized approaches become killing grounds, water sources become bait or objectives, the attacker's sustainment problem becomes the defender's advantage. A static defense in open desert is vulnerable to envelopment; the defense must be mobile, deep, and prepared to counterattack.
- **Types and forms:** mobile defense, area defense, retrograde; strongpoints on key terrain (passes, water, high ground), obstacles exploiting natural canalization, reserves positioned to strike an enveloping force.
- **Warfighting function considerations:** protection (dispersion, camouflage in open terrain), sustainment (stockpiled water and fuel at the defensive position), C2 across desert distances.
- **Transitions:** the defense must be able to transition to the offense — a defense that cannot counterattack is a siege waiting to be outflanked.

## Desert stability operations (Chapter 6)

- Characterized by restrictive fire support coordination measures, protecting the local population, and sustainment support to systems and organizations beyond the force — including host-nation government and, where the host nation lacks medical capabilities, medical support to the population.
- Intelligence relies heavily on the population: human interaction is one of the most valuable methods, within OPSEC constraints; commanders share relevant information and coordinate with host-nation and civilian partners.
- **Protection priorities change** as focus shifts from large-scale combat to stability: protect sensitive religious, cultural, and historical sites; provide security for host-nation government functions; perform area security against irregular threats (guerrillas, insurgents). Air/missile threat is low; CBRN threat typically reduced but not dismissible.
- **Transitions** are gradual and vary by AO: transferring an area to a legitimate authority relieves the force of area security but requires focus, careful planning, and close coordination. Military-to-civil control is a process, not an event.
- **Supply demand:** stability operations consume more of some supplies (water, food, medical, construction) than combat operations of the same size.
