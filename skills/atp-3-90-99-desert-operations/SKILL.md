# ATP 3-90.99 / MCTP 12-10D Desert Operations (2021, Change 1)

## Purpose

ATP 3-90.99 / MCTP 12-10D, Desert Operations, U.S. Army and U.S. Marine Corps, 7 April 2021 (including Change 1), 180 pages — the joint Army–Marine Corps doctrine for fighting in deserts, superseding FM 90-3/FMFM 7-27 (24 August 1993). It is a commander's manual, not a survival handbook: its subject is how units operate, maneuver, sustain themselves, and fight across desert regions — and its central claim is that the desert is not empty space to cross but an environment that dictates the operation. Deserts feature the greatest extremes of temperature and weather volatility; successful desert operations require modifying tactics, techniques, and procedures to the environment, not merely enduring it.

Two structural facts matter for using this skill. First, it is written for both services at once (Army ATP, Marine Corps MCTP 12-10D), with Appendix D converting Marine Corps doctrinal schema to Army terms. Second, it treats the desert as a multi-domain problem: the air, land, maritime, space, and cyberspace domains all affect desert operations, and the information environment — where actions speak louder than words and information moves in near-real time — is a warfighting consideration, not a public-affairs afterthought.

For individual desert survival (water discipline, heat injury, shelter), use this skill's Chapter 1 health and water material together with the `summer-survival-course` skill; for desert survival medicine, defer to the TCCC/first-aid skills. This skill is about operating a force in the desert.

## 1. The desert environment — classification, weather, terrain, water

**Deserts are arid, barren regions; lack of precipitation is the defining characteristic.** Desert regions feature the greatest extremes of temperature and weather volatility. Key terrain is largely determined by the presence of water or by terrain that restricts movement.

**Desert classifications (Chapter 1):**

- **Mountain deserts:** scattered ranges, barren hills or mountains separated by flat basins. Avenues of approach are often limited to dry riverbeds (wadis) or valleys; vehicle travel in mountainous desert country severely restricts movement.
- **Rocky plateau deserts:** extensive flat areas with solid or broken rock at or near the surface — good trafficability for vehicles, poor cover, excellent observation.
- **Polar deserts:** the Antarctic and Arctic polar regions are the largest deserts on earth, each covering 5.5 million square miles. Icecaps (Greenland, Antarctica) and glaciers are rivers of ice and snow; overflow ice occurs when a layer of ice ruptures and water underneath flows up to the surface.
- **Sandy or dune deserts:** extensive flat areas covered with sand or gravel. The steep slopes of dunes can severely restrict vehicular movement; the lack of foliage and open terrain make these environments especially favorable to electronic warfare and signals collection.

**Weather.** Air stability varies with the extreme day–night temperature fluctuation. High winds occur in certain desert seasons and greatly affect operations through reduced visibility (dust and sandstorms). Dust degrades optics, engines, and lungs alike.

**Terrain and mobility.** Desert terrain ranges from nearly flat with high trafficability to impassable; it can canalize operations because of poor cross-country mobility, limited hardened roads, and the few improved routes available. Roads and trails are rare in the open desert. Salt marshes are normally impassable (detailed reconnaissance is required to assess them). Ruins of earlier civilizations, oil wells, pipelines, refineries, and irrigation works are both key terrain and hazards — man-made features, mountain passes, water sources, and high ground are what key terrain means in a desert.

**Navigation and observation.** The lack of terrain features in many deserts can make land navigation extremely difficult — in featureless terrain, dead reckoning and GPS discipline replace terrain association. Observation and fields of fire are generally excellent on flat desert terrain, but the landscape contains dead spaces invisible until close, and some desert terrain varies enough to hinder observation. Unmanned aircraft systems, electronic observation, radar, night vision devices, and infrared imaging sensors extend observation — and the open terrain that favors your sensors favors the enemy's. → `references/environment-and-health.md`

## 2. Environmental effects on personnel — heat, water, acclimatization

**Heat is the primary personnel threat.** A non-acclimatized Soldier/Marine requires more water than an acclimatized one; troops doing heavy work such as constructing fighting positions at 80°F wet bulb-globe temperature (WBGT) lose large volumes of water per hour through sweating — and in very high temperatures and low humidity, sweating may not be noticeable because it evaporates immediately. Soldiers/Marines may not drink their required amount and will need to be encouraged to drink more than they think necessary, particularly during acclimatization.

**Table 1-1, Work/rest and water consumption guide.** Work/rest times and fluid replacement volumes by WBGT category and work level (easy/moderate/hard), designed to sustain performance and hydration for at least 4 hours of work. Adjustments: wearing body armor in humid climates adds 5°F to the WBGT index; wearing CBRN protective ensemble (MOPP gear) in humid climates adds 10°F. Leaders adjust work/rest periods by the table and the WBGT index — a critical leadership activity in the desert.

**Water planning numbers (Chapter 1, paras 1-96 to 1-99):**

- Plan roughly **two gallons (eight liters) of water per Soldier/Marine per day**.
- When rations are issued, plan **one gallon (four liters) per meal for the mess kit** and **0.5 gallon (two liters) per meal for food preparation**.
- Individual consumption: **one quart of water per hour of hard work** (including rest periods) during the heat of the day; **1.5 cups (0.8 liter) per hour of hard work** during the cooler parts of the day.
- Non-acclimatized troops need additional salt during acclimatization. A lack of salt can cause heat cramps; too little salt with insufficient water can cause heat exhaustion; heat exhaustion can collapse the body's cooling mechanism — heatstroke.
- Dehydration nullifies the benefits of heat acclimatization and physical fitness.

**Heat injuries.** Some troops develop heat cramps, others heat exhaustion or heatstroke. Heat cramps and heat exhaustion are treatable and the Soldier/Marine can return to duty; heatstroke is a medical emergency. Watch for previous heat exhaustion and medication use as risk factors.

**Acclimatization (1-106 to 1-107).** Allow approximately two weeks, with progressive increases in heat exposure and physical exertion. Significant acclimatization is attainable in 4–5 days, but full acclimatization takes 7–14 days, with two to three hours per day of exercise in the heat. Acclimatization does not reduce — and may increase — water requirements. Leaders minimize physical activity during the first days of heat exposure and gradually increase it. → `references/environment-and-health.md`

## 3. Environmental effects on equipment, uniform, and health

**Equipment (Chapter 1, paras 1-108+; Appendix B).** Heat, sand, dust, and temperature swings degrade weapons, optics, vehicles, and communications. Appendix B gives techniques for operating equipment in the desert — cleaning and lubrication intervals, protection from sand ingress, battery and cooling management, and the desert-specific maintenance burden. The practical rule: maintenance time in the desert is not overhead, it is combat power — a weapon or vehicle that fails from sand is a casualty the enemy did not have to inflict.

**Uniform and personal protective equipment.** The desert uniform is a system: sun protection, ventilation, and dust protection traded against the heat burden of body armor (+5°F WBGT) and MOPP gear (+10°F WBGT). Leaders account for both in work/rest planning.

**Health considerations.** Beyond heat: sunburn and eye injury from glare, dust-related respiratory problems, diarrheal disease from water discipline failures, and the interaction of all of them with fatigue. Fatigue is the most dangerous leadership degrader in the desert — and commanders and leaders often regard themselves as the least vulnerable to it, which is exactly why it bites them. → `references/environment-and-health.md`

## 4. Foundations for desert operations — the operational framework

**Desert operations span the range of military operations**, from military engagement to major operations and campaigns. Factors influencing a desert area may originate beyond its physical boundaries (water sources, lines of communication, regional politics).

**Areas of operations (Chapter 2).** Commanders designate **close, deep, support, rear, and consolidation areas** to describe physical space: the deep area is where the commander sets conditions for future success in close combat; the close area is where the majority of subordinate units fight; the rear area extends forward from the rear boundary; the support area facilitates the operation; the consolidation area (which requires a task-organized combined-arms unit for area security) is where gains are made permanent. Commanders assign subordinates an area of operations to create freedom of action, and designate main and supporting efforts to establish clear priorities of support and resources — a unit conducting a shaping operation may be the main effort until the decisive operation begins.

**Multi-domain extended desert battlefield.** The air, land, maritime, space, and cyberspace domains all impact desert operations. Space capabilities provide information collection, early warning, environmental monitoring, and satellite communications. The information environment is the aggregate of individuals, organizations, and systems that collect, process, and disseminate information; across the globe it moves in near-real time. Cyberspace is a global domain within the information environment, and the electromagnetic spectrum — the range of frequencies from zero upward — is contested: the lack of foliage and open terrain in sandy and dune deserts makes them especially favorable to electromagnetic operations. Advantages in cyberspace and the EMS come from synchronizing Department of Defense capabilities, not from any single system.

**Fundamentals of desert operations (2-28 to 2-37).** The harsh environment and its characteristics impact operations at all echelons. The desert's vast spaces and lack of foliage present both a challenge and an opportunity: open spaces and unrestricted terrain are conducive to combined-arms maneuver, but most desert areas are too large to be completely occupied or even effectively controlled without an enormous force. The **twelve principles of joint operations** apply — but physical control may not be possible in parts of many desert environments, so commanders plan for presence, influence, and control of key terrain (water, routes, passes) rather than occupation of ground. Many deserts contain critical infrastructure for resource extraction, refinement, and processing. The harsh environment and large distances can wreak havoc on equipment and people. Desert operations are resource-intensive — **commanders plan to end them as quickly as possible**. → `references/foundations-and-combat-power.md`

## 5. Combat power in desert regions — eight elements, desert-applied

Combat power is the total means of destructive and/or disruptive force a unit can apply. The Army describes it using **eight elements: leadership, information, command and control, movement and maneuver, intelligence, fires, sustainment, and protection**. Each is treated in Chapter 3 as a warfighting function with desert-specific considerations:

- **Leadership:** applied through mission command; leaders ensure Soldiers/Marines understand the purpose and are employed efficiently. Desert factors — above all fatigue — degrade leadership performance.
- **Information / information warfighting function:** every engagement requires complementary information operations; actions are more powerful than words in the information environment. Open desert terrain facilitates sending, receiving, and collecting information — for both sides.
- **Command and control:** unity of effort remains essential and is harder to achieve across desert distances; commanders establish processes to organize headquarters activities; the military decision-making process (Army) and the Marine Corps planning process (MCPP) are the planning methodologies; intelligence preparation of the battlefield is the systematic analysis of mission variables; targeting requires careful analysis so forces avoid fratricide (see Appendix C).
- **Movement and maneuver / maneuver:** desert distances and terrain make maneuver both freer (open ground, combined arms) and harder (canalization, water, fuel, maintenance). Commanders balance risk against mission requirements when developing the operational approach.
- **Intelligence:** the size and limited trafficability of the operational area pose serious challenges to information collection; UASs and other reconnaissance fill the gaps that ground patrols cannot cover.
- **Fires:** correction of artillery fires, especially larger pieces, is complicated by desert conditions (heat, dust, wind, and the difficulty of observing impacts at long range in featureless terrain).
- **Sustainment / logistics:** the dominant desert problem — water, fuel, and maintenance over long distances. Water planning numbers are in Chapter 1 (two gallons per person per day, one quart per hour of hard work in the heat). Sustainment determines how far and how long a force can operate; it is not a tail, it is the operation's limiting factor.
- **Protection / force protection:** dispersion, camouflage, and cover in terrain that offers little of any; protection of water sources, lines of communication, and the force itself against an enemy who has the same observation advantages you do. → `references/foundations-and-combat-power.md`

## 6. Desert offensive operations — speed, depth, and consolidation

**Characteristics of the offense (Chapter 4).** Surprise, concentration, audacity, and tempo — amplified by desert openness and constrained by desert logistics. The offense in the desert is a race between maneuver and sustainment: the force that can move, fight, and be resupplied at distance wins; the force that outruns its water and fuel stops, and a stopped force in the desert is a target.

**Types of offensive operations and forms of maneuver.** The chapter covers the standard offensive types (movement to contact, attack, exploitation, pursuit) and forms of maneuver (envelopment, turning movement, infiltration, penetration, frontal attack), each with desert considerations — envelopments exploit open flanks that deserts provide in abundance; penetrations must reckon with canalized approaches; pursuits are limited by fuel and water, not by the enemy's will to run.

**Warfighting function considerations** apply Chapter 3's eight elements to the offense: C2 across distance, intelligence on an enemy who is hard to find in vast space, fires at long range with observation problems, sustainment as the pacing function, and protection of lengthening lines of communication. **Consolidate gains:** the offense does not end at the objective — consolidation (security, reorganization, resupply, and preparation for the next operation) is where desert offensives are most often lost, because the force arrives exhausted, low on water, and dispersed. → `references/offense-defense-stability.md`

## 7. Desert defensive operations — use the desert as the defense

**Characteristics of the defense (Chapter 5).** The defense in the desert uses the environment as a weapon: canalized approaches become killing grounds; water sources become bait or objectives; the attacker's sustainment problem becomes the defender's advantage. But a static defense in open desert is vulnerable to envelopment — the defense must be mobile, deep, and prepared to counterattack.

**Types of defensive operations and forms of the defense.** Mobile defense, area defense, and retrograde, with defensive methods matched to terrain — strongpoints on key terrain (passes, water, high ground), obstacles that exploit natural canalization, and reserves positioned to strike an enveloping force. **Warfighting function considerations** again apply the eight elements, with emphasis on protection (dispersion and camouflage in open terrain), sustainment (stockpiled water and fuel at the defensive position), and C2 (controlling a defense spread across desert distances). **Transitions:** the defense must be able to transition to the offense — a defense that cannot counterattack is a siege waiting to be outflanked. → `references/offense-defense-stability.md`

## 8. Desert stability operations — the population is the terrain

**Stability operations (Chapter 6)** in the desert are characterized by restrictive fire support coordination measures, the need to protect the local population, and sustainment support to systems and organizations beyond the force itself — including host-nation government and, where the host nation lacks medical capabilities, medical support to the population. Intelligence in stability operations relies heavily on the population: one of the most valuable methods is human interaction, within OPSEC constraints, and commanders share relevant information and coordinate with host-nation and civilian partners.

**Protection priorities change** as the focus shifts from large-scale combat to stability: forces protect sensitive religious, cultural, and historical sites, provide security for host-nation government functions, and perform area security to defeat irregular threats — guerrillas and insurgents, whose threat from air and missile attack is low but whose CBRN threat, while typically reduced, cannot be dismissed. **Transitions** are gradual and vary by area of operations: transferring an area to a legitimate authority relieves the force of area security but requires focus, careful planning, and close coordination; transitioning from military to civil control is a process, not an event. **Increased demand on classes of supply** is the norm — stability operations consume more of some supplies (notably water, food, medical, and construction) than combat operations of the same size. → `references/offense-defense-stability.md`

## 9. Appendices — regions, equipment, fratricide, conversions

**Appendix A — Desert regions of the world.** A survey of the world's desert regions (their classification, climate, terrain, and military significance) — the reference for planning an operation in an unfamiliar desert rather than the one you trained in.

**Appendix B — Techniques for operating equipment in the desert.** Desert-specific equipment techniques: sand and dust protection, lubrication and cleaning intervals, cooling and battery management, and the maintenance burden that desert conditions impose. Read it as the practical companion to Chapter 1's equipment section.

**Appendix C — Techniques for fratricide reduction in the desert.** Long engagement ranges, excellent observation, and featureless terrain make fratricide a special desert risk: target identification, coordination measures, and the discipline of positive identification at ranges where the enemy and a friendly unit look identical through a sight.

**Appendix D — Marine Corps doctrinal schema conversions.** Converts Marine Corps doctrinal terms and structures to Army equivalents so the joint publication reads cleanly for both services. → `references/appendices.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about desert operations, answer at the right echelon: environment and health numbers for the individual (WBGT table, water planning figures, acclimatization timeline), operational framework and combat power for the commander (areas of operations, eight elements, multi-domain considerations), and offense/defense/stability for the operation type. Give the numbers the manual gives (two gallons per person per day, one quart per hour of hard work in the heat, 1.5 cups per hour in cooler parts, body armor +5°F WBGT, MOPP +10°F, 4–5 days significant acclimatization, 7–14 days full) and distinguish the manual's numbers from general knowledge. When asked about desert survival as an individual, pair this skill with the `summer-survival-course` skill.

## Operating Rules

1. **The desert dictates the operation.** Water, routes, and passes are the key terrain; plan around them, not through them.
2. **Sustainment is the limiting factor.** Water, fuel, and maintenance determine how far and how long — plan to end resource-intensive desert operations as quickly as possible.
3. **Work/rest by WBGT, not by feel.** Body armor adds 5°F, MOPP adds 10°F; enforce the table.
4. **Acclimatize on a timeline.** 4–5 days significant, 7–14 days full, 2–3 hours of exercise in the heat per day — and acclimatization does not reduce water needs.
5. **Fatigue is the leadership threat.** Leaders are the most vulnerable and the least likely to admit it.
6. **Open ground cuts both ways.** Your observation and sensors are excellent — and so are the enemy's. Positive identification at long range is a fratricide control, not a courtesy.
7. **Consolidate or lose it.** An offense that cannot consolidate its gains in the desert has not won them.
