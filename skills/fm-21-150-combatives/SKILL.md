# FM 21-150 Combatives (1992)

## Purpose

FM 21-150, Combatives, U.S. Army, 30 September 1992, 232 pages — the Army's hand-to-hand combat manual of the early 1990s, covering rifle-bayonet fighting and empty-handed combat. It is organized by **range**, studied in reverse order of how a fight is encountered: close-range grappling first, then medium-range striking, then long-range punches, kicks, and weapons — because the manual's premise is that a soldier must first survive the range he is already in before he can create the range he wants.

**Currency warning — read this first.** This edition is superseded. FM 3-25.150 (18 January 2002) superseded it, and the current standard is TC 3-25.150, Combatives (March 2017). The 2017 manual rebuilt the system around a ground-fighting strategy — dominant body positions, the guard, mount, chokes and arm bars, and a competition program — and dropped much of what makes this 1992 edition distinctive. Use this skill for what it uniquely preserves: the **range-based framework** (close/medium/long), the **vital-targets striking system**, **sentry removal**, **pugil training**, and the older **bayonet assault course** tradition. For the current ground-fighting system, use the `combatives` skill (TC 3-25.150, 2017). Where the two disagree, the 2017 manual governs modern training; where this manual adds material the later ones cut, that material is flagged as legacy.

The manual's own definition: hand-to-hand combat is an engagement between two or more persons in an empty-handed struggle or with handheld weapons such as knives, sticks, and rifles with bayonets. Projectile weapons may be lost or broken, or fail to fire; when friendly and enemy forces are so intermingled that firearms and grenades are not practical, hand-to-hand skills become vital assets. The manual is explicit that this training is not only for forward units — with mechanized, airborne, and air-assault mobility, and with low-intensity conflict and guerrilla conditions, any soldier may face an unarmed confrontation. Beyond battlefield use, combatives training builds individual and unit strength, flexibility, balance, cardiorespiratory fitness, courage, confidence, self-discipline, and esprit de corps.

## 1. Basic principles — the fighter's operating system

**Physical balance.** The ability to maintain equilibrium and remain stable and upright. Without balance there is no base of power for attack and no stability for defense. Two aspects: (a) keeping or regaining your own balance — feet about shoulder-width apart, knees flexed, center of gravity lowered; (b) exploiting weaknesses in the opponent's balance. Balance is developed through experience, and it is the first thing a throw or takedown attacks.

**Mental balance.** Do not let fear or anger overcome concentration or the ability to react instinctively. The fighter who loses his temper loses his timing.

**Position.** Move your whole body to a safe position — one where the attack cannot continue unless the enemy moves his whole body. To position for a counterattack, move off the opponent's line of attack, usually at a 45-degree angle, either toward or away from him. That position gives safety and lets you exploit weaknesses in his counterattack position. Movement to an advantageous position requires accurate timing and distance perception.

**Timing.** Perceive the best time to move. Move too soon and the enemy anticipates and adjusts; move too late and he strikes you. The same applies to launching your own attack.

**Distance and range.** The manual treats fighting as a problem of range, and studies the ranges in reverse order of encounter: close range (grabbing distance — throws, chokes, grappling), medium range (touching distance — short punches, elbows, knees, head butts), long range (fully extended punches and kicks, or handheld weapons). The spheres of influence around each fighter come into contact in long-range combatives; the interval gaps between them are what transitions exploit (Chapter 6). → `references/principles-and-training.md`

## 2. Training — how the system is taught

**Train the trainer first.** Professional instruction is the key to success. Instructors must be physically fit and highly proficient in demonstration and practical application, with confidence, enthusiasm, and technical expertise. A unit program that skips instructor quality fails at the first contact drill.

**Safety precautions before anything else.** Combatives training is inherently injurious; the manual requires safety rules, protective equipment, and controlled progression before live work. Warm-ups and stretches are prescribed, not optional, and training moves crawl-walk-run: techniques are demonstrated, executed by the numbers, drilled, and only then executed at combat speed.

**Training areas.** Formations for mass instruction; a matted room for ground work; a constructed pit for throws and falls; and a bayonet assault course for rifle-bayonet qualification under near-combat demands. The bayonet court precedes the assault course — soldiers learn the techniques on the court, then are graded on the course.

**Stances and falls.** The fighting stance is the base for everything; falls (breakfalls) are taught early because a soldier who cannot fall safely cannot be thrown in training, and a soldier who has never been thrown cannot learn throws. Foam pads and other protective equipment let soldiers strike at full intent without full injury.

**Sustainment.** Once a unit has basic proficiency in Chapters 3–7, sustainment training keeps the skills alive with little preparation of the training area — the program is designed to run anywhere, which is why the manual keeps coming back to drills that need nothing but a partner and ground. → `references/principles-and-training.md`

## 3. Close-range combatives — throws, chokes, grappling

Close range means the opponents have closed the gap so they can grab one another. The principles of balance, leverage, timing, and body positioning apply directly. Throws and takedowns upset the opponent's balance and force him to the ground; chokes render him unconscious quickly; grappling wins through superior body movement.

**Throws and takedowns (3-1).** A throw attacks balance: break the opponent's posture, load his weight onto a fulcrum (hip, leg, shoulder), and drive through. The manual teaches them as whole-body movements, not arm techniques — the legs and hips do the work, the arms steer. After the throw, the soldier follows to the ground to maintain control rather than admiring the result.

**Strangulation and choking techniques (3-2, 3-3).** Chokes are the close-range finisher: applied correctly they render an opponent unconscious in seconds, silently — which is why they matter for sentry removal (Chapter 7) as well as open combat. The manual distinguishes blood chokes (compressing the carotid arteries) from air chokes (compressing the windpipe); blood chokes act faster and are the preferred technique. Key: the choke is applied with the whole body — arms lock the hold, legs and hips anchor it, and the opponent's own struggle tightens it.

**Counters to chokes (3-4).** Every choke has a counter, and the manual teaches them alongside the chokes: tuck the chin, protect the neck with hands and shoulders, turn into the choke to relieve pressure, attack the choking arm's leverage, and break the opponent's base before the hold sets. The counter must start the instant the choke is felt — once it is locked and the body is committed, escape is far harder. A soldier who knows only chokes and not their counters is half-trained.

**Grappling (3-5).** Skillful fighting at close range to win through superior body movement. Pain compliance is a tool, not a plan: pain can disable an opponent, but a determined or drugged enemy may fight through it, so grappling aims at control of the body — position, leverage, and balance — rather than at hurting him into submission. → `references/close-range.md`

## 4. Medium-range combatives — vital targets and short strikes

Medium range means the opponents are already within touching distance. The arsenal is short punches and strikes with elbows, knees, and hands — and head butts, which the manual explicitly says not to forget. The soldier uses peripheral vision to evaluate the targets presented and chooses his target, attacking aggressively and concentrating on vital points to end the fight as soon as possible.

**Vital targets (4-1).** The body is divided into three sections, each with targets whose effects the manual spells out:

- **High section (head and neck — the most dangerous target area).** Top of the head (weak where the frontal cranial bones join — trauma to the cranial cavity, unconsciousness, hemorrhage, possible death); forehead (whiplash, cerebral hemorrhage); temple (thin skull, artery and large nerve near the skin — unconsciousness, concussion, massive hemorrhage if the artery severs); eyes (a slight jab causes watering and blurred vision; a forceful jab can blind or gouge); ears (cupped-hand strike can rupture the eardrum, cause concussion); nose (thin bones break easily, extreme pain, eye watering); under the nose (nerve center near the surface — great pain, watery eyes); jaw (break or dislocation; facial nerve pinched against the jaw paralyzes one side of the face); chin (paralysis, mild concussion, unconsciousness — the jawbone transmits force to the back of the brain where cardiac and respiratory mechanisms are controlled); back of the ears and base of the skull (moderate blow can cause unconsciousness by jarring effect); throat (see below).
- **Middle section (torso).** Solar plexus, ribs, kidneys, groin-adjacent targets — strikes here disrupt breathing, break the body's structure, and drop the opponent's guard for a high-section finish.
- **Low section (legs).** Knees, shins, insteps — strikes here destroy mobility and balance, which is the setup for a throw or a finish.

**Striking principles (4-2).** Strike through the target, not at it; use the body's natural weapons (elbow, knee, heel of the hand, forehead) rather than the fist where the fist is fragile; combine strikes — a single strike rarely ends a fight, but a strike that opens a target followed by a strike to the opened target does. **Short punches and strikes (4-3):** elbow strikes (horizontal, vertical, rear), knee strikes, hand strikes (knife-hand, heel-palm, hammer-fist), and head butts — all delivered from the close/medium boundary where a full punch has no room. → `references/medium-and-long-range.md`

## 5. Long-range combatives — punches, kicks, and weapons

Long range means the distance is such that combatants engage with fully extended punches and kicks or with handheld weapons — rifles with fixed bayonets, clubs, sticks. The fighter continuously monitors available body weapons and opportunities, and knows when to increase distance and when to close the gap.

**Extended arm punches and strikes (5-1)** and **kicks (5-2).** Long-range striking uses the full extension of the arm and leg; the power comes from the hips and the step, not the limb. Kicks target the low section (knee, shin, groin) more than the high — a high kick against a standing opponent sacrifices balance for a target he can see coming.

**Defense against an armed opponent (5-3, 5-5, 5-6).** The manual's knife defense is blunt about the odds: an unarmed man against a knife is in a desperate position, and the first choice is always to gain a weapon or create distance. If engagement is unavoidable: control the weapon arm first — never the blade — move off the line of attack at 45 degrees, and attack the weapon arm's structure. Unarmed defense against a rifle with fixed bayonet follows the same logic: get inside the weapon's effective arc, control the rifle, and strike vital targets.

**Angles of attack (5-4).** Weapon attacks are taught as numbered angles — a common language so that attack, defense, and counter can be drilled by the numbers. Learn the angles before the techniques; the angles are the map.

**Bayonet and knife (5-8, 5-9, 5-10).** Bayonet/knife work, the knife-against-knife sequence, and the rifle with fixed bayonet are the manual's core weapons chapters — thrust, slash, butt-stroke, and parry taught as instinctive patterns of natural movement, drilled until they do not require thought. The manual's framing matters: bayonet fighting is instinctive rifle fighting, and the fear of cold steel has won fights that the steel itself never had to finish (the Patton epigraph carried into the 2002 edition).

**Field-expedient weapons (5-11 to 5-14).** Entrenching tool, three-foot stick, three-foot rope, six-foot pole. The principle is the same for each: the tool extends reach and multiplies force, and the vital targets and angles of attack from Chapters 4 and 5 still apply. A soldier who understands angles and targets can fight with whatever is in his hand; a soldier who only memorized one weapon's drills cannot. **Advanced weapons techniques and training (5-7)** integrate all of the above at combat speed against a resisting opponent. → `references/medium-and-long-range.md`

## 6. Transition techniques — bridging the gap between ranges

Chapter 6 is the manual's most strategically interesting chapter, and the one most absent from the later ground-fighting editions. In battle, when a soldier closes to small-arms and grenade range and his ammunition is exhausted or his weapon fails to fire, the fluidity of the battle may dictate hand-to-hand combat — and the fight will move between ranges whether he plans it or not.

**Combat scenarios (6-1).** Noncombatants may be present; firing small arms or grenades would needlessly endanger them, so the soldier may need to engage in hand-to-hand combat even when he still has ammunition. The decision to go hands-on is a tactical judgment, not a failure.

**Bridging the gap (6-2).** The spheres of influence surrounding each fighter come into contact in long-range combatives; the interval gaps between them are the openings. A fighter bridges the gap by moving through the interval — changing range on his own terms, at the moment the opponent's sphere is weakest (after his attack, during his recovery, when his balance is committed). The transition itself is the most dangerous moment: the fighter is between ranges, with neither range's tools fully available. Train transitions as their own skill, not as an afterthought to the techniques on either side. → `references/transitions-and-sentry-removal.md`

## 7. Sentry removal — planning, rehearsal, execution

Sentry removal may be necessary to gain access to an enemy location or to escape confinement. Careful planning, rehearsal, and execution are vital — this is a mission, not a technique.

**Planning considerations (7-1).** A detailed schematic of the guarded area, with known and suspected sentry locations marked. Learn the schedule for changing of the guard and checking of posts, and the guard's meal times — the best time to attack is often soon after he has eaten, when his guard is lowered. Know his route, his relief, his communications, and what happens if he misses a check-in.

**Rehearsals (7-2)** and **execution (7-3).** Rehearse on a mock-up of the actual ground, in the actual conditions (darkness, noise, footing). Execution is silent, fast, and final: approach from the sentry's blind side, control the body and the weapon simultaneously, apply the choke or strike, and lower the body without noise. **Psychological aspects (7-4):** the soldier must be prepared for the reality of killing at arm's length — the manual treats this as a training problem to be addressed before the mission, not discovered during it. **Techniques (7-5)** combine the chokes of Chapter 3 with the approach and control methods specific to a sentry who does not know he is in a fight. → `references/transitions-and-sentry-removal.md`

## 8. Appendices — pugil training and the bayonet course

**Appendix A — Pugil training.** Pugil techniques prepare the soldier to confidently and aggressively use the rifle-bayonet, furnishing a thinking, moving, evading opponent who fights back and makes corrections — realism no dummy provides. Equipment is the pugil stick and protective gear designed to let soldiers fight at full intent with controlled injury. Pugil bouts are the bridge between the bayonet court and the assault course.

**Appendix B — Bayonet training.** After instruction on a bayonet training court, soldiers train on a bayonet assault course, if available, to improve as rifle-bayonet fighters. Instructors grade all previously learned techniques on the qualification course, which places demands approaching near-combat situations. The court teaches; the course tests. → `references/transitions-and-sentry-removal.md`

## Output Contract

When asked about a combatives technique from this manual, answer in its terms: name the range (close/medium/long), the principle it serves (balance, position, timing, distance), the vital target or weapon angle involved, and the progression (demonstrate → by the numbers → drill → combat speed). When asked how this differs from the modern system, say plainly: the 1992 manual is range-based and strike-and-weapons-heavy with sentry removal and pugil/bayonet-course training; the 2017 TC 3-25.150 is ground-fighting-strategy-based with dominant positions, submissions, and competition. Cite the 2017 manual as current for training programs. Never present a 1992 technique as current Army doctrine without the supersession flag.

## Operating Rules

1. **Range first.** Every technique belongs to a range; name the range before the technique.
2. **Position before strike.** Move off the line of attack at 45 degrees; a strike from a bad position is a gift.
3. **Balance is the target.** Throws, takedowns, and low strikes all attack balance first.
4. **Chokes are silent finishers — and counters are half the lesson.** Teach the counter with the choke.
5. **Transitions are a skill, not a gap.** Train bridging the gap between ranges deliberately.
6. **Current doctrine governs.** This manual is legacy: TC 3-25.150 (2017) is the standard; use this skill for its range framework, vital targets, sentry removal, and pugil/bayonet material.
